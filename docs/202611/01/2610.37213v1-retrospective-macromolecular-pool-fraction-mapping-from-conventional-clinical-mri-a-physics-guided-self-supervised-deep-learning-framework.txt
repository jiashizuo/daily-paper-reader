# ABSTRACT 

Macromolecular pool fraction (MPF) is a quantitative imaging biomarker sensitive to myelin integrity and other changes in tissue macromolecular content, but its dependence on specialized acquisitions and reconstruction methods limits its availability for large-scale studies. We present a physics-guided, self-supervised deep learning framework for retrospectively estimating MPF directly from conventional clinical brain MRI contrasts ( T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery [FLAIR]), without paired quantitative reference scans. The framework embeds an analytical two-pool magnetization transfer signal model that relates sequence-averaged radiofrequency transmit power to apparent longitudinal relaxation. To accommodate heterogeneous clinical protocols, the network conditions intermediate feature representations on acquisition parameters via adaptive instance normalization. The framework was trained and evaluated on a multisite, multivendor, multiprotocol dataset comprising 7,705 MRI sessions (3 T) from 4,489 subjects across Philips and Siemens systems. Across 1,237 test sessions, mean MPF was 0.182 ± 0.007 in white matter and 

0.144 ± 0.009 in gray matter, with limited variation across vendor-by-protocol groups (between-group coefficient of variation: 2.65% in white matter and 5.63% in gray matter). In 70 sessions with demyelinating-disease labels, MPF was 35.6% lower in segmented lesions than in normal-appearing white matter. As a technical demonstration, MPF maps from two different clinical protocols acquired in a healthy volunteer showed a mean voxel-wise absolute percentage difference of 7.7% and a concordance correlation coefficient of 0.888 , indicating good cross-protocol consistency. These results suggest that magnetization transfer-informed self-supervised learning enables stable estimation of MPF maps from conventional clinical MRI, providing a scalable approach to investigate demyelinating pathology in both existing imaging archives and prospectively acquired conventional clinical scans without dedicated qMT acquisitions.

# 1 Introduction 

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides excellent soft-tissue contrast and is routinely used for clinical diagnosis. Signal intensity in conventional MR images depends on tissue properties such as longitudinal and transverse relaxation times ( T1, T2, T ∗ 

> 2

) and proton-density (PD). In addition, magnetization transfer (MT) is a fundamental tissue- and sequence-dependent effect that influences the MR signal [1]. MT is commonly described using a two-pool model comprising a free-water pool and a semisolid pool [2]. The semisolid pool contains protons bound to macromolecules, including proteins and lipids, and has a transverse relaxation time on the order of microseconds. It is therefore not directly observable with conventional MRI. However, exchange of longitudinal magnetization between the free and semisolid pools alters the longitudinal relaxation of the observable free-water signal and produces biexponential arXiv:2610.37213v1 [physics.med-ph] 27 Oct 2026 A P REPRINT 

relaxation. MT is consequently a major contributor to longitudinal relaxation in biological tissue [3]. The relative size of the semisolid pool is quantified by the macromolecular pool fraction (MPF, denoted ms0), which quantifies the relative amount of protons bound to biological macromolecules that exchange magnetization with water protons [4]. Among quantitative MT tissue parameters, MPF has attracted particular interest as a marker of myelin integrity in multiple sclerosis (MS), the most common demyelinating disease of the central nervous system [5–7]. MPF is sensitive to myelin content and is markedly reduced in MS lesions [3, 4, 6, 8–10]. This sensitivity has enabled investigations of spatial heterogeneity in demyelination and of lesion evolution over time [4, 9, 11–13]. Moreover, post-mortem studies associated reduced MPF with histopathological evidence of demyelination [4, 14, 15]. MPF mapping could therefore provide a quantitative imaging readout for studying demyelinating pathology, monitoring disease progression, and assessing treatment response. Despite this clinical potential, MPF mapping requires dedicated prospective quantitative MT (qMT) acquisitions with specialized pulse sequences and reconstruction pipelines that are generally not included in standard neuroimaging protocols. Several qMT methods have been developed over the past three decades, including multi-point off-resonance Z-spectroscopy [2, 7, 10], rapid single-point mapping [16, 17], on-resonance selective inversion recovery [18, 19], steady-state saturation [20], and transient hybrid-state methods [3, 21, 22]. These approaches require additional acquisition time and can only be applied prospectively. In addition, they cannot recover MPF from the large collections of conventional scans that already exist in clinical archives. These limitations restrict access to MPF maps and impede both large-scale biomarker studies and subsequent clinical translation. The widespread availability of routine clinical MRI provides an opportunity to derive quantitative tissue parameters without dedicated prospective acquisitions. Initial efforts to exploit these routine scans have relied on mathematical combinations of conventional contrasts—such as T1w/T 2w and T1w/FLAIR ratios or empirical polynomial fits—as accessible proxies for myelin integrity and myelin water fraction (MWF) [23–25]. However, these approaches provide relative, intensity-based indices rather than quantitative physical parameters. More recently, self-supervised, physics-guided deep learning frameworks have begun to address these limitations by retrospectively estimating quantitative maps from routine multi-contrast acquisitions ( T1w, T2w, and FLAIR) without requiring paired reference scans [26–28]. These frameworks embed analytical Bloch equations-based signal models directly into the training objective, enabling scalable deployment across clinical archives. Existing frameworks, however, assume single-pool relaxation and remain restricted to relaxometry parameters ( T1 and T2) alongside PD. Consequently, the retrospective estimation of quantitative MT parameters, including the MPF, from conventional clinical MRI remains unexplored. In this work, we introduce a framework for estimating an MT-model-based MPF directly from routine T1w, T2w,and FLAIR images without paired qMT reference data. We extended a physics-guided, self-supervised deep learning framework by incorporating a simplified closed-form description of MT into its forward signal model. Following Teixeira et al. [29], the model relates the MT-induced apparent longitudinal relaxation time to the tissue-dependent MPF and the average RF transmit power, B1,rms , the latter being determined by the acquisition protocol. To reduce protocol-dependent variation in these estimated maps, we conditioned the network on the input scans’ acquisition parameters. We trained and evaluated the framework using a clinically heterogeneous, multicenter, multivendor dataset comprising more than 7,700 MRI sessions (23,000+ image volumes), including sessions from patients with demyelinating lesions. As a technical demonstration, we assessed cross-protocol consistency using repeated acquisitions in a healthy volunteer. 

# 2 Theory 

2.1 Two-pool magnetization transfer model 

MT in biological tissue is generally modeled using a two-pool framework comprising a liquid free-water pool (superscript 

f) and a semisolid macromolecular pool (superscript s) [2]. Each pool is characterized by its own longitudinal and transverse relaxation rates. However, the transverse relaxation time of the semisolid pool, T s2 , is on the order of microseconds, preventing its direct observation with conventional MRI. Longitudinal magnetization is exchanged between the two pools with first-order rate constants kf (free to semisolid) and ks (semisolid to free) [29]. In this work, we define the macromolecular pool fraction (MPF) as the fractional equilibrium magnetization of the semisolid pool relative to the total equilibrium magnetization. Here, M f0 and M s0 denote the equilibrium longitudinal magnetizations of the free and semisolid pools, respectively. The MPF is therefore defined as ms0 = M s0 /(M f0 + M s0 ),such that mf0 + ms0 = 1 . Note that this definition follows recent quantitative MT literature [3, 16, 22, 30]. In contrast, earlier qMT studies [2, 7, 18] frequently report the pool size ratio relative to the free pool, F = M s0 /M f0. The two definitions are related by 2A P REPRINT

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Dataset 3.1.1 Clinical dataset 

We compiled a multicenter clinical dataset comprising 7,705 non-contrast-enhanced 3 T brain MRI sessions from 4,489 unique subjects from two different sources. The dataset combined (1) retrospectively collected examinations 4A P REPRINT 

from the University Medical Center Utrecht (UMCU) with (2) examinations from the publicly available MR-RATE dataset acquired at Istanbul Medipol University 1. Each session included a 3D T1-weighted ( T1w) acquisition, an axial 2D T2-weighted ( T2w) acquisition, and a 3D T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) acquisition. Gadolinium-enhanced T1w acquisitions were excluded. The two data sources were combined to broaden the distribution of scanner systems, pulse-sequence implementations, and acquisition parameters, including B1,rms . Table 1 summarizes the acquisition characteristics of both datasets. The UMCU dataset expanded upon the clinical dataset used in our previous work [26] by including additional examinations acquired with a broader range of protocols. The collected data were acquired between 2016 and 2023 and processed in accordance with institutional regulations and ethical guidelines. The resulting dataset comprised 5,312 MRI sessions from 2,192 unique subjects (age range, 6–91 years; mean ± standard deviation, 51 .5 ± 17 .6 years; 51.1% male and 48.9% female). The dataset included patients with a broad range of clinical conditions, including oncological, neurodegenerative, epileptic, and vascular disorders. Examinations were performed on four different Philips 3 T MRI systems: Achieva (33.4% of sessions), Ingenia (28.4%), Ingenia CX (20.6%), and Ingenia Elition X (17.6%). The T1w

acquisitions used either a 3D spoiled gradient-echo sequence (Spoiled GRE: 78.5%) or 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE; 21.5%). All T2w and FLAIR acquisitions used 2D and 3D turbo spin-echo (TSE) sequences, respectively. A subset of the MR-RATE data was selected based on the availability of the sequence parameters required for the forward signal model: TE, TR, inversion time TI, FA, and root-mean-square RF transmit field strength over the sequence (B1,rms ). These criteria yielded 2,393 MRI sessions from 2,297 unique subjects, acquired between 2023 and 2026 (age range, 13–93 years; mean ± standard deviation, 42 .0 ± 17 .7 years; 43.2% male and 56.8% female). All selected examinations were acquired on a Siemens Magnetom Vida 3 T system using 3D MPRAGE for T1w, 2D TSE for T2w,and 3D TSE for FLAIR. For each dataset, unique subjects were randomly assigned to the training, validation, and test sets using proportions of 80%, 5%, and 15%, respectively. All MRI sessions from the same subject were assigned to the same partition to prevent subject-level data leakage. We then combined the corresponding partitions from both datasets for model training, validation, and testing. The final training set comprised 6,084 sessions from 3,595 subjects, the validation set comprised 384 sessions from 225 subjects, and the test set comprised 1,237 sessions from 669 subjects. Table 2 summarizes the partition sizes. MR-RATE provides pathology labels derived automatically from the accompanying radiology reports. Within the MR-RATE test set, 70 sessions from 65 subjects carried the label “demyelinating disease of the central nervous system” (mean age, 34 .4 ± 11 .0 years; 35.4% male and 64.6% female). Because diagnostic labels were not available for the UMCU dataset, the radiologist in our team (J.W.D.) performed a targeted review and identified four test-set subjects with clinically confirmed MS. This targeted review was not intended to identify all subjects with MS in the UMCU test set. Supplementary Table S1.1 provides the demographic and acquisition characteristics of these four subjects. 

3.1.2 Data preprocessing 

The UMCU MRIs were converted from DICOM to NIfTI format using dcm2niix [33]. We extracted sequence parameters from the DICOM headers. The MR-RATE dataset was already in NIfTI format, and we obtained the corresponding sequence parameters from the accompanying metadata files. All MRI sessions from the combined dataset underwent a series of preprocessing steps. First, all T2w acquisitions were resampled to an in-plane resolution of 1 × 1 mm while preserving their native slice thickness. Across the combined dataset, the native T2w slice thickness was either 3.0 or 4.0 mm. Second, the 3D T1w and FLAIR images were rigidly registered and resampled onto the voxel grid of the corresponding resampled 2D T2w acquisition using ANTsPy [34]. Third, a brain mask was generated from the T1w image using HD-BET [35], and all images were skull stripped using the resulting mask. Fourth, N4 bias-field correction [36] was applied to the skull-stripped T1w, T2w, and FLAIR images. Fifth, axial slices were center-cropped or zero-padded to a matrix size of 224 × 224 voxels. Finally, superior and inferior slices with low signal-to-noise ratio were excluded to reduce artifacts. Before training and inference of our proposed framework, each input image was independently normalized by dividing all voxel intensities by the histogram mode of the corresponding whole-brain volume. To obtain tissue masks, we applied SynthSeg [37] to the preprocessed T1w images from each MRI session. White matter (WM) and gray matter (GM) masks were extracted from the resulting segmentations. To reduce partial-volume contamination from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), we excluded WM and GM voxels located within 2.0 mm of CSF.

3.2 Model 

We extended our previously proposed physics-guided self-supervised framework for retrospective qMRI mapping from conventional clinical scans [26] by incorporating the MT-informed apparent longitudinal relaxation model into the differentiable forward signal model (Eqs. (4)–(8)). This extension enables the multi-contrast convolutional neural network (CNN) to estimate voxel-wise maps of the free-pool longitudinal relaxation time ( T f1 ), free-pool transverse relaxation time ( T f2 ), PD , and macromolecular pool fraction ( ms0; MPF) directly from routine T1w, T2w, and FLAIR acquisitions. Training remains self-supervised and does not require reference quantitative maps, which are not available. 

3.2.1 Network architecture 

The network retains the three-level 2D U-Net architecture described in [26]. A shared-weight encoder processes each input contrast of an MRI session separately, and attention-based contrast-fusion modules combine the resulting feature representations at each resolution level. The fused features are passed to a common decoder with skip connections from the corresponding encoder levels. The network operates on 2D axial slices of 224 × 224 voxels. We modified the original architecture in two respects. First, we expanded the output layer from three to four channels to generate T f1 , T f2 , PD , and ms0 maps. A Sigmoid activation function constrained the outputs to [0 , 1] , after which they were linearly scaled to physical ranges of [0 , 5] s for T f1 , [0 , 3] s for T f2 , [0 , 1] arbitrary units for PD , and [0 , 0.4] for ms0.We otherwise kept the decoder and attention-based contrast-fusion modules unchanged. Second, we replaced the standard instance normalization in the encoder and bottleneck with acquisition-conditioned adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) [39–41]. Conditioning neural networks on sequence parameters has previously been shown to mitigate protocol-dependent variability across heterogeneous protocols [42–44]. Here, AdaIN enables the shared encoder to dynamically modulate its intermediate feature representations based on contrast type and sequence timing parameters. To condition the network on the acquisition settings, each input contrast m ∈ { T1w, T 2w, FLAIR } is paired with a nine-dimensional acquisition parameter vector cm ∈ R9:

3.2.2 Self-supervised physics-guided objective 

The framework was trained using a physics-guided self-supervised objective that enforced consistency between the acquired input clinical scans and synthetic images generated by the forward signal model. For each MRI session, the network predicted voxel-wise maps of PD , T f1 , T f2 , and ms0. The MT-informed forward signal models described in Section 2.3 then combined these maps with the acquisition parameters of each input scan, including TR , TE , TI , FA ,7A P REPRINT 

and B1,rms , to synthesize images ˆSm for contrasts m ∈ { T1w, T 2w, FLAIR } using Eqs. (5)–(8). Semisolid-pool and exchange parameters that were not estimated by the network were fixed to values from recent qMT literature for WM at 3 T: γ = 2 .675 × 10 8 rad s −1T−1, Rx = 13 .6 s−1, T s1 = 300 ms, and T s2 = 12 .5 μs [3, 30, 46]. For the TSE-based T2w and FLAIR acquisitions, the forward signal model required an additional correction for effective 

T2 weighting. Specifically, the analytical Bloch-based equations are less accurate for TSE acquisitions because these sequences use long echo trains with variable refocusing flip angles, so the effective T2 weighting differs from that predicted by the nominal echo time. To account for this difference, we calculated TE correction factors following Busse et al. [47] and applied them to the nominal TE values in the forward signal model. The factors were 0.90 and 0.42 for the Philips T2w and FLAIR acquisitions, respectively, and 0.86–0.96 and approximately 0.51 for the corresponding Siemens acquisitions. Supplementary Section S2 details the calculations. Conventional MRI signal intensities are expressed in arbitrary, non-standardized units and therefore contain an unknown scan-dependent global scaling factor. Before computing the training loss, each synthesized image ˆSm was globally rescaled to the corresponding normalized input image Sm using the calibration procedure described in our previous work [26]. Because the whole-brain histogram mode used for input normalization corresponds to WM intensity, the scaling factor was constrained to within 20% of a reference value derived from representative WM tissue parameters: 

T f1 = 2000 ms, T f2 = 70 ms, PD = 0 .70 , and ms0 = 0 .21 [3, 30, 46, 48]. In the Bloch-based forward signal models, T f1 and ms0 both affect the synthesized signal intensity through Rapp 1 (Eq. (4)), resulting in an identifiability problem. For a fixed B1,rms , multiple combinations of T f1 and ms0 can therefore produce identical image contrast. Variation in B1,rms across the dataset helps constrain this ambiguity. To further separate the two parameters, we imposed a prior on T f1 within WM only, with μT1 = 2000 ms and σT1 = 400 ms. This prior mean reflects the free-water relaxation time at 3 T reported in recent qMT literature [3, 46], which is substantially longer than the apparent tissue T1 (∼ 700 –1100 ms [48]). No direct prior or regularization term was imposed on ms0.As in our previous work [26], a soft lower bound was imposed on PD to constrain the solution space of the ill-posed inverse problem. This constraint was implemented as a hinge penalty with a threshold τ = 0 .60 . The total training loss combined a data-consistency image-reconstruction term with the PD constraint and the white-matter T f1 prior regularization terms:

3.2.3 Training procedure and implementation details 

The network was trained on the full combined dataset for 20 epochs using the Adam optimizer [49] with momentum parameters β1 = 0 .9 and β2 = 0 .999 . Network weights were initialized using Kaiming initialization [50]. Each training batch consisted of slices randomly sampled from the entire training dataset. To identify the optimal model configuration, hyperparameter optimization was performed using a grid search. Table S3.1 in the Supplementary Materials summarizes the search space and explored values. Furthermore, the necessity of the two regularization terms (PD-constraint and 

T1-prior ) was evaluated through an ablation study, detailed in Supplementary Materials S7. Model selection was based on minimizing validation loss while ensuring quantitative outputs matched physiologically expected ranges for human brain qMT literature values in WM and GM [3, 46, 48]. The final model was trained using a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, a batch size of 16 slices, and regularization weights 

λT1-prior = 0 .0025 and λPD -constraint = 0 .5. We developed the implementation in Python 3.11.9 using PyTorch 2.1.2 [51], with data loading, preprocessing, and slice sampling handled by TorchIO 0.20.6 [52]. Training was performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 4500 Ada Generation GPU and took approximately 12 hours. The complete PyTorch implementation and pretrained network weights are available on GitHub: https://github.com/JelmervanL/ MPF-mapping-from-conventional-MRI .8A P REPRINT 

3.3 Evaluation 

We evaluated the biological plausibility and protocol robustness of the generated MPF ( ms0) maps, their sensitivity to demyelinating pathology, their cross-protocol reproducibility, and the contribution of acquisition-conditioned AdaIN. 

3.3.1 Macromolecular pool fraction in white matter and gray matter 

Estimated MPF values were evaluated in WM and GM across the full clinical test set ( n = 1 ,237 ). To assess robustness against scanner and protocol variation, sessions were stratified into three vendor-by-protocol groups based on the T1w

acquisition: Philips Spoiled GRE, Philips MPRAGE, and Siemens MPRAGE. Stratification was based on the T1w

sequence because it exhibited the largest protocol variation in the dataset, including differences in pulse-sequence type. In contrast, T2w and FLAIR acquisitions consistently employed turbo spin-echo sequences across the entire dataset, differing primarily in timing parameters ( TR , TE , and TI ). We quantified protocol stability using the between-group coefficient of variation (CV) of tissue-specific mean MPF values. The CV was calculated as the standard deviation of the three group means, each obtained by averaging the per-session means within that group, divided by the unweighted mean of the three group means. 

3.3.2 Demyelinating pathology 

Sensitivity to demyelinating pathology was evaluated using the 70 MR-RATE test sessions with report-derived demyelinating-disease labels and the four UMCU test sessions with confirmed MS. Mean MPF values within segmented WM lesions were compared with NAWM and NAGM using relative percentage differences. Furthermore, because T f1 and ms0 jointly determine Rapp 1 in the forward signal model (Eq. (4)), we also assessed whether their estimates covaried across individual lesions. Following the approach of Assländer et al. [3], individual lesions were defined as distinct three-dimensional connected components of the WM lesion masks. Components containing fewer than 10 voxels were excluded from the analysis. Each lesion was represented by its median ms0 and free-pool longitudinal relaxation rate ( Rf1 = 1 /T f1 ). Principal component analysis (PCA) of these paired lesion-level estimates quantified the dominant direction of joint variation and the remaining variation orthogonal to this direction. 

3.3.3 Prospective volunteer reproducibility 

As a technical demonstration, prospective cross-protocol reproducibility of the generated MPF maps was evaluated in a 26-year-old male volunteer scanned on a 3 T Philips Ingenia system with written informed consent and institutional review board approval. The volunteer was considered healthy based on the absence of visible lesions on the acquired images. Two clinical protocols, each comprising a separate set of T1w, T2w, and FLAIR acquisitions, were performed consecutively within the same scanning session. The protocols differed primarily in the T1w sequence, which used Spoiled GRE in one protocol and MPRAGE in the other; detailed acquisition characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S4.1. The prospective data were preprocessed using the same pipeline as the clinical dataset. Voxel-wise agreement between the generated MPF maps was assessed across brain parenchyma (WM + GM) using the mean absolute percentage difference, Pearson correlation coefficient ( r), and concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). 

3.3.4 Impact of acquisition-conditioned AdaIN 

Finally, the contribution of acquisition-conditioned AdaIN was assessed through an ablation experiment in which all AdaIN layers were replaced with standard instance normalization, while all other model and training settings were kept unchanged. Performance was compared using test-set L1 reconstruction error, the between-group CV of tissue MPF estimates across vendor-by-protocol groups, and cross-protocol agreement in the prospective volunteer.

# 5 Discussion 

In this work, we introduced a physics-guided, self-supervised deep learning framework for retrospectively estimating MPF from conventional T1w, T2w, and FLAIR brain MRI without paired qMT data. The framework extends retrospec-tive quantitative MRI mapping by incorporating an analytical two-pool MT model that relates apparent longitudinal relaxation to sequence-averaged RF power, together with acquisition-conditioned AdaIN to account for differences among acquisition protocols. We trained and evaluated the framework using a large multicenter, multivendor clinical dataset and examined tissue-level MPF estimates, sensitivity to demyelinating pathology, vendor/protocol robustness, and the contribution of AdaIN acquisition conditioning. We first assessed whether the retrospectively estimated MPF maps showed expected values for WM and GM. Across the clinical test set, estimated MPF was consistently higher in WM ( 0.182 ± 0.007 ) than in GM ( 0.144 ± 0.009 ), in agreement with the higher macromolecular and myelin content of WM [4, 53]. These tissue mean values fell within the ranges reported by qMT studies that model distinct longitudinal relaxation rates for the free and semisolid pools (ms0 ∈ [0 .17 , 0.27] in WM and ms0 ∈ [0 .08 , 0.18] across cortical and subcortical GM) [3, 30, 46, 54, 55]. However, absolute MPF values should be compared cautiously across studies, as they depend on the fitting strategy and the assumptions and constraints of the two-pool model. In particular, our estimates were higher than values commonly obtained using models that use the constraint T s1 = T f1 [16, 17, 56]. Previous studies have shown that this constraint can substantially underestimate the semisolid pool fraction [3, 46, 54]. In contrast, our model fixes T s1 to the substantially shorter value of 300 ms [3, 30, 46, 54] and estimates T f1 voxel-wise. This difference in model assumptions likely contributes to the higher MPF estimates obtained with our framework. Despite the tissue means falling within reported ranges, the contrast between WM and GM was smaller than expected. The GM-to-WM MPF ratio was approximately 0.79 , whereas prospective qMT studies using both constrained and unconstrained two-pool models have generally reported ratios closer to 0.5 [3, 16, 56]. Our generated maps preserved the expected ordering of WM and GM values but showed reduced MPF contrast between these tissues. This reduced tissue contrast may stem from model simplifications, globally fixed semisolid-pool and exchange parameters, the WM-specific T f1 prior, and partial-volume effects, as discussed further in Section 5.1. 13 A P REPRINT T1w T2w FLAIR ms0

For retrospective application to heterogeneous clinical MRI archives, MPF estimates must remain stable across acquisition protocols and scanner systems. The prospective within-subject experiment further supported this protocol robustness: MPF maps estimated from consecutive Spoiled GRE and MPRAGE acquisitions showed good voxel-wise agreement (CCC = 0 .888 ) and a mean absolute percentage difference of 7.7% (Figures 6 and 7). Acquisition-conditioned AdaIN contributed substantially to this stability. Replacing AdaIN with standard instance normalization substantially increased the between-group CV of MPF, and reduced cross-protocol agreement in the prospective volunteer, with the CCC decreasing from 0.888 to 0.672 (Supplementary Materials S6). This finding is con-sistent with other quantitative mapping frameworks showing that conditioning neural networks on sequence parameters improves performance across heterogeneous acquisition settings [42, 43]. However, protocol hold-out experiments indicated limited generalization to sequence types absent from the training data (Supplementary Materials S8). Robust deployment across clinical archives therefore requires representative coverage of the target sequence types during training. The observed protocol stability is particularly relevant given the ill-posed nature of the parameter-estimation problem. The framework estimates four quantitative maps ( ms0, T f1 , T f2 , and PD ) from only three conventional image contrasts (T1w, T2w, and FLAIR). Moreover, as discussed in the Methods (Section 3.2.2), T f1 and ms0 exhibit a specific identifiability problem. To constrain the solution space, the model incorporates two regularizers: a WM-specific 

T f1 prior and a soft lower bound on PD . The importance of these regularizers was evaluated by the ablation study (Supplementary Materials S7). Removing the WM T f1 prior caused shifts in T f1 and MPF, increased differences between vendor-by-protocol groups, and reduced cross-protocol agreement in the prospective volunteer experiment. Removing the PD constraint primarily affected the estimated PD and T f2 maps and increased vendor-by-protocol group variation. These findings suggest that the regularization improves the stability and reproducibility of the generated quantitative maps. This is consistent with previous physics-guided self-supervised approaches that used similar regularizers to derive quantitative maps from conventional MRI [26, 27]. Beyond these regularization constraints, protocol-dependent variation in sequence-averaged RF power ( B1,rms ) provides additional information that may help distinguish the contributions of T f1 and ms0 to the observed image intensities. Combining the UMCU and MR-RATE datasets broadened the range of acquisition protocols and corresponding B1,rms values represented during training (Table 1). 15 A P REPRINT 

5.1 Limitations 

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the framework relies on simplified descriptions of MT and conventional MRI signal formation. The analytical MT model adopted from Teixeira et al. [29] incorporates several assumptions, as described in the Theory section (Section 2). In addition, the Bloch-based signal equations do not account for factors such as B1 inhomogeneity or detailed echo-train dynamics. More comprehensive approaches, including the MT models proposed by Graham and Henkelman [60], and Sled and Pike [7], EPG-X [61], and the generalized Bloch model [22], may provide more accurate descriptions of these effects. However, these methods generally require detailed sequence information that is often unavailable in retrospective clinical MRI archives. The model also relies on fixed tissue parameters and explicit regularization to constrain parameter estimation. We fixed the exchange and semisolid relaxation parameters to literature values reported for healthy WM and applied them to all tissues and subjects. Because these parameters vary across tissue types and may also change with pathology [3], applying fixed healthy-WM values to all tissues and subjects may bias the estimated MPF. Moreover, although the WM-specific T f1 prior and soft lower bound on PD improved model stability, they constrained the range of admissible solutions and made the estimated parameter maps dependent on the selected regularization settings. In addition to these modeling assumptions, the heterogeneous acquisition geometry of clinical MRI imposed spatial limitations, as previously discussed in [26]. We registered all MRI contrasts to the 2D T2w acquisition geometry and resampled them to an in-plane resolution of 1 × 1 mm. This procedure reduced the through-plane resolution of the original 3D T1w and FLAIR images and removed volumetric detail. Registration errors and partial-volume effects may also propagate into the estimated parameter maps. Moreover, slice-wise processing does not use spatial information from adjacent slices. The evaluation design also has several limitations. Establishing the absolute accuracy of the MPF maps remains challenging because no universally accepted qMT reference standard exists [4, 62]. Available qMT methods differ in sequence design, fitting procedures, and the constraints imposed on the two-pool model, which can lead to systematic differences in the estimated MPF [4]. Comparison with a dedicated prospective qMT implementation would therefore establish agreement with that specific methodology, rather than establishing absolute biological accuracy. In the absence of paired qMT data, we evaluated the generated MPF maps through complementary measures of biological plausibility, sensitivity to demyelinating pathology, stability across vendor-by-protocol groups, and prospective cross-protocol reproducibility. Furthermore, these analyses relied on tissue segmentations derived from clinical MRI, which may be inaccurate in the presence of lesions, anatomical abnormalities, or limited image quality. Errors in the WM and GM masks may affect the reported tissue-level MPF estimates, while errors in the automated WM lesion segmentations may affect the lesion analysis. Moreover, we relied primarily on report-derived diagnostic labels to evaluate demyelinating pathology, and FLAIR-hyperintense white-matter lesions are not specific to demyelination. The four clinically confirmed MS UMCU cases supported the findings but did not constitute a systematic validation cohort. These caveats should be considered when interpreting the observed reduction in lesion MPF. Future studies should evaluate the framework in clinically characterized cohorts with expert tissue and lesion annotations. While the large and heterogeneous clinical dataset enabled evaluation across multiple vendors and protocols, the vendor-by-protocol groups differed in patient demographics. As a result, biological variability across the populations confounded the group comparisons, preventing an independent assessment of the effects of vendor and protocol on MPF estimates. Moreover, all MRIs were acquired at 3 T, and protocol diversity was concentrated mainly in the T1w

acquisitions; other common acquisitions, including 3D T2w and 2D FLAIR, were not represented. Generalizability beyond the scanner systems and acquisition protocols represented in this study remains to be established. In particular, protocol hold-out experiments showed substantial parameter shifts for T1w sequence types absent from training (Supplementary Materials S8); the framework should therefore not be applied to unseen sequence types without retraining or dedicated validation. Finally, the prospective reproducibility experiment included only a single healthy volunteer and should therefore be considered a technical demonstration; larger, dedicated cohorts are required to prospectively assess cross-protocol and cross-vendor reproducibility more comprehensively. 

5.2 Outlook 

By deriving MPF maps directly from routine T1w, T2w, and FLAIR scans, the framework enables quantitative MT analysis without dedicated qMT acquisitions. The framework can generate MPF maps from existing multicenter conventional clinical imaging archives, supporting large-scale retrospective biomarker research. Applied prospectively, it can provide MPF as a quantitative biomarker without increasing scan time and may support longitudinal analysis of lesions in patients undergoing repeated clinical MRI. Although the present study focuses on MS, the proposed MPF mapping approach may also enable investigation of macromolecular tissue changes in other neurological conditions. 16 A P REPRINT 

Potential applications include Alzheimer’s disease [63, 64], Parkinson’s disease [65], Huntington’s disease [66], and myelin damage and repair after stroke [67]. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Physics-guided self-supervised learning enables retrospective estimation of MPF maps from conventional clinical brain MRI without requiring paired qMT reference data. Across heterogeneous protocols, the resulting maps showed good consistency and marked MPF reductions in lesions of patients with demyelinating disease. This highlights the framework’s potential for large-scale biomarker research using existing clinical imaging archives. It may also enable prospective MPF mapping from conventional MRI without requiring a dedicated qMT acquisition or additional scan time.

# A MT signal model assumptions 

The following sections provide further justification for the sequence-averaged RF saturation and near-resonance approximations introduced in the Theory section (Section 2), and explain the omission of the sequence-dependent apparent equilibrium magnetization from the forward signal model. 

A.1 Sequence-averaged RF saturation 

Teixeira et al. [29] applied the continuous-wave-equivalent formulation to short-TR steady-state acquisitions and defined B1,rms over a single repetition period. We extend this formulation to conventional clinical acquisitions by averaging the cumulative RF power of all excitation, inversion, and refocusing pulses over the entire acquisition. This extension assumes that the combined RF pulse rate is high relative to the free- and semisolid-pool longitudinal relaxation rates ( Rf1 and Rs1), the exchange rate ( Rx), and the sequence-averaged semisolid-pool saturation rate ( ⟨W ⟩). The combined RF pulse rate includes pulses applied to the imaged volume and slice-selective pulses applied to other slices, whether on- or off-resonance. Consequently, even acquisitions with a long TR can contain RF pulses at intervals on the order of milliseconds, primarily because of frequent TSE refocusing pulses. These intervals correspond to combined RF pulse rates on the order of 10 2 s−1, which exceed the longitudinal relaxation and exchange rates used in the model ( Rf1, Rs1, and Rx < 15 s −1). The modeled semisolid-pool saturation rate is also substantially lower than the combined RF pulse rate. For example, Eq. (3) gives ⟨W ⟩ ≈ 6 s −1 for B1,rms = 2 .3 μT and T s2 = 12 .5 μs. The semisolid longitudinal magnetization is therefore expected to change little between successive pulses, allowing its cumulative saturation to be approximated using the sequence-averaged RF power. 

A.2 Near-resonance assumption and apparent equilibrium magnetization 

In the continuous-wave-equivalent formulation of Teixeira et al. [29], the semisolid-pool saturation rate depends on the absorption lineshape G(∆) evaluated at the RF frequency offset ∆. In this work, we assume a Gaussian lineshape: 

G(∆) = ( T s2 /√2π) exp[ −(2 π∆T s2 )2/2] , (A.1) where ∆ denotes the frequency offset from the water resonance. We assume near-resonance irradiation and therefore approximate the lineshape by its peak value, G(∆) ≈ G(0) = T s2 /√2π, as used in Eq. (3). This approximation requires 

2π|∆|T s2 ≪ 1, or equivalently, 

|∆| ≪ 1/(2 πT s2 ) ≈ 12 .7 kHz (for T s2 = 12 .5 μs) . (A.2) This near-resonance assumption is generally satisfied for the 3D acquisitions considered in this study, as all RF pulses are applied at or near the water resonance. The assumption is less valid for the multislice 2D T2w acquisitions in our dataset. An RF pulse applied to one slice is off-resonant for spins in the other slices. The corresponding frequency offsets increase with interslice distance and may no longer be small relative to 1/(2 πT s2 ), as required by Eq. (A.2). Explicitly modeling these contributions would require information about pulse timing, waveforms, and slice selection that is typically unavailable in retrospective clinical MRI archives. We therefore applied the assumption G(∆) ≈ G(0) 

to all acquisitions, yielding an analytical saturation model that can be used efficiently during self-supervised network training. Under the continuous-wave-equivalent approximation, Teixeira et al. [29] showed that the longitudinal dynamics of the two-pool MT system can be represented by an effective single-pool model. This model is characterized by an apparent longitudinal relaxation rate, Rapp 1 (Eq. (4)), and a sequence-dependent apparent equilibrium magnetization:

# S2 TSE echo time correction 

Turbo spin-echo (TSE) acquisitions use echo trains that can produce stimulated-echo contributions, causing the effective 

T2 weighting to differ from that predicted by the nominal echo time. In the forward signal models for the T2w and FLAIR acquisitions, we accounted for this difference by replacing the nominal echo time with a contrast-equivalent effective echo time, TE eff = cTE TE nom , where cTE denotes the echo-time correction factor [47]. For the Philips acquisitions, console-reported contrast-equivalent echo times yielded cTE = 0 .90 for 2D T2w and 

cTE = 0 .42 for 3D FLAIR. These factors corresponded to an effective echo time of 72 ms for the nominal T2w echo time of 80 ms, and a median effective echo time of approximately 132 ms for the median nominal FLAIR echo time of 314.5 ms. Equivalent console values were unavailable for the Siemens acquisitions. Correction factors were therefore estimated using extended phase graph (EPG) simulations with representative tissue relaxation times of T1 = 1000 ms and 

T2 = 100 ms. Because the echo spacing and center-echo index were not provided in the metadata, the center of 

k-space was assumed to occur halfway through the echo train. For Siemens 2D T2w, the constant refocusing flip angles (119 ◦–150 ◦), echo-train lengths (13–20), and nominal echo times reported in the metadata were simulated directly, yielding correction factors between 0.860 and 0.958 ( TE eff = 76 .5–100 .1 ms). The Siemens 3D FLAIR acquisitions used a SPACE readout with a proprietary variable refocusing flip-angle schedule that was not exported in the metadata. We approximated this schedule using the pseudo-steady-state target-signal formulation of Busse et al. [47], constrained to a maximum refocusing angle of 120 ◦. Calibrating the normalized target plateau amplitude to Starget = 0 .292 reproduced the Philips 3D FLAIR correction factor ( cTE = 0 .42 ) under representative sequence parameters ( TE = 314 .5 ms, ETL = 199 ). Applying this calibrated schedule to the Siemens acquisitions yielded correction factors of 0.502–0.509, corresponding to a median effective echo time of approximately 196 ms. S2 A P REPRINT

# S7 Ablation of the regularization terms 

The training objective in Eq. (12) combines a data-consistency loss with two regularization terms: a soft lower bound on PD and a prior on white-matter T f1 . To evaluate their individual contributions, we trained four models differing only in the corresponding loss weights ( λPD -constraint ∈ { 0.5, 0} and λT1-prior ∈ { 0.0025 , 0}). Each model was evaluated across the clinical test set using the between-group coefficient of variation (CV) and in the prospective healthy volunteer using mean absolute percentage difference (MPD), concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), and Pearson correlation (r) (Supplementary Tables S7.1 and S7.2). Removing the white-matter T f1 prior primarily affected the estimated T f1 and ms0 maps. Compared with the full model, these configurations exhibited larger differences between vendor-by-protocol groups and reduced cross-protocol agreement in the prospective volunteer experiment. When both regularization terms were omitted, the estimated T f1

values were substantially lower than those obtained with the full model. Removing the PD constraint primarily affected PD and T f2 , producing larger between-group variation and poorer prospective agreement, as reflected by higher MPD and generally lower CCC. It also shifted ms0 to higher values, particularly in WM, although its cross-protocol agreement changed less markedly. Similar trends were observed when both regularization terms were omitted simultaneously. S10 A P REPRINT

# S8 Generalization to unseen T1w protocols 

To evaluate whether the framework generalizes to vendor-by-protocol groups not represented during training, we performed two protocol hold-out experiments based on the T1w sequence used to define these groups in the main analysis. We trained one model exclusively on sessions from the Philips and Siemens MPRAGE groups, withholding the Philips Spoiled GRE group. We trained a second model exclusively on sessions from the Philips Spoiled GRE group, withholding both MPRAGE groups. Both protocol-restricted models used the same network architecture, loss weights, and optimization schedule as the pooled model (presented in the main paper) and differed only in the training data provided to them. We evaluated all models on the complete clinical test set ( n = 1 ,237 sessions). Table S8.1 summarizes the corresponding training subsets. Withholding a protocol during training substantially shifted the parameter estimates for the corresponding out-of-domain vendor-by-protocol groups, indicated by † in Table S8.2. When applied to the unseen Philips Spoiled GRE group, the MPRAGE-only model produced a mean white-matter MPF of 0.126 , compared with approximately 0.18 in the two in-domain MPRAGE groups. White-matter T f1 and T f2 also shifted to markedly higher values. Accordingly, the between-group CV of white-matter MPF increased from 2.65% for the pooled model to 19 .71% .Conversely, when applied to the unseen MPRAGE groups, the Spoiled-GRE-only model produced lower white-matter MPF values and markedly higher white-matter T f1 and T f2 values than in the in-domain Spoiled GRE group. Mean white-matter MPF decreased from 0.177 in the Spoiled GRE group to approximately 0.15 in both MPRAGE groups, increasing the between-group CV to 11 .06% .Despite differences in scanner vendor, the MPRAGE-only model produced similar estimates for the Philips and Siemens MPRAGE groups. In contrast, both protocol-restricted models produced substantial parameter shifts when applied to the withheld sequence regime.
