Abstract 

Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging provide complementary information for the brain, but acquiring both modalities is not always possible. The cycle-consistent adversarial network (CycleGAN) enables unpaired CT-to-MR synthesis, but because the standard L1-norm cycle-consistency penalty grows linearly with the residual magnitude, the network tends to focus on large residuals. This paper introduces an L0-norm-based cycle-consistency term, in which the L0-norm is approximated by a smooth and bounded function. This approximation not only increases sensitivity to small residuals but also effectively suppresses the influence of large residuals. The pro-posed model and an otherwise identical L1-norm baseline were trained on 102 images and evaluated on 9 test images. The proposed model achieves a lower mean squared error (0.02746 versus 0.03464, a 20.7% reduction), a higher mean peak signal-to-noise ratio (15.80 versus 14.77 dB), and a higher mean structural similarity index (0.5689 versus 0.5407). It also has a lower maximum MSE and higher minimum PSNR and SSIM than the baseline. These results indicate that modifying the cycle-consistency penalty can improve reconstruction accuracy on this dataset. 

Keywords: CycleGAN; L0-norm; cycle-consistency loss; unpaired CT-to-MR synthesis; brain imaging.

Disorders of the nervous system are among the largest contributors to ill health world-wide. In the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, 37 neurological conditions ranked as the leading group cause of disability-adjusted life-years, affecting 3.40 billion peo-ple, or 43.1% of the world population [1]. Stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and other dementias are among the ten conditions with the highest age-standardised rates of disability-adjusted life-years [1]. Imaging is central to the diagnosis and treatment of neurological disease, and computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging provide comple-mentary information for the brain. MR resolves soft tissue, which is central to most assessments of the brain, whereas CT depicts bone clearly, is faster, is more widely available, and is routinely used for radiotherapy treatment planning [2]. Neither modal-ity supplies what the other does, so both are used in the diagnosis and treatment planning of diseases such as cancer [2]. However, in clinical practice, imaging costs and equipment accessibility pose practical barriers to acquiring both CT and MR [3], and some patients are unable to undergo MR imaging due to metallic implants or other contraindications [4]. Consequently, many patients end up with only a brain CT and lack a brain MR. Synthesising an MR image from an existing CT image could reduce the need for an additional MR acquisition when synthetic images are acceptable for the intended task. Supervised convolutional models are widely used in medical image analysis [5]. For CT-to-MR synthesis, representative examples include a three-dimensional fully convolutional network for CT estimation from MR [6] and a SegNet-based two- and three-dimensional CNN for synthetic CT generation from T1-weighted MR [7]. These supervised models require spatially aligned CT-MR image pairs from the same patient [8], which are difficult to assemble because the two examinations are acquired on dif-ferent scanners, in different sessions, and with different fields of view. To overcome the bottleneck of scarce paired data, the cycle-consistent adversarial network (CycleGAN) is used to learn from unpaired datasets [9]. The network trains two generators with a cycle-consistency loss, which encourages each translation to be approximately invert-ible on the data support. Cycle-consistent models have since been applied to unpaired cross-modality synthesis [3, 10, 11]. For example, Wolterink et al. [12] applied Cycle-GAN to MRI and CT images to synthesize CT of the head and neck. Hiasa et al. [13] added a gradient-consistency loss to improve boundary accuracy in pelvic MRI-to-CT synthesis. To the best of our knowledge, current cycle-consistency losses in CycleGAN and its variants employ the L1-norm, where the penalty grows linearly with the residual magnitude. This means that the network focuses more on reducing large residuals, potentially overlooking the correction of finer details. In contrast, the L0-norm assigns equal weight to every non-zero residual. It is equally sensitive to small residuals and does not diminish its attention to them in the presence of large ones. Therefore this study replaces the L1-norm penalty with an L0-norm-based cycle-consistency term implemented through a smooth approximation of the L0 indicator. This forces the network to drive the error of each pixel toward zero, thereby effectively preserving reconstruction details. Moreover, the approximation makes the L0-norm 2differentiable and gives it a redescending influence function: the per-residual gradient is bounded and exhibits an initial rise followed by a decline as the residual increases from 0 to 1. This approximation not only increases sensitivity to small residuals but also effectively suppresses the influence of large residuals. Finally we appliy the proposed cycle-consistency term to unpaired brain CT-to-MR synthesis and evaluates it against an otherwise identical L1-norm baseline in an image-level split. The contributions are as follows. 1. An L0-norm-based cycle-consistency term is introduced for unpaired brain CT-to-MR synthesis. The L0-norm is replaced by a smooth, bounded approximation that substitutes a redescending influence function for the linear weighting of the L1-norm penalty, so the loss remains sensitive to small residuals while the influence of very large residuals decays. 2. The evaluation is performed at the image level on 111 images, with 102 images used for training and 9 images held out for testing. 3. The comparison reports aggregate performance and the extreme-case value of each metric on the test set. The proposed model has a lower maximum MSE and higher minimum PSNR and SSIM. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 sets out the problem, recalls the CycleGAN formulation, and develops the L0-norm-based cycle-consistency term together with the smooth approximation that makes it differentiable, and it then introduces the least-squares adversarial loss and states the training objective and procedure. Section 3 describes the data and split, defines the three evaluation metrics, and reports the quantitative and qualitative results, including the analysis of where in the distribution the two models differ. Section 4 explains the mechanism behind the observed pattern and discusses the interpretation of the three metrics. Section 5 concludes.

The generators follow the CycleGAN residual architecture: a 7 ×7 convolution with 32 filters, two 3 × 3 convolutions with stride two and 64 and 128 filters, nine residual blocks with 128 filters, two transposed convolutions with 64 and 32 filters, and a final 7 × 7 convolution to three channels, followed by a tanh output and a skip connection that adds the input of the generator to its output. Instance normalisation and ReLU are used throughout the generators. The discriminators are 70 × 70 PatchGANs, that is, patch-based discriminators, each consisting of five 4 × 4 convolutions with 64, 128, 256, 512 and one output channel, the first three with stride two, leaky ReLU activations of slope 0.2, and a linear output layer. The discriminator is trained to output 1 for real images and 0 for synthetic images, while the generator aims to make the discriminator output close to 1 for generated images. When updating the discriminator, samples are drawn from a pool of fifty previously generated images rather than images produced in the current iteration. All comparison experiments in this paper adopt identical adversarial loss, network architecture, training schedule and cycle weight λ. The cycle weight was λ = 10 and the approximation scale was σ = 0 .1. The baseline is the same model with an L1-norm penalty in place of the approximation, trained on the same data, architecture, adver-sarial loss, and schedule, so that the two models differ only in the cycle-consistency term within a common training configuration. # 4 Discussion 

Taken together, the test results show that replacing the L1-norm cycle penalty with the approximation of the L0-norm improves mean reconstruction accuracy and the extreme-case value of each metric in this dataset. The CycleGAN architecture and the other training components are shared by the two models, so the observed differences can be examined under a common training configuration. 

4.1 Interpretation of the observed improvement 

The lower maximum MSE and higher minimum PSNR and SSIM reported in Section 3.4 are consistent with the design of the approximation. Under the L1-norm penalty, every non-zero residual has a gradient of magnitude one. Under the approximation, the gradient is bounded, peaks at |Iij | ≈ 1.02 σ, and decays for larger residuals. The latter therefore reduces the relative influence of very large residuals, while retaining sensitivity near the transition band. What’s more, the scale parameter σ governs the location of the transition region on the gradient curve of the proposed approximation. For σ = 0 .1, the gradient reaches its maximum value of 7 .87 at |Iij | = 0 .102. The gradient of the approximate loss remains larger than that of the L1-norm penalty for residuals below approximately 0.21, and decays afterwards to about 0 .19 at residual 0 .25 and 0 .015 at residual 0 .3. 10 The root-mean-square reconstruction error for the synthetic reference is 0 .166, i.e., 1.66 σ, which indicates that the typical scale of reconstruction errors on this dataset lies near this transition band and facilitates the desirable residual-modulating property of the loss function. It should be noted that the cycle residual is not identical to the reconstruction residual, so the above matching is only qualitative. The overall balance of the cycle-consistency constraint is jointly controlled by the hyperparameters λ and 

σ.

4.2 Relation to previous work and metric interpretation 

Most existing improvements to CycleGAN focus on network architecture design or introduce additional loss terms while retaining the original cycle-consistency penalty. The core contribution of this paper lies in directly modifying the cycle-consistency loss itself. Consequently, the proposed scheme can be readily transferred to other existing CycleGAN variants. MSE and PSNR measure global error and are closely related for a fixed dynamic range, whereas SSIM compares local luminance, contrast, and structure. Agreement between the mean MSE and mean SSIM therefore supports the observed improvement. 

# 5 Conclusion 

This paper introduced an L0-norm-based cycle-consistency term, implemented through a smooth, bounded approximation, and applied it to unpaired brain CT-to-MR synthesis. The approximation provides a redescending influence function: its per-residual gradient is bounded, peaks at residuals of order σ, and decays for much larger residuals. This design offers a principled alternative to the linear L1-norm penalty, which weights the loss by residual magnitude. After training on 102 images and holding out the remaining 9, the proposed model achieved a lower mean MSE (0.02746 versus 0.03464), a higher mean PSNR (15.80 versus 14.77 dB), and a higher mean SSIM (0.5689 versus 0.5407) on the test set. It also produced a lower maximum MSE and higher minimum PSNR and SSIM. These results show that changing the cycle-consistency penalty can improve recon-struction accuracy in this dataset. Future work will incorporate a systematic ( σ, λ )sensitivity analysis, multi-centre data, and downstream task evaluation.
