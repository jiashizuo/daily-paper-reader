ABSTRACT
Decoding sensory-evoked functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data is
a central analytical tool in cognitive neuroscience. Here we ask whether self-
supervised learning (SSL), which has been scarcely explored in this domain, can
yield representations that support strong downstream performance. To do so, we
adapt a joint embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) to train participant-specific
encoders on brain data alone. The encoders never see the stimulus images or any
representation derived from them. Applied to the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD),
we evaluate our learned brain embeddings on the downstream tasks of brain cap-
tioning and image reconstruction, as well as cross-participant generalization. Our
self-supervised approach achieves competitive performance with parameter-rich
supervised pipelines that are trained end-to-end with access to image and language
data. We further find that encoders trained independently for different participants
can be aligned fully unsupervised, using neither images nor stimuli shared be-
tween them. After alignment, a neural decoding head trained on one participant
can be applied to another without fine-tuning and retains most of its within-subject
performance on image reconstruction and brain captioning. By removing the need
for images and shared stimuli, our approach is a step towards pooling fMRI data
across participants and, eventually, across datasets.

INTRODUCTION
The decoding of information from brain activity is a central analytical tool in cognitive neuro-
science. Classical applications ask whether experimental conditions, visual categories, or other
stimulus properties can be read out from measured brain responses alone. More recently, advances
in generative modeling and the availability of large-scale neural datasets, such as the Natural Scenes
Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022), have substantially expanded the scope of which information
can be derived from brain data. Capabilities of brain decoding now include largely veridical image
reconstruction from functional MRI (fMRI) responses, obtained while participants observe visual
stimuli (Takagi & Nishimoto, 2023; Ozcelik & van Rullen, 2023; Scotti et al., 2024), up to ap-
proaches that map brain activity into language representations, enabling versatile brain captioning
and question-answering interfaces beyond reconstruction itself (Mai & Zhang, 2023; Bosch et al.,
2025).
These approaches, however, place substantial demands on the available data.
Modern decod-
ing pipelines are typically trained separately for each participant, and many additionally rely on
paired supervision between brain responses and stimulus-derived representations, such as image
embeddings or language annotations.
Both requirements are restrictive.
Even unusually large
fMRI datasets contain orders of magnitude fewer training examples per participant than are com-
monly used to train modern machine-learning models, simply because acquiring fMRI data is time-
consuming and expensive. Pooling data across participants could substantially increase the amount

of brain data available for representation learning, but this is difficult because brains differ in func-
tional organization and in the precise spatial pattern through which information is represented. Con-
sequently, a decoder trained on one participant does not straightforwardly generalize to another. Ex-
isting approaches to functional alignment can reduce this problem, but typically depend on paired
measurements, i.e., responses from multiple participants to the same stimuli.
An appealing alternative would therefore be to learn brain representations that address both limita-
tions at once: such a representation should make effective use of the brain data itself, without requir-
ing large-scale external supervision during representation learning, while at the same time enabling
versatile downstream applications and exposing a structure that can be aligned across participants
despite their anatomical and functional differences. Self-supervised learning (SSL) provides a natu-
ral framework for this objective. Rather than learning brain representations by predicting an image
embedding or a language annotation, we learn them from the structure that the measured brain re-
sponses share across repetitions of the same stimulus. Because the objective is self-supervised, the
resulting representation is not tied to the feature space of a particular external image or language
model, and can serve multiple readouts. In addition, the objective does not depend on the stimulus
modality: it applies to any experiment that repeats its stimuli.
Here, we explore this possibility for stimulus-evoked fMRI using a joint-embedding predictive ar-
chitecture (JEPA). SSL has previously been applied to fMRI, particularly in the resting-state setting
for downstream phenotype or clinical prediction (Ortega Caro et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024). We
instead ask whether it can recover representations of stimulus-evoked activity that retain the fine-
grained information required for generative decoding. We pre-train one encoder per participant on
NSD, asking it to predict the latent representation of masked cortical parcels from a different repe-
tition of the same stimulus. Pairing repetitions this way leaves stimulus-locked structure as the only
predictable signal and does not reward the model for measurement-specific noise; the model also
never sees the stimulus, an image embedding, or a language annotation during pre-training.
We find that the resulting brain embeddings are highly expressive. After self-supervised pre-training,
we freeze the encoders and train downstream heads for image reconstruction and brain captioning
using MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024) and CorText (Bosch et al., 2025), respectively. Despite the
absence of image or language supervision during encoder training, these brain embeddings sup-
port downstream performance competitive with the corresponding pipelines trained end-to-end with
stimulus supervision. Thus, much of the information required for mapping brain activity into mod-
ern image and language models can be captured through latent prediction directly within brain data,
reducing the amount of task-specific parameter learning required at the decoding stage.
Self-supervised training also yields representations that can be aligned across participants without
externally defined anchors, such as shared stimuli or stimulus content. This has recently been shown
for compact, largely linear embeddings of NSD participants (Marcos-Manch´on et al., 2026). What
remains open is whether alignability survives in representations rich enough to drive generative de-
coders and whether a decoder trained on one participant works on the aligned representations of
another. Applying that alignment procedure, extended to parcel sequences, to our per-participant
encoders, we find the full parcel embeddings still related by orthogonal maps recoverable without
paired data, and a decoding head trained on one participant applies unchanged to another’s aligned
representations, retaining most of its within-participant quality. Self-supervised pre-training there-
fore provides both an expressive within-participant representation and a basis for cross-participant
transfer.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce self-supervised pre-training of stimulus-evoked fMRI encoders using a brain-
only JEPA objective. The objective exploits repeated responses to the same stimulus, but
requires no access to the stimulus itself or to externally pre-trained image or language
representations.
• We show that the resulting frozen brain embeddings simultaneously support both image
reconstruction and brain captioning at performance levels competitive with corresponding
end-to-end supervised pipelines, despite requiring substantially less task-specific parameter
learning.
2


• We show that independently trained, participant-specific brain embeddings can be aligned
fully unsupervised, without images or stimuli shared across participants, enabling cross-
participant decoding without retraining the downstream decoder.
Together, these results suggest that latent prediction can provide a common representational basis for
both expressive brain decoding and cross-participant generalization. By decoupling representation
learning from stimulus-derived supervision and cross-participant alignment from shared stimuli,
this approach opens a path towards combining brain data across participants and, ultimately, across
datasets that were not originally acquired with joint decoding in mind.

RELATED WORK
Self-Supervised Representation Learning.
SSL is a family of training objectives that require
only input data and no target labels. It is commonly used to pre-train representations that can be
deployed across a range of downstream tasks. Prominent SSL objectives include reconstruction or
masked prediction in the input space (Vincent et al., 2008; Pathak et al., 2016; Devlin et al., 2019;
He et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022), and prediction or matching in latent representation spaces (Chen
et al., 2020; Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Bardes et al., 2022). Joint embedding predictive
architectures (JEPAs) (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025)
are a more recent class of latent-prediction models: rather than reconstructing raw observations,
they predict latent representations of masked regions from a visible context. Because unpredictable
input-level details need not be represented faithfully (Littwin et al., 2024), this objective may be par-
ticularly suitable for fMRI, where scanner, physiological, and other non-stimulus-related variation
is common.
SSL methods have been widely applied in neuroimaging (Kim et al., 2023; Ortega Caro et al.,
2023; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; 2026a;b), though many operate on resting-state data
and are evaluated on phenotype and clinical readouts. In that setting, Dong et al. (2024) report
that a JEPA objective outperforms reconstruction- and contrastive-based SSL on demographic and
disease prediction, motivating our use of this objective family for sensory-evoked fMRI. Closest to
our setting, Marcos-Manch´on et al. (2026) construct subject-specific representations on NSD (Allen
et al., 2022) using a largely linear pipeline (PCA and multiset CCA), refine them with a contrastive
objective, and evaluate them through cross-trial retrieval. We differ by learning these representations
end-to-end and evaluating them on generative readouts rather than retrieval alone.
Decoding from fMRI: vision and language.
Advances in generative architectures for vision and
language have enabled increasingly detailed decoding of sensory-evoked fMRI. One line of work
maps brain signals into the latent space of generative image models to reconstruct the viewed stimu-
lus (Takagi & Nishimoto, 2023; Ozcelik & van Rullen, 2023; Chen et al., 2023b; Scotti et al., 2023);
a parallel line maps them into language models to generate textual descriptions instead (Ferrante
et al., 2023; Mai & Zhang, 2023; Chen et al., 2023a; Huang, 2024; Qiu et al., 2025). We adopt
MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024) for image reconstruction and CorText (Bosch et al., 2025) for cap-
tioning. Both methods train the brain-side encoder against stimulus-derived targets, such as frozen
image embeddings or caption tokens. We instead use these pipelines as downstream readouts —
trained against the same targets — of a frozen brain encoder whose pre-training objective has no
access to stimulus images or stimulus-derived representations.
Cross-Subject Functional Alignment.
Brains differ across individuals in both anatomy and func-
tional organization, so the same measurement location does not correspond across people. Because
establishing that correspondence is difficult, decoding models are typically built separately for each
individual. Hyperalignment addressed this by learning transformations from responses to shared
stimuli (Haxby et al., 2011), an approach extended by the shared response model (Chen et al., 2015)
and optimal-transport formulations (Bazeille et al., 2019) and inherited by several deep learning-
based methods (Chen et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2021). More recent methods remove the requirement
for shared stimuli, but instead anchor participants to representations of pre-trained image encoders
(Wasserman et al., 2026; Papathanasiou et al., 2026). Our setting removes both requirements: nei-
ther pre-training nor alignment uses shared stimuli or representations derived from the stimulus
images.
3


Outside neuroimaging, it has already been shown that different embedding spaces can be brought
into correspondence without paired examples by matching their internal similarity structure (Con-
neau et al., 2018; Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola, 2018; Jha et al., 2025). Marcos-Manch´on et al. (2026)
recently adopted this framework for sensory-evoked fMRI data, aligning per-participant embeddings
without paired stimuli across participants or access to stimulus images. We use their alignment pro-
cedure to test whether independently and end-to-end learned neural representations develop corre-
sponding geometries.

METHOD
Our method has three components: we pre-train one JEPA encoder per participant on parcellated
sensory-evoked fMRI responses (Section 3.2), evaluate the resulting brain embeddings with gener-
ative decoding heads trained on top of the frozen encoder (Section 3.3), and align the independently
trained encoders across participants without shared stimuli (Section 3.4). Appendix A lists the pa-
rameters for all three. We first describe the data and the parcel representation that all three build
on.
3.1
DATASET
We use NSD (Allen et al., 2022) for both pre-training and downstream evaluation.
NSD is a
sensory-evoked 7T fMRI dataset in which eight participants viewed up to 10,000 images from MS
COCO (Lin et al., 2014) over 40 scanning sessions, where each image was presented up to three
times in total. We restrict our experiments to participants 1, 2, 5, and 7, the four who completed
all scanning sessions. For each participant, the training set comprises the 9,000 unique stimuli seen
only by that participant, while the 1,000 shared stimuli, which all participants viewed, are held out
for evaluation. We use the fsaverage-surface betas provided with NSD and z-score each session
independently within each participant, with train and test sets standardized separately. We then par-
cellate the surface data with the 500-parcel 7-network Schaefer atlas (Schaefer et al., 2018), yielding
500 parcels per trial.
We explore restricting the readout to a subset of parcels containing visually responsive regions.
We define this subset anatomically and independently of any functional response. Glasser et al.
(2016) group their 180 cortical areas into 22 coarser cortical divisions; we assign each Schaefer
parcel to the division containing the largest share of its vertices on the shared fsaverage surface, and
retain the parcel tokens assigned to the five visual divisions — Primary Visual, Early Visual, Dorsal
Stream Visual, Ventral Stream Visual, and MT+ Complex. This yields 66 parcels spanning 41,248
of the 299,761 labeled vertices (13.8%), 35 in the left and 31 in the right hemisphere. Because
the two atlases’ boundaries do not coincide, individual Schaefer parcels may span multiple Glasser
divisions; nonetheless, 44 of the 66 selected parcels lie entirely within visual divisions, and, on
average, 93.1% of a selected parcel’s vertices do. Since the criterion uses only anatomical atlas
overlap, the subset is identical across participants and involves no selection on the neural data.

3.3
GENERATIVE NEURAL DECODING
To evaluate the quality of our pre-trained encoder, we train generative decoding heads on top of the
resulting brain embeddings. Throughout, we keep the encoder frozen: its weights are never updated
by any task-specific learning signal. All readouts use the target encoder from the pre-training stage.
Image Reconstruction.
The first task is image reconstruction, where the goal is to generate the
image a participant viewed while their brain response was recorded. We adopt the MindEye2 (Scotti
et al., 2024) pipeline, which uses pre-trained CLIP and VAE image representations as supervision
targets for its decoder. Its head consumes a 256 × 1664 token sequence produced by the MindEye2
backbone. To bridge into this space from our brain embeddings of shape P×D, we apply an attentive
pooling layer with 256 queries followed by a linear map to 1664 dimensions. The pipeline trains
a contrastive projection alongside the reconstruction objective, from which we additionally report
retrieval: image retrieval ranks the correct image among a set of candidates given a brain response,
whereas brain retrieval ranks the correct brain response given an image. In contrast to MindEye2,
we do not pre-train the head on other subjects. The inputs also differ: MindEye2 operates on the
participant-specific nsdgeneral region, defined by the NSD authors as the voxels most responsive
to visual stimuli, whereas our encoders read the Schaefer parcellation.
Brain Captioning.
The second task is decoding textual descriptions of the stimulus images from
the brain embeddings. We use the CorText (Bosch et al., 2025) framework, which integrates brain
data into a pretrained LLM, enabling open-ended, natural-language decoding akin to visual question-
answering setups. Most LLM-based methods pair a brain encoder with a vision-language model and
train the encoder against the representations of that model’s vision encoder, so that image repre-
sentations act as supervision targets. CorText instead trains its brain encoder end-to-end from brain
responses alone, under a cross-entropy loss over target answer tokens sourced from VQA datasets
defined over COCO images (see Bosch et al. (2025) for more information). Our pre-trained en-
coders are therefore a drop-in replacement for its brain encoder, with no image supervision entering
the pipeline at any point. We project our brain embeddings into the representation space of the
pre-trained LLM (Llama 3.1 8B-Instruct) with a projection layer consisting of a two-layer MLP,
following common practice in vision-language models (Liu et al., 2024). Training proceeds in two
stages: first, only the projection layer is trained; second, the LLM is additionally tuned with low-rank
adaptation (Hu et al., 2022). The pre-trained brain encoder remains frozen in both stages.
3.4
CROSS-SUBJECT ALIGNMENT
To align our independently pre-trained brain encoders across participants, we employ the alignment
scheme of Marcos-Manch´on et al. (2026). The method matches the internal similarity structure of
two embedding spaces without paired samples: it summarizes each space with k-means centroids,
matches centroids across spaces by solving a quadratic assignment problem on their similarity struc-
ture, and uses the resulting correspondence to fit an orthogonal map via Procrustes transformation.
This initial map is then refined by iterative closest point, which alternates between re-estimating
pseudo-pairs under the current map and refitting it. All steps use only the representations of each
participant’s brain responses from the training set; no stimulus correspondence or label information
enters at any point.
While the pipeline of Marcos-Manch´on et al. (2026) produces a single 128-d embedding per partici-
pant, our encoders produce a sequence of P tokens of width D, with PD ≫128. Learning a single
map R ∈RP D×P D is infeasible, so we instead fit one orthogonal map per token, R(t) ∈RD×D.
We whiten the representations with each participant’s own training statistics — a single ZCA trans-
form shared across tokens rather than a per-token one — which improves alignment quality (see
Table 4). Writing WA and µA for participant A’s whitening matrix and mean, a token t of A’s brain

RESULTS
We report within-subject brain captioning and image reconstruction results in Tables 1 and 2, re-
spectively (see Appendix B for per-subject results and Appendix C for example reconstructions).
Two controls remove the information decoding depends on: a trial permutation control that breaks
the correspondence between brain responses and stimuli, and a zeroed betas control that replaces
the input with zeros. Our frozen pre-trained encoders outperform both controls by a wide margin on
every metric except SSIM in image reconstruction, which fails to separate the zeroed-betas control.
This is consistent with Scotti et al. (2024), who note that SSIM is unreliable and can score well even
for degenerate outputs. Decoding from the full parcel sequence and from the visual token subset
(Section 3.1) performs comparably on both tasks, indicating that the heads are largely insensitive to
the additional, mostly non-visual parcels. Captioning is on par with the fully supervised CorText
baseline; reconstruction trails MindEye2 slightly, plausibly because its head is pre-trained on seven
further subjects. Together, these results show that a representation learned without access to the
stimuli supports generative decoding at a level close to pipelines trained end-to-end against them.
Next, we turn to within-subject cross-trial retrieval (Table 3). Given one stimulus repetition, this
evaluation tests for retrieval of another repetition of the same stimulus, measuring how well a repre-
sentation preserves stimulus-related information while suppressing trial-specific variability. We first
apply it to the raw betas, which serve as a control: they ablate our pre-training entirely and establish
what the task yields with no learned representation at all. We then evaluate the brain embeddings
learned by our objective, along with each component’s contribution. The default JEPA baseline
— which draws context and target branches from the same trial and uses only the prediction loss
— falls below the raw betas. Both cross-trial pairing and the context loss are therefore necessary
for stimulus-discriminative representations, with the context loss yielding the largest gain. Relia-
bility weighting adds a small further improvement, visible mainly in mean rank, with Recall@1
essentially unchanged. Finally, restricting retrieval to the visual cortex token subset substantially
improves performance over the full token sequence, raising Recall@1 from 0.39 to 0.63 and reduc-
ing mean rank from 31.48 to 16.53, indicating that the remaining parcels contribute variability that
is not stimulus-locked.

Retrieval shows that the aligned spaces correspond, but the question alignment is ultimately meant
to answer is whether a decoding head can read them. We therefore transfer a head trained on one
subject to another without any refinement, and evaluate it on both decoding tasks (Tables 1 and 2;
results for all subject pairs in Appendix B and example reconstructions in Appendix C). As a control
(visual-readout w/o alignment), we transfer the head directly between subject encoders, ablating the
alignment transformation itself; performance collapses, confirming that the independently learned
subject spaces are not directly interchangeable. Mapping the other subject’s representations into the
space on which the head was trained substantially recovers decoding performance.
Within participants, the visual cortex token subset and the full sequence perform equivalently on both
tasks. Across participants, they diverge, and not in the same direction: the visual subset transfers
better for captioning (0.453 vs. 0.394 CIDEr), whereas the full sequence transfers slightly better
for reconstruction on the high-level metrics (+1.2 CLIP, +1.1 Inception). Since both decoders read
the same brain embeddings through the same alignment frames, the difference must lie in how they
consume the token sequence. We attribute it to the pooling stage: the reconstruction head pools its
input attentively and can learn which parcels to weight, whereas the captioning projector applies
fixed weights to every token. Poorly aligned non-visual parcels would then be discounted in the first
case and injected as noise in the second.

CONCLUSION
Latent prediction offers a common representational basis for expressive within-subject decoding and
for generalization across subjects. We trained one JEPA encoder per NSD participant, predicting the
latent representation of masked cortical parcels from a different repetition of the same stimulus. The
encoder never sees the stimulus images or any representation derived from them. Kept frozen, the
encoder supports both brain captioning and image reconstruction at a level close to CorText and
MindEye2, whose brain encoder is trained end-to-end on text or image targets.
Encoders trained independently for different subjects can also be aligned by orthogonal maps esti-
mated without paired data. After alignment, a decoding head trained on one subject can be applied
unchanged to another, retaining most of its within-subject quality on captioning and reconstruction
alike; without alignment, decoding collapses. Marcos-Manch´on et al. (2026) showed that com-
pact embeddings built for retrieval admit such maps. Our results extend this to representations rich
enough to drive generative decoders — which is what makes the alignment useful rather than merely
measurable.
9


Because neither pre-training nor alignment uses the content of the stimuli, and alignment needs no
stimuli shared between subjects, the approach can in principle combine data from subjects who saw
different stimuli. Future work will test whether pooling data in this way improves the learned repre-
sentations, whether alignment holds across datasets with different stimulus distributions, whether the
objective extends to data without repeated presentations, and whether the encoders can be adapted
to subjects unseen during pre-training.
