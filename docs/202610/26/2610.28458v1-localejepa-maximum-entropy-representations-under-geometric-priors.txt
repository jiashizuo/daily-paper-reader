ABSTRACT
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) offer an appealing approach to
self-supervised learning, but are typically prone to representation collapse. LeJEPA
resolves this by introducing a theoretically grounded distribution-matching ob-
jective that drives the embedding towards an isotropic Gaussian. However, this
approach is fundamentally tied to Euclidean assumptions, and its global target
does not reflect domain-specific geometric priors, such as the use of non-Euclidean
embedding geometries, e.g., equivariant CNNs or hyperbolic ViTs. We thus
introduce LocaLeJEPA, a framework motivated by a manifold-theoretic refor-
mulation of LeJEPA’s entropy-maximization property. The accompanying Lo-
caLeReg, a novel locally Euclidean distribution-matching regularizer, enables
a stable and scalable implementation of the LocaLeJEPA framework. We then
perform extensive experiments in the vision setting on models trained on ImageNet-
1K. LocaLeJEPA significantly outperforms LeJEPA when paired with hyperbolic
ViT-S/8 and equivariant ResNet-50 backbones, and also with ordinary Euclidean
ViT-S/8 and ViT-L/14 backbones. This holds both in terms of linear and k-NN
classification performance on ImageNet and on a series of downstream tasks
tested. We view the LocaLeJEPA formalism as a principled approach to the
JEPA paradigm that better respects intrinsic data geometry while also accom-
modating generalizations to exotic embedding geometries. Code available at:
https://github.com/alessioborgi/LocaLeJEPA.

INTRODUCTION
The Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) paradigm (Bromley et al., 1993; LeCun, 2022;
Assran et al., 2023) provides a tantalizing alternative to both feature-space masked modeling and
contrastive sample-based objectives for Self-Supervised Learning (SSL). Allowing the model to
instead learn relevant features by understanding the relationship between various semantically
overlapping ‘views’ of the same object in latent space, it ideally represents a simpler approach to
SSL. However, in practice, this learning objective falls prey to representation collapse, requiring the
incorporation of techniques such as stop-gradient (Chen & He, 2021), EMA schedulers (Caron et al.,
2021), and various whitening-based regularizers (Ermolov et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). The recent
Latent-Euclidean JEPA (LeJEPA) (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) bypasses these ad-hoc methodologies
and identifies the isotropic Gaussian as the optimal embedding distribution for downstream tasks.
Optimality is minimax, i.e., no other embedding distribution yields a lower worst-case risk for linear
regression or k-NN classification on an unknown downstream task. LeJEPA therefore adds a Gaussian
distribution matching objective to the JEPA loss, preventing collapse while being stable and scalable.
Consequent to the introduction of LeJEPA, there has been a flurry of activity in adapting this
methodology for use in video models (Kuhn et al., 2026a), world models (Maes et al., 2026), and
vision-language models (Kuhn et al., 2026b), as well as diverse domains such as time series (Chemeris
et al., 2026) and molecular representations (Rottach et al., 2026).

Motivation. Concurrently, however, another line of recent work questions the claimed optimality of
LeJEPA’s isotropic Gaussian, proposing instead objectives that match to rectified Gaussians (Kuang
et al., 2026), subspace Gaussians (Zhao et al., 2026), or the uniform distribution on the sphere
(Nicollier et al., 2026a;b). A throughline of these works is that they operate in regimes that violate
the fundamental Euclidean-space assumptions of LeJEPA, thus voiding the theoretical optimality of
the Gaussian distribution. For instance, Nicollier et al. (2026a) considers embeddings constrained to
a hypersphere Sd while Kuang et al. (2026) considers lp-normed spaces. As the JEPA framework is
increasingly adapted to diverse, and often explicitly non-Euclidean domains, this naturally raises the
question whether there is a principled generalization of LeJEPA to such domains that still retains its
theoretical grounding and its stability in practice.

Contribution. In this work, we introduce LocaLeJEPA, a manifold-theoretic JEPA objective
designed to couple the geometry of the data manifold M with that of an exotic embedding space N.
The accompanying regularizer, Locally Latent euclidean Regularization (LocaLeReg), drives the
embeddings to be locally uniformly distributed on a Euclidean ball. Since every manifold is locally
Euclidean and the uniform distribution is entropy maximization on a compact space, we view this as
a local adaptation of the global entropy maximization property of LeJEPA’s Gaussian distribution.
We implement this by first partitioning each batch of embeddings into K neighborhoods by K-means
in latent space, and then applying an Epps-Pulley-based distribution-matching objective to each
neighborhood. This retains LeJEPA’s sketched projections approach and thus inherits its linear cost
and stability, while allowing for a far more expressive embedding distribution due to locality. While
locality allows us to consider exotic N, even the global K = 1 case already improves on LeJEPA
trained with the same optimization settings, on ViT-S/8, hyperbolic ViT-S/8 and the D4-equivariant
ResNet-50 (Table 2). Our target distribution is also compact, theoretically allowing the encoder to
learn a continuous function with respect to M.
We experimentally instantiate LocaLeJEPA in the vision setting, though we note the framework is
suitably general to support diverse modalities such as graph data. Across Euclidean, hyperbolic,
and D4-equivariant backbones pretrained on Imagenette and ImageNet-1K, we show LocaLeJEPA
produces qualitatively better embeddings that inherently preserve the intrinsic topology of the data
manifold M, such as preserving known circular or toroidal features, while also accommodating
non-Euclidean embedding spaces N. We then quantitatively establish the downstream performance
of LocaLeJEPA’s embeddings, showing it outperforms LeJEPA under linear probing and k-NN classi-
fication using Euclidean, hyperbolic, and equivariant backbones, for all datasets tested. LocaLeJEPA
expands the scope of the JEPA paradigm as a whole while retaining the stability and scalability
afforded by the entropy-maximizing objective of LeJEPA.

RELATED WORKS
Self-Supervised Learning The goal of self-supervised learning (SSL) is to learn data representations
that capture intrinsic features that are useful for a wide-range of downstream tasks. Initial approaches
to SSL relied largely on positive-negative sample pairs combined with a contrastive loss, as in CPC
(van den Oord et al., 2018), MoCo (He et al., 2020), and SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020). However,
requiring such pairs can be costly and inefficient. This led to the development of non-contrastive
approaches such as BYOL (Grill et al., 2020), SimSiam (Chen & He, 2021), Barlow Twins (Zbontar
et al., 2021), VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022), which instead involve objectives based on learning
invariant features with respect to certain transformations. The JEPA paradigm extends this non-
contrastive approach and may be understood as instead predicting a latent image representation from
semantically overlapping views (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023).
SSL with Geometric Priors The adaptation of principles of geometric deep learning and its incorpo-
ration of geometric priors is still a growing subfield of SSL. Initial works in this field have explored
applications of equivariance to SSL, such as in the contrastive learning setting (Dangovski et al., 2022)
or as alternatives to invariance-based learning objectives (Wang et al., 2024). In another direction,
self-supervised and unsupervised approaches have also seen applications to 3D and point-cloud data,
as in Xie et al. (2020) or Zhang et al. (2021). Other works have also used geometric architectures
towards representation learning for molecular data, such as Stärk et al. (2022) and Liu et al. (2022a),
as well as for graph-valued data more generally (Veliˇckovi´c et al., 2019; You et al., 2020; Hassani &
Khasahmadi, 2020; Thakoor et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2026).

THEORETICAL MOTIVATION
By the manifold hypothesis (Fefferman et al., 2016), we may suppose that our data lies on a compact
Riemannian manifold M, though embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, i.e., M ⊂RD.
The general objective of self-supervised learning may be understood as learning a f : RD →N, for
N an appropriate embedding space for the downstream tasks under consideration. For simplicity, we

Riemannian manifold, carrying the metric induced from N. Here, taking N to be the Euclidean Rd
recovers the setting of LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) while letting N be the hypersphere Sd
recovers the setting of Sphere-JEPA (Nicollier et al., 2026a). However, we may consider more general
geometric priors. For instance, we may consider N to be hyperbolic space or a space equivariant
under actions of a given Lie group.

distribution on N. While there is no canonical analog of a Gaussian distribution such that it is

Theorem 3.1 therefore lets us define a ‘Gaussian’ intrinsically on a manifold, as the heat kernel
KW(x, ·, t) centered at x with t in the role of a variance. That identification holds only as t →0,
however, and in exactly that regime the kernel is concentrated near x. An intrinsic Gaussian is
therefore a strictly local object on W. As such, in practice, we may consider a local version of
LeJEPA’s SIGReg as an appropriate analog for the manifold setting. Unfortunately, this naive
approach does not preserve LeJEPA’s optimality guarantees. For instance, in Sphere-JEPA (Nicollier
et al., 2026a), it is shown that for the case N = Sd, the uniform distribution is optimal for downstream
linear regression and k-NN classification tasks, and they propose SUSReg, a distribution matching
objective that instead drives towards the uniform distribution. Taking on a heat flow perspective
allows us to interpolate between these objectives on an arbitrary N, of which the sphere is a special
case. In particular, we then have an entire family of distributions KW(·, ·, t), parameterized by time,
that may reasonably be understood as targets for a distribution-matching objective that ‘lifts’ LeJEPA
to manifolds. However, this would not consider the entropy maximization property of the Gaussian
distribution that has been tied to the success of LeJEPA, in works such as Rectified LpJEPA (Kuang
et al., 2026). More generally, an entropy-maximization objective has been shown to be desirable in
SSL (Liu et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2025). We recall that the usual Gaussian
distribution, under a given variance, is the unique entropy-maximizing distribution in Euclidean
space, while the uniform distribution is the unique entropy-maximizing distribution on an arbitrary
compact Riemannian manifold. The optimal transport perspective allows us to understand heat flow
as gradient flow towards entropy maximization on an arbitrary manifold.

As such, under this additional constraint of entropy maximization, the optimal embedding distribution
sits at the t →∞end of the heat-flow family. That is, we want f(M) ⊂N to be ‘maximally spread
out’ while preserving the relevant geometry of M. The simple ‘geometry preservation’ assumption
already precludes the final embedding from being Gaussian even in the Euclidean setting N = Rd.
Indeed, a continuous f carries the compact M to a compact image, and thus driving towards the
infinitely-supported Gaussian distributions necessarily causes us to learn an f that ‘tears’ M. Thus,
we wish to modify the LeJEPA paradigm such that i. we can preserve the structure of M and ii.
without conditioning on a predetermined N, while iii. still allowing for an implementation that is
stable and scalable (Section A.4). To drive towards a true entropy-maximizing distribution would
typically require either a static but globally formulated objective, conditioned on a fixed choice of N
and violating ii, or a local-to-global heat flow style objective exploiting Theorem 3.2, which would
then require dynamic re-computations of the distribution matching objective during training, likely
violating iii. As most reasonable notions of ‘geometry preserving’ for i would imply that f(M)
should be locally Euclidean, we propose a compromise locally entropy maximizing objective, by
driving towards the uniform distribution on a Euclidean ball Bd on neighborhood clusters.

from the centered local target, our local entropy-maximizing reference, and locality alone does not

6
CONCLUSION
LocaLeJEPA brings a manifold-theoretic perspective to the JEPA paradigm, introducing the heuristic
that self-supervised training should behave like heat flow. To implement this heuristic, we develop
the novel LocaLeReg regularizer, based upon a locally Euclidean entropy-maximizing objective. We
find that both qualitatively and quantitatively, LocaLeJEPA produces embeddings that better preserve
intrinsic semantic structure and can adapt to the geometry of the representation space, while being
stable and scalable. Comparisons at matched optimization settings, on four encoders up to ViT-L/14
and over three pretraining seeds on ViT-S/8, show that the gains do not come from the training settings.
Within the same neighborhoods, an uncentered Gaussian target loses all of the gain over LeJEPA (our
settings), so locality alone does not explain it. While we instantiate our framework on image data
in the current work, we believe LocaLeJEPA represents a general-use framework that allows for a
principled extension of the JEPA paradigm to settings that require handling non-Euclidean data or
using non-Euclidean representations.

Minimum Neighborhood Size.
Although the Epps–Pulley discrepancy is defined for any nonempty
sample, very small neighborhoods provide limited information about the shape of a distribution.
Centering further constrains their empirical geometry. For instance, a singleton becomes identically
zero, while a two-point neighborhood reduces to a pair of opposite residuals. As a result, a discrepancy
computed from very few points need not reliably distinguish the uniform ball target from alternative
distributions. We therefore exclude neighborhoods containing fewer than four images. This minimum
size is a heuristic choice. It is neither a theoretical condition for the statistic to be defined nor a
guarantee of reliable distribution discrimination.

ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
This appendix complements Section 5 with the full experimental specification underlying the reported
results. We first consolidate the experimental conventions shared across studies in Section C.1,
including the representation interface, training and checkpointing conventions, and frozen-feature
evaluation protocol. We then provide some more details on the baselines employed in Section C.2, the
experiment-specific settings and analyses for the torus geometry-preservation study in Section C.3, fol-
lowed by the ImageNet-1K experiments across Euclidean, hyperbolic, and equivariant representation
spaces in Section C.4.
C.1
SHARED EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL
Across all experiments, we adopt a common interface between self-supervised pretraining and
downstream evaluation. The encoder produces a 512-dimensional representation, which is kept frozen
for all downstream readouts, while the self-supervised regularizer is applied after a 2048–2048–16
MLP (ViT-L/14: 2048–2048–512, LeJEPA’s own) projector. This separation allows us to evaluate the
information retained by the learned representation independently of the lower-dimensional space in
which distribution matching is enforced.
Training Details. Each image contributes four views (ViT-L/14: two global and six local views). On
ImageNet-1K, each optimizer step processes 256 images (ViT-L/14: 512) in shards of 64 images,
one per GPU, without gradient accumulation. On Imagenette, the per-step batch is stated with each
experiment (64 images with four accumulation steps, or 256 with one). Local neighborhood assign-
ments and distribution statistics, however, are computed independently within each 64-image shard
on ImageNet-1K and within each per-step batch on Imagenette. Experiment-specific optimization
choices, including the number of training epochs, learning rate, and regularization parameters, are
reported in the corresponding sections below.
Online Evaluation and Checkpointing.
Following Balestriero & LeCun (2025), we train a
lightweight classifier on detached encoder features during pretraining as an online diagnostic. Its
gradients never propagate through the encoder or projector and therefore do not affect representation
learning. Each model is trained for the complete prescribed schedule. The ImageNet-1K tables
and the Imagenette ablations of Section B use the final checkpoint, at epoch 100 and epoch 800,
respectively. The rotation-recovery study (Section C.3) evaluates each encoder at the checkpoint
with the best online-probe accuracy. The COIL-100 analysis (Section D.1) reports both the final
checkpoint and the one with the best online-probe accuracy.
Frozen-feature Evaluation. All downstream evaluations keep the pretrained encoder fixed and fit
only the specified readout. Reported differences therefore reflect information already present in the
frozen representation, since the encoder is not adapted to a particular downstream task.

in which the locality of the regularizer is progressively refined during pretraining. At epoch e, the
number of neighborhoods is:

C.2
DISTRIBUTION-MATCHING OBJECTIVES AND REFERENCE DISTRIBUTIONS
Our comparisons span distribution-matching objectives that differ in both the domain on which
regularization is imposed and the reference distribution toward which representations are driven. We
make this important distinction in our evaluation because LocaLeJEPA changes the regularization
from a single global constraint to a collection of locally centered distribution-matching problems,
while preserving the same self-supervised prediction objective.
27


Global baselines. SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025), the regularizer underlying LeJEPA, matches
projected representations globally to an isotropic Gaussian. SUSReg (Nicollier et al., 2026a) instead
promotes uniformity on the hypersphere, providing a reference distribution adapted to spherical
embeddings. SubReg (Zhao et al., 2026) applies Gaussian regularization within frozen random
feature subspaces of the complete projected representation. Finally, RDMReg, introduced by Rectified
LpJEPA (Kuang et al., 2026), matches rectified representations to a generalized-Gaussian target. We
use p=1, corresponding to the rectified Laplace distribution.
Except for RDMReg, these objectives can be written as:
L = (1 −λ) Lpred + λ R
(45)
where Lpred matches the representations of the different views of each image and R denotes the
corresponding distribution-matching regularizer. RDMReg instead follows the weighting proposed
by its authors:
LRDM = 25 Lpred + 125 RRDM
(46)
whose coefficients are absolute and therefore not directly comparable to the mixing parameter λ used
by the other objectives.
LocaLeJEPA and Local Control. LocaLeJEPA replaces the global reference constraint with
distribution matching within learned neighborhoods. Projected representations are averaged over
views and partitioned once per step by cosine-similarity K-means using at most ten iterations, and
the partition is shared by all views. In order to avoid uninformative clusters, we discard clusters
containing fewer than four samples, and the regularizer is averaged uniformly across the remaining
clusters. Each cluster is centered independently, and the resulting residuals are matched to the uniform
distribution on the d-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius:
R =
√
d + 2
(47)
which has identity covariance. With the d=16 projector used throughout our experiments, this gives

18.

LocaLeJEPA but replaces the centered bounded reference with Gaussian matching on the uncentered
cluster representations. This comparison tests whether local partitioning alone is sufficient to explain
the gains of LocaLeJEPA, while not separately isolating the effects of centering and reference
distribution.

be explained by locality alone and that the centered ball construction contributes to them. However,
the experiment does not disentangle the contributions of centering and of the reference distribution.

SPATIAL STRUCTURE OF ROTATION RECOVERY
We also investigate the visualization of the spatial structure of the rotation recovery experiment to
expose where the errors occur over the underlying angular domain, a factor that the aggregate probe
metrics do not reveal. For that purpose, we use (θ0, θ1) as coordinates on T2 and embed the resulting
torus in R3 for visualization. These plots should be interpreted as visualizations of coordinate
recovery. Their geometry is determined only by the ground-truth or probe-decoded angles. They
are not a direct embedding of the encoder features themselves. They therefore show how faithfully
the periodic factors are recovered across the angular domain, but do not by themselves establish the
topology of the learned representation space.
Results. Figure 6 provides the basic construction. The first two panels show the ground-truth torus
colored by the two periodic coordinates, while the final panel places held-out frames according to the
angles decoded from fixed-K LocaLeJEPA (λ=0.1, K=2) and colors them by their angular error.
The decoded coordinates cover the full angular domain, with most residual error remaining small
relative to the range of the two periodic variables. Figure 7 extends this view across all evaluated
encoders and probe-label budgets. Rows correspond to representations and columns to 10, 50, 100,
500, and all 5,182 probe-training frames. Each panel reports the MAE for the same held-out split
and colors individual predictions using a shared angular error scale. The progression across columns
makes the effect of probe supervision visible directly: sparse-label probes produce more dispersed
and higher-error reconstructions, whereas increasing the training budget progressively concentrates
the predictions around the underlying toroidal coordinate structure. To compare methods spatially,

C.4
IMAGENET-1K: TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
This section specifies the experimental choices particular to the ImageNet-1K study underlying
Sections 5.1 and 5.2. We first describe the three encoder geometries and their optimization settings,
and then detail the frozen-feature classification and downstream-transfer protocols. Shared training,
checkpointing, and representation conventions follow Section C.1, while the compared distribution-
matching objectives are defined in Section C.2.
C.4.1
IMAGENET-1K: PRETRAINING DETAILS
Following the experimental setup of (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025), we pretrain all models for 100
epochs on the 1.28M-image ImageNet-1K (Deng et al., 2009) training split.
Encoder Families. We consider three encoder families spanning distinct representation geometries.
The Euclidean encoder is a standard ViT-S/8 (21.7M parameters). The Hyperbolic encoder uses
the same ViT-S/8 backbone and maps its output to a Poincaré ball of curvature −0.1, with feature
clipping at radius 2.3 (Ermolov et al., 2022). Finally, the D4-equivariant encoder is a 24.4M-
parameter ResNet-50 implemented with escnn (Klee et al., 2023), retaining the regular representation
at its output without pooling to an invariant feature. All three encoders feed the common Euclidean
projector defined in Section C.1. We also train a ViT-L/14 (303.2M parameters) at 238 × 238 pixels,
with LeJEPA’s published ViT-L settings for both methods.
Details. All runs use AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay and weight decay 5 × 10−2. Global
SIGReg at LeJEPA’s published settings and RDMReg use one warmup epoch, a learning rate of
2 × 10−3 for all three encoders, and decay to half the peak rate. LocaLeJEPA, its local control
and LeJEPA (our settings) use a learning rate of 10−3, ten warmup epochs, decay to 10−3 of the
peak rate, and gradient clipping at 1.0. SIGReg uses λ=0.02 at LeJEPA’s published settings and
λ=0.1 in LeJEPA (our settings), while the LocaLeJEPA values of λ and K are reported with the
corresponding configurations. RDMReg uses the objective weighting defined in Section C.2. For
the dynamic-neighborhood variant, we instantiate the schedule of Section C.1 with Kmax = 10, and
Estep = 10, so that the partition evolves from K=1 to K=10 over the 100-epoch training schedule,
reaching its maximum granularity at epoch 90. Following the shared checkpointing protocol described
in Section C.1, every downstream evaluation uses the final epoch-100 checkpoint. For ViT-L/14,
both methods use LeJEPA’s published ViT-L settings, with a learning rate of 5 × 10−4, ten warmup
epochs, decay to 10−3 of the peak rate, batches of 512 images, λ=0.05, 1024 slicing directions and
an integration range of [−5, 5].
C.4.2
IMAGENET-1K FROZEN-FEATURE READOUTS
Following the in-domain evaluation protocol of SPHERE-JEPA (Nicollier et al., 2026a), the ImageNet-
1K training split forms the feature bank and the validation split is used for evaluation. Images are
resized and center-cropped to 128 × 128 (238 × 238 for ViT-L/14) and normalized using ImageNet
statistics.
k-NN and Linear Probes.
For what concerns k-NN probe, the frozen representations are
ℓ2-normalized and compared using cosine similarity.
Class votes are weighted according to
exp(sim /0.07). We evaluate k ∈{10, 20, 100, 200} and use k=20 in the main paper. Across
the tested configurations, this value is within 0.81 percentage points of the best setting, and the
ordering of methods is the same at every k on ViT-S/8 and ViT-L/14. On the other two encoders, three
pairs of runs that are within one point of each other swap places. Instead, in the linear probe, frozen
representations are standardized using statistics computed from the ImageNet-1K training split. We fit
a single affine classifier for 50 epochs using SGD with momentum 0.9, zero weight decay, batch size
4096, and cosine learning-rate decay. The learning rate is selected from {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10},
using a 2% holdout from the training split. After selection, the classifier is refitted on the complete
training split and evaluated once on the ImageNet-1K validation set. Both readouts operate on the
same frozen epoch-100 representations. ViT-S/8 results for LeJEPA, LeJEPA (our settings) and
LocaLeJEPA with K=1 (at both weights) and K=4 are means over three pretraining seeds. All other

Regularization Strength. At K=2, λ=0.1 gives stronger ImageNet classification than λ=0.3 on

Neighborhood granularity. At λ=0.1, the dynamic schedule stays within 0.4 points of fixed K=4

D.1
TOPOLOGY PRESERVATION ON COIL DATASET
Data and training.
COIL-100 (Nene et al., 1996) contains 7200 images of 100 objects, each taken
on a turntable at 72 poses 5◦apart. For a fixed object the frames sample a closed curve θ 7→xθ,
θ ∈S1, in image space, and we ask whether an encoder f maps this curve to a closed curve in
embedding space or collapses it. We train ViT-S/8 encoders with LocaLeReg and SIGReg for 100
epochs with appearance-only augmentations (color jitter, grayscale, Gaussian blur) and analyze the
512-d encoder output.
Persistent homology.
To measure whether the 72 embeddings {f(xθ)} of an object lie on a loop
we use persistent homology (Edelsbrunner et al., 2002; Zomorodian & Carlsson, 2005). For a scale
r ≥0, the Vietoris–Rips complex VRr contains a simplex for every set of points whose pairwise
distances are at most r. These complexes are nested as r grows, and each topological feature appears
at some scale b (its birth) and disappears at a scale d (its death): in dimension 0 the features are
connected components, in dimension 1 they are loops. The persistence diagram plots every feature
as the point (b, d), and its lifetime d −b is the range of scales over which it exists. For a sampled
closed curve, the loop is born once consecutive samples connect, and dies once the hole fills in. A

from the same ImageNet-1K validation images are projected onto their first three principal components
and mapped to RGB. Each model uses an independently fitted PCA basis, so colors are directly

Results. For each backbone–objective pair, we select five ImageNet-1K validation images from the
golden-retriever class (class 207) and extract spatial features from the frozen encoder immediately
before global aggregation. For the Euclidean and hyperbolic ViT-S/8 encoders, we use the 384-
dimensional tokens after the final transformer normalization, excluding prefix tokens, pooling, and
output heads. At the 128 × 128 input resolution, these features form a 16 × 16 spatial grid. For
the hyperbolic encoder, features are taken immediately before the Poincaré-ball mapping. For the
D4-equivariant ResNet-50, we use the 512-channel equivariant feature map immediately before
average pooling. For each model, spatial features from the five images are concatenated into an N ×d
matrix, where the N spatial locations are observations and the d feature channels are variables. PCA
is fitted once to this matrix, and the three leading components are retained. We fix the sign of each
component by requiring its largest-magnitude loading to be positive and independently rescale the
resulting component values to [0, 1]. The three channels are then mapped to RGB and reshaped to the
native spatial grid. For visualization only, the result is upsampled to 512×512 using nearest-neighbor
40


interpolation and overlaid on the input image at 55% opacity. No foreground masking or thresholding
is applied. Figure 10 compares LocaLeJEPA (λ=0.1, K=4) with the corresponding SIGReg baseline
on four representative images. Both objectives exhibit visible object–background structure in their
leading principal components. These qualitative plots allow the PCA to identify directions of feature
variance without using semantic labels, and each model uses an independently fitted PCA basis. As a
result, colors are comparable across images within a row, but not across independently fitted models,
and the visualization should not be interpreted as a quantitative segmentation or localization result.
D.3
CLASS CONSISTENCY OF FROZEN K-NN NEIGHBORHOODS
On the three 128-pixel encoders, the largest ImageNet-1K improvement of LocaLeJEPA over LeJEPA
(our settings) appears under the k-NN readout (Table 19), which depends directly on the local
geometry of the frozen representation. We therefore examine whether this improvement is reflected in
the composition of the neighborhoods on which the classifier operates. Specifically, we ask whether
the nearest ImageNet-1K training examples of a validation image are more likely to share its semantic
class under LocaLeJEPA than under SIGReg. We use exactly the representation space employed by
the main k-NN evaluation: the 128 × 128 evaluation view, frozen 512-dimensional encoder features,
cosine similarity, and the complete 1.28M-image ImageNet-1K training split as the retrieval bank.
This analysis uses encoders from earlier pretraining runs, not those reported in Table 2.
Qualitative Retrieval. Figure 11 visualizes the eight nearest training examples for three query
images under the Euclidean ViT-S/8. As shown, for the polar-bear query, the number of same-class
neighbors increases from four under SIGReg to seven under LocaLeJEPA. For the Komodo dragon, it
rises from one to five, and for the triceratops, from three to four. Several SIGReg neighbors instead
share strong visual attributes with the query (such as color or background) without sharing its class.
The aggregate analysis below evaluates whether this pattern extends beyond these examples.
Neighborhood Class Consistency. For a validation image x with class label c(x), let Nk(x) denote
its k nearest training examples under cosine similarity. We define the neighborhood class consistency
as:

Thus, qk(x)=1 when every retrieved neighbor belongs to the query class and qk(x)=0 when none

Results. Table 23 reports the statistic over all 1,000 ImageNet-1K classes, grouped by WordNet
category. Under SIGReg, approximately 13% of the top-20 neighborhood shares the query class
for each of the three encoder families. LocaLeJEPA increases this fraction to approximately 20%
for the Euclidean ViT-S/8 and D4-equivariant ResNet-50, and to 21% for the hyperbolic ViT-S/8.
LocaLeJEPA increases class consistency in every reported WordNet group and in 989, 984, and 971
of the 1,000 individual classes for the Euclidean, hyperbolic, and equivariant encoders, respectively.
Table 24 provides a representative 36-class breakdown, where LocaLeJEPA yields higher consistency
in 105 of the 108 class–encoder comparisons. Absolute neighborhood consistency varies widely
across semantic groups. Fine-grained families containing many visually similar categories, such as
dog breeds and reptiles, exhibit lower class purity under both objectives, whereas visually distinct
categories such as zebra, daisy, and monarch butterfly show much higher values. This behavior
is expected for ImageNet-1K: the weighted k-NN prediction requires only that the correct class
receive the largest aggregate vote, not that a majority of retrieved samples share the query label.
Hence, the k-NN top-1 accuracies of these encoders are compatible with neighborhoods whose
majority consists of other classes. Together, the retrieval examples and full-validation statistics
show that the k-NN improvement of LocaLeJEPA is accompanied by a systematic increase in local
semantic class consistency across architectures and categories. This analysis directly characterizes
the neighborhoods used by the classifier and needs no additional learned readout.
