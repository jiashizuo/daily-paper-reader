Abstract
Hierarchical and modular organization is a defining feature of brain architecture, yet how it supports flexible
computation and functional specialization remains largely unclear. Likewise, although dynamic range is a hallmark of
efficient information processing, it has been studied predominantly as a global network property, leaving how it is
distributed across modules and contributes to computation within hierarchical levels largely unexplored. Here, we
show that hierarchical-modular networks naturally give rise to heterogeneous modular dynamics and firing rates,
generating extended regimes of critical-like dynamics with enhanced intrinsic timescales, reflecting critical slowing
down, and diverse scaling exponents, without fine-tuning when the system operates near a critical point. Progressively
longer timescales emerge at higher hierarchical levels, generating a hierarchy of intrinsic timescales, and spread of
timescales within hierarchical levels arises only for networks with heterogeneity in the connectivity profile and on
firing rate. We introduce the concept of modular dynamic range to characterize how individual modules respond to
external inputs and show that heterogeneity enhances both global and module-specific sensitivity, with module-specific
intrinsic timescales increasing with dynamic range. Under local stimulation of a single module, dynamic range is
reduced in homogeneous networks but enhanced in heterogeneous networks relative to global stimulation, in which all
modules receive external input. Moreover, varying the hierarchical level of the stimulated module reveals a principle of
functional specialization: Selective stimulation of intermediate hierarchical levels maximizes both global and modular
dynamic range. Modular dynamic range is more variable in heterogeneous networks and is enhanced in modules that
do not receive direct stimulation, revealing distributed computational benefits that extend beyond the stimulated
region. Together, these results show that heterogeneity in hierarchical-modular networks can extend critical-like
dynamics while generating hierarchical timescales and a gradient of modular dynamic range, providing a mechanistic
basis for distributed computation and functional specialization without fine-tuning to criticality.
Author summary
The remarkable flexibility of the brain is supported by its hierarchical and modular architecture, yet how this
structural organization enables efficient and flexible computation remains poorly understood. Using computational
excitable models of hierarchical-modular networks, we show that heterogeneity in neural dynamics is not merely
biological noise or variability, but a fundamental design principle. Hierarchical-modular networks can naturally
generate a hierarchy of intrinsic timescales, with progressively slower dynamics emerging at higher hierarchical levels,
while extending the regime of critical-like dynamics without the need for fine-tuning. We introduce the concept of
modular dynamic range to quantify how individual modules respond to external inputs and show that heterogeneity
enhances both local and global sensitivity to stimulation. Moreover, selective stimulation of specific hierarchical levels
can maximize network-wide and module-specific responses, revealing a potential mechanism for functional
specialization. These computational benefits extend beyond directly stimulated modules, demonstrating how local
interactions can produce distributed enhancements in network responsiveness. Together, our findings suggest that
heterogeneity is not a biological imperfection to be averaged away, but a fundamental organizational principle that
supports hierarchical timescales, distributed information processing, and flexible computation.

20

22

23

30

31

36

Results
53
We investigate the dynamical properties that arise in hierarchical-modular networks (Figs. 1 and 2), proposed by
54
Moretti and Mu˜noz [12], and their role in facilitating efficient computation. The inherent organization of these
55
networks offers a robust and versatile framework for generating a broad spectrum of biologically relevant dynamical
56
behaviors without requiring precise parameter tuning. We introduce the fundamental structural (Tables 1 and 2) and
57
dynamical characteristics of these networks, emphasizing the emergence of heterogeneous dynamics, and explore the
58
implications of this heterogeneity of dynamics across modules. We focus on how this heterogeneity shapes the
59
dynamic repertoire of the network and its components, contributes to a rich diversity of INT, and improves the
60
dynamic range of the network.
61
2.1
Hierarchical-modular networks (HMN)
62
We study the dynamics of hierarchical-modular networks in which each network has N nodes with m modules, h
63
hierarchical levels, and M0 nodes in the innermost modules (Fig. 2 A-D), such that N = M0 × mh−1. Connectivity
64
depends on the hierarchical level ℓ= {0, 1, 2, . . . , h −1}, with the connection density ϕℓwithin each module given by
65
ϕℓ= α · ϕh−ℓ, where α and ϕ are control parameters (Fig. 2 E, F).
66
The parameter α determines the finite network dimension D (Fig. 2 G) and is related to the network’s mean
67
degree [20]. The network dimension is a key metric that characterizes the scaling of the “hyper-sphere” occupied by
68
the network nodes [62,63], i.e., how the number of reached nodes Nr increases with path length r: Nr ∼rD. Low-D
69
networks rapidly saturate their node count, whereas infinite-dimensional networks exhibit growth faster than any
70
power law. Networks with ϕ > 2−m are considered infinite-dimensional regardless of the value of α [12,20].
71
Throughout this work, we contrast these networks with their finite-dimensional counterparts to investigate the impact
72
of structural heterogeneity on network dynamics.
73
The parameter ϕ directly controls the network’s sparsity, with lower values producing sparser connectivity and
74
higher values producing denser connectivity. Since ϕ < 1, the connection density decreases with increasing hierarchical
75
level, giving rise to progressively coarser modular organization, as illustrated by the adjacency matrices in Fig. 2 A, B.
76
The HMN topology naturally defines modules according to their location in the adjacency matrix, with each
77
module corresponding to a specific set of neurons. To simplify their representation, we introduce a modular coordinate
78
to identify the module to which each neuron belongs, complementing the traditional neuron indexing from 1 to N.
79
This coordinate consists of a sequence of h −1 numbers ranging from 1 to m, where each digit specifies the module
80
index at its corresponding hierarchical level. This representation allows us to naturally assign and analyze both
81
neuronal activity and external stimuli at the module level. See Section 4.6 for further details.

2.2
Sparsity enhances dynamical heterogeneity in hierarchical-modular networks
83
The neuronal dynamics is simulated using the susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) model, in which each neuron is
84
represented as a two-state cellular automaton: resting (si = 0) or excited (si = 1). A neuron becomes excited either
85
through an external Poisson stimulus with rate r, occurring with probability η(r) = 1 −e−r at each time step
86
(δt = 1ms), or through excitation from an active neighboring neuron with probability p. The resulting dynamics
87
generate a time-dependent density of active sites, ρ(t), whose temporal average corresponds to the network activity
88
(F).
89
The connectivity matrix C is the adjacency matrix A multiplied by propagation probability p (i.e., C = pA). The
90
key control parameter governing the dynamics is λ, defined as the largest eigenvalue of C [57].
91
In the absence of external input, the model exhibits a continuous phase transition as λ is varied [64]. For weak
92
coupling, activity cannot be sustained and eventually dies out, whereas sufficiently strong coupling gives rise to
93
persistent self-sustained activity. These distinct dynamical regimes are illustrated in panel A of Fig. 3 for different

2.3
Heterogeneity stretches criticality and breaks the mean-field approximation
111
Random networks are effectively infinite-dimensional and exhibit a continuous second-order phase transition [48,57],
112
with F0, the mean activity in the absence of external stimulation, serving as the order parameter and λ as the control
113
parameter. This mean-field description of the directed percolation phase transition is exact only in the limit of
114
homogeneous, infinite-dimensional networks [12]. In contrast, dynamical heterogeneity of hierarchical-modular
115
networks can fundamentally alter the critical behavior. This is exacerbated in finite-dimensional systems but can also
116
be relevant in the dynamics of hierarchical-modular networks with infinite dimension. Different dynamical properties
117
can reflect the distance to criticality [35,44], including the autocorrelation function, as recently chararcterized in
118
HMNs [26].
119
The dynamical effects of finite dimensionality in HMNs is illustrated in Fig. 4A,B. In the infinite-dimensional
120
network (Fig. 4A), the transition between the absorbing and active phases is sharply localized around a single critical
121
coupling. By comparison, the finite-dimensional network (Fig. 4B) displays a broad region of enhanced fluctuations,
122
reflected by the stretched peak in the standard deviation of the spontaneous activity, σF0. These large fluctuations
123
coincide with the extended parameter region in which power-law activity is observed, a hallmark of Griffiths-like
124
dynamics.
125
Another characteristic signature of criticality is critical slowing down, whereby perturbations decay increasingly
126
slowly as the system approaches the critical regime. To quantify this phenomenon, we computed the temporal
127
autocorrelation function (ACF) of the activity density ρ(t) and measured the associated intrinsic neural timescale
128
(INT), defined as the area under the ACF from lag 0 to the first lag k satisfying ACF(k)=0.1. Consistent with
129
previous studies [42,43], infinite-dimensional networks exhibit a sharp peak in INT precisely at the critical point
130
(Fig. 4C). In contrast, finite-dimensional networks display a broad plateau of elevated INT values extending across a
131
wide range of coupling strengths (Fig. 4D). Rather than peaking sharply, INT decreases gradually beyond its
132
maximum, indicating that critical slowing down persists throughout an extended parameter region. Together with the
133
broadened fluctuations in spontaneous activity (Fig. 4B) and the extended power-law regime in ρ(t) (Fig. 3), these
134
results demonstrate that dynamical heterogeneity of HMN stretches the signatures of criticality into a broad

2.4
A hierarchy of timescales emerges in hierarchical-modular networks
146
Whole-network measures provide a useful characterization of the critical behavior of hierarchical-modular networks
147
and have helped explain several aspects of brain dynamics [20,48,65,66]. However, they represent spatial averages
148
over the entire network and therefore obscure the variability arising from individual modules. This limitation becomes
149
particularly important in strongly heterogeneous networks, where disorder and rare-region effects are expected to
150
produce substantial local differences in the dynamics. To capture these effects, we exploit the modular and
151
hierarchical organization of the network by analyzing the activity of modules independently. Instead of a single time
152
series for the global activity density ρ(t), we compute one time series for each module at every hierarchical level,
153
following the procedure described in Section 2.1. Each modular time series is then analyzed using the same procedure
154
employed for the whole network (Fig. 4), allowing us to compute the corresponding mean activity F0, autocorrelation
155
function, and intrinsic neural timescale (INT). This modular analysis enables us to investigate how signatures of
156
criticality emerge locally within different regions of the network.
157
Our results reveal that heterogeneity, controlled by ϕ, strongly increases the diversity of module dynamics. As
158
shown in Fig. 6A, the mean activity of individual modules in heterogeneous networks spans a broad range of values,
159
with some overlap between neighboring hierarchical levels, in sharp contrast to the much narrower distribution
160
observed in homogeneous networks (Fig. 6D). This broad dispersion persists beyond the global transition, indicating
161
that different modules can simultaneously occupy distinct dynamical regimes. Rather than undergoing a collective
162
phase transition, the disordered connectivity promotes local dynamical heterogeneity, allowing subcritical modules to
163
coexist with others that already exhibit self-sustained activity across an extended range of coupling strengths.
164
This heterogeneous behavior is even more evident when examining the intrinsic neural timescales. The histograms
165
in Fig. 6C,F show that INT generally grows for higher hierarchical levels (lower values of ℓ), and that the distribution
166
of INT values is substantially broader in heterogeneous networks than in homogeneous ones across all hierarchical
167
levels. The effect is strongest in the innermost modules (ℓ= h −1 = 7), where the range of INT values is
168
approximately three times larger than in the homogeneous network. These results demonstrate that critical slowing
169
down is itself spatially heterogeneous, providing direct evidence that criticality is distributed across modules rather

A direct consequence of this modular heterogeneity is that, even for coupling strengths near the onset of the
172
globally active phase (e.g., λ ≈1.04 in Fig. 6A), modules at the same hierarchical level can exhibit markedly different
173
dynamical states. While some innermost modules remain predominantly subcritical, others already display
174

175

176
pronounced in heterogeneous networks, whereas homogeneous networks exhibit weaker correlations together with

178

179
findings demonstrate that decreasing ϕ gives rise to a hierarchy of local dynamical states, with individual modules
180
approaching criticality (transition to the self-sustained state) at different coupling strengths.

2.5
Heterogeneity-driven recruitment enhances stimulus discrimination
182

183
feature of neural computation. To assess this capability, we extend our analysis beyond spontaneous dynamics and
184

185

the maximum dynamic range as the topological dimension D decreases (Fig. 7D).
199
This enhancement originates from systematic changes in the shape of the response curves. Networks with lower
200
topological dimension exhibit a steeper increase in activity for weak stimuli, allowing them to distinguish subtle
201
variations in input while delaying saturation at higher stimulus intensities (Fig. 7B). Consequently, these networks can
202
achieve substantially larger dynamic ranges than their high-dimensional counterparts.
203
In addition to the larger values of ∆, low-dimensional networks exhibit a distinctive hallmark of extended critical
204
regimes, reminiscent of Griffiths-like phases. The dependence of ∆on the control parameter λ exhibits broad, smooth
205
maxima rather than the narrow peaks characteristic of conventional critical points (Fig. 7C). These properties
206
indicate that the enhanced sensitivity associated with critical dynamics is no longer confined to a single finely tuned
207
parameter value and persists over an extended region of parameter space.
208
The mechanism underlying these changes in the curves of dynamic range as a function of λ as the dimensionality of
209
the system decreases (Fig. 7C) is attributed to the heterogeneous activations imposed by HMN. In low-dimensional
210
networks with broad distributions of connectivity (in-degree) and convergence index κ (Table 2), heterogeneity is
211
meaningful, and the activity spreads locally through structurally heterogeneous pathways, so different regions become
212
activated at different stimulus intensities (Figs. 3 and 6). As the external input increases, regions and modules are
213
recruited progressively rather than simultaneously, generating a broad distribution of local responses that enhances
214
the global dynamic range. In contrast, higher-dimensional networks allow activity to spread more uniformly across the
215
system (Figs. 3 and 6), leading to a more homogeneous activation pattern and a narrower range of distinguishable
216
inputs. Therefore, the locality of interactions and the dynamical heterogeneity characteristic of sparse HMNs naturally
217
give rise to enhanced stimulus discrimination without requiring fine-tuning to a single critical point.
218
2.6
Module-specific critical slowing down enhances dynamic range
219
Mirroring the heterogeneity observed in endogenous activity (Figs. 3 and 6), responses to external stimulation also
220
vary considerably across modules. Figure 8 quantifies this variability by displaying the dynamic range of individual
221
modules at each hierarchical level. Variability in dynamic range increases toward more internal levels (higher ℓ),
222
although its magnitude depends strongly on network architecture. In this case, the dynamic range varies from the
223
mean by at most 3% (< 1 dB) across modules in the homogeneous network, whereas this variation reaches up to 23%
224
(6 dB) in the heterogeneous network.
225
This architecture-dependent heterogeneity extends to the relationship between intrinsic timescales and stimulus
226
discrimination. A positive correlation between INT and dynamic range emerges exclusively in heterogeneous networks
227
(Fig. 9), suggesting that local criticality can induce module-specific critical slowing down. Modules operating near
228
their local critical points exhibit prolonged intrinsic timescales, which enhance their dynamic range when the global
229
network is tuned close to a transition [48]. These results indicate how heterogeneity in the modular activity can
230
diversify both temporal dynamics and functional responsiveness across modules.

3
Discussion
278
The architecture of the brain, characterized by modular and hierarchical organization alongside pronounced
279
heterogeneity across spatial scales, plays a crucial role in flexible information processing. Using HMNs, our study
280
demonstrates that heterogeneous dynamics can emerge from disordered connectivity within these networks,
281
fundamentally reshaping the interplay between network structure, critical-like dynamics, and stimulus discrimination.
282
The emergence of this dynamical heterogeneity is governed by the sparsity parameter ϕ: sparser HMNs (smaller ϕ)
283
exhibit stronger heterogeneous connectivity and dynamics, and it is precisely in this regime that the mean-field
284
approximation breaks down. This effect is further amplified in lower-dimensional networks, where GP occurs. Notably,
285
substantial heterogeneity persists even in the limit of infinite-dimensional networks, indicating that it is not merely a
286
finite-size or low-dimensional artifact.
287
Rather than producing a single critical point at which the entire network exhibits maximal sensitivity, dynamical
288
heterogeneity distributes distinct dynamical regimes across modules, generating broad critical-like dynamics,
289
heterogeneous INT, and substantial variability in stimulus responsiveness. This local heterogeneity confers a
290
functional advantage: modular dynamic range is enhanced in modules with longer INT, and, under localized
291
stimulation, in modules that do not receive direct input. Furthermore, the spatial scale of stimulation strongly shapes
292
network responsiveness, with stimulation at intermediate hierarchical levels yielding the largest global dynamic range.
293
These findings indicate that hierarchical-modular organization provides a structural substrate for functional
294
specialization, supporting flexible, spatially distributed information processing without requiring the network to be
295
precisely tuned to a single critical point.
296
3.1
Functional specialization: Heterogeneity distributes critical-like dynamics across
297
the network
298
A central result of this study is that dynamical heterogeneity transforms the conventional picture of critical dynamics
299
from a global phenomenon into a spatially distributed one, exhibiting functional specialization. In homogeneous or
300
effectively infinite-dimensional networks, critical slowing down and dynamic-range enhancement are concentrated
301
around a well-defined transition. In contrast, hierarchical-modular networks can exhibit broad regions of enhanced
302
fluctuations, long intrinsic timescales, and power-law activity over an extended range of coupling strengths. This
303
behavior is consistent with a Griffiths-like regime [12], in which structurally distinct regions can approach dynamical
304
16/24


transitions at different parameter values, allowing them to optimize performance at different tasks [61].
305
Our modular analysis provides a direct view of this mechanism. At a given global coupling strength, individual
306
modules can occupy markedly different dynamical states, with some remaining predominantly subcritical while others
307
already exhibit self-sustained activity. These differences are accompanied by broad distributions of intrinsic neural
308
timescales, particularly in the deepest modules. Thus, the broad critical-like regime observed at the network level is
309
not simply a broadened version of a homogeneous transition. Instead, it emerges from the coexistence of multiple
310
locally distinct dynamical regimes [60,61].
311
This observation is important for interpreting criticality in biological networks [67,68]. If different brain regions
312
have different effective coupling strengths, connectivity patterns, or dynamical states, then a single global measure
313
may necessarily average across regions that are not equally close to a transition [35,69,70]. The resulting global
314
signature of criticality can therefore reflect the collective contribution of multiple local dynamical regimes rather than
315
a single network-wide critical point. In this sense, heterogeneity provides a natural mechanism for reconciling
316
critical-like dynamics with the biological variability and structural disorder of real neural systems.
317
The relationship between local activity and intrinsic timescales further supports this interpretation. Modules with
318
higher endogenous activity exhibit longer intrinsic timescales, and this association is substantially stronger in
319
heterogeneous networks (in contrast to single neuron results [38]), showing that each spatial scale might have its own
320
governing rules [37,71,72]. Critical slowing down therefore becomes a local property that varies across the hierarchy.
321
This distributed organization may be particularly relevant to the hierarchy timescales observed across cortical
322
systems [30–32], where regions with different anatomical and functional roles exhibit distinct temporal dynamics, and
323
regions (or modules) closer to criticality would exhibit longer INT owing to critical slowing down [35,69].
324
3.2
From global criticality to recruitment in modular dynamic range
325
The conventional notion of dynamic range treats the network as a single computational unit [48–55,57–59]. Our
326
results suggest that this perspective can obscure an important consequence of hierarchical organization: different
327
modules can have substantially different capacities to encode external inputs, similarly to the idiosyncratic signatures
328
of dynamic range also observed across neurons [73,74]. We therefore introduced modular dynamic range as a local
329
measure of stimulus responsiveness. Under global stimulation, heterogeneous networks exhibit substantially broader
330
distributions of dynamic range than homogeneous networks, with variability becoming particularly pronounced at
331
deeper hierarchical levels.
332
This heterogeneity is not merely a variability in output. It is systematically related to the intrinsic dynamics of
333
individual modules. A positive association between intrinsic timescale and dynamic range emerges in heterogeneous
334
networks, linking prolonged temporal integration to enhanced stimulus discrimination. This relationship suggests that
335
local critical slowing down may have a direct computational consequence: modules that retain activity over longer
336
timescales can remain sensitive to a broader range of input intensities.
337
The global enhancement of dynamic range in lower-dimensional networks provides a complementary perspective.
338
As dimensionality decreases, activation becomes increasingly localized and heterogeneous, causing different regions to
339
be recruited at different stimulus intensities. Rather than the entire network responding simultaneously, activity
340
progressively propagates through structurally distinct regions, broadening the range over which changes in stimulus
341
intensity can be discriminated. This is a proof-of-concept for the recruitment theory [75], and potentially also
342
meaningful to explain recruitment differences and differences in heterogeneity of responses observes in autism
343
spectrum disorder compared to the neurotypical population [76]. This finding suggests a testable prediction: that
344
brain networks in autism spectrum disorder might exhibit lower effective dimensionality than in the neurotypical
345
population, a hypothesis that warrants future investigation.
346
This spatially heterogeneous recruitment mechanism provides an intuitive interpretation of the Griffiths-like
347
enhancement of dynamic range. In a homogeneous system, increasing the stimulus primarily changes the overall
348
magnitude of activity. In a heterogeneous hierarchical system, increasing the stimulus can additionally change which
349
modules become active and how strongly they contribute. The response curve therefore reflects a progressive
350
recruitment of multiple local dynamical regimes. Thus, heterogeneity effectively distributes the sensitivity of the
351
network across stimulus intensities.
352
3.3
Local stimulation reveals a distributed computational benefit
353
A distinctive implication of our results emerges when external stimulation is spatially localized. In homogeneous
354
networks, stimulating only a single module reduces global dynamic range relative to stimulating the entire network. In
355
heterogeneous networks, however, the opposite occurs: local stimulation can produce a larger global dynamic range
356
than global stimulation. This result indicates that heterogeneity can convert spatially restricted inputs into enhanced
357
network-level sensitivity.
358
This enhancement is not restricted to the directly stimulated module, and modules that receive no external input
359
can also exhibit enhanced modular dynamic range under localized stimulation. This finding suggests that the
360
computational consequence of local stimulation is distributed across the network rather than being confined to the site
361
17/24


of input. Such a property is particularly relevant for hierarchical neural systems, in which information entering
362
through a restricted subset of regions can influence distributed processing across multiple spatial scales, whilst
363
increasing network-level dynamic range.
364
This result provides a possible mechanistic interpretation of functional specialization as a results of diversity in
365
dynamics and function [61]. Anatomical modules need not be intrinsically dedicated to a single computational
366
function solely through differences in their local response properties. Instead, their position within a heterogeneous
367
HMN can determine how strongly they respond to particular spatial patterns of input. Structural organization can
368
therefore sculp functional specialization through differences in dynamical susceptibility.
369
3.4
Intermediate-scale stimulation maximizes responsiveness
370
A particularly striking result is the non-monotonic dependence of global dynamic range on the hierarchical level at
371
which stimulation is applied. Neither global stimulation nor stimulation of the smallest possible module maximizes
372
network responsiveness. Instead, the largest global dynamic range occurs when stimulation is applied at an
373
intermediate hierarchical level, approximately halfway through the hierarchy.
374
This finding suggests that optimal information transmission in HMNs require a balance between competing effects.
375
Stimulation that is too spatially broad reduces the opportunity for differential recruitment because much of the
376
network receives the input simultaneously. Conversely, stimulation that is too localized may fail to engage sufficiently
377
many pathways to generate a strong distributed response. Intermediate-level stimulation provides a compromise: it is
378
sufficiently localized to preserve spatial heterogeneity while encompassing enough of the network to engage multiple
379
downstream modules. The fact that an optimum stimulation profile emerges at an intermediate level in both
380
homogeneous and heterogeneous networks, while the magnitude and variability of the enhancement are substantially
381
larger in heterogeneous networks, is also informative. The hierarchical architecture itself establishes a preferred spatial
382
scale for information propagation, whereas heterogeneity amplifies the functional consequences of this organization.
383
This intermediate optimum is likely reminiscent of a matching between the spatial scale of an input and the
384
intrinsic organization of the network. Such a mechanism could provide a simple explanation for why different
385
hierarchical levels may be particularly effective for different classes of stimuli. Inputs acting at distinct spatial scales
386
could preferentially engage different portions of the hierarchy, thereby producing distinct and flexible global response
387
profiles without requiring structural changes in the underlying network architecture.
388
3.5
Implications for brain organization
389
Although the model is deliberately abstract, our results point to several principles that may be relevant to dynamics
390
in biological HMNs such as the brain: (i) heterogeneity need not be merely a source of noise that neural systems must
391
overcome; instead, structural heterogeneity can itself generate a repertoire of dynamical states supporting flexible
392
computation; (ii) hierarchical organization can convert this heterogeneity into an ordered hierarchy of temporal and
393
response properties; and (iii) localized inputs can exploit this organization to produce distributed responses whose
394
sensitivity depends on both the spatial scale and the hierarchical position of the input.
395
These principles provide a potential mechanistic bridge between anatomical hierarchy, intrinsic timescales, and
396
functional specialization. Brain regions with different hierarchical positions are known to exhibit different temporal
397
dynamics [30–32,34–36,39,69,77], while sensory inputs are typically neither perfectly global nor restricted to single
398
neurons. Our results suggest that these two features (hierarchical timescales and spatially structured stimulation) can
399
interact through heterogeneous network dynamics to improve stimulus sensitivity.
400
More broadly, the results support a view of criticality in which the computational benefits traditionally associated
401
with a finely tuned critical point can emerge from an extended and spatially distributed regime. Rather than requiring
402
the entire system to operate at a unique optimal coupling, different modules can occupy different portions of a broad
403
critical-like landscape. This organization provides robustness to parameter variations while simultaneously increasing
404
functional diversity. In this sense, disorder and heterogeneity may provide not only tolerance to imperfect tuning but
405
also an additional computational degree of freedom.
406
3.6
Limitations and future directions
407
While the present study offers a controlled framework for isolating how structural heterogeneity and hierarchical
408
organization shape local dynamics and dynamic range, several extensions would help establish the generality of these
409
findings. Future work should validate these principles in biologically detailed spiking models, which may require
410
incorporating inhibition, adaptation, and synaptic kinetics, and should extend the analysis to heterogeneous weighted
411
synapses, spatially embedded networks, and realistic connectomes (as illustrated in Fig. 1).
412
3.7
Conclusion
413
Our findings indicate that heterogeneity in hierarchical-modular networks has implications extending well beyond
414
simply broadening critical-like dynamics. It produces a spatially distributed array of dynamical states, establishes a
415
18/24


gradient of intrinsic timescales, and enhances stimulus responsiveness at both global and modular levels. Notably,
416
localized stimulation can enhance the global dynamic range in heterogeneous networks, with peak responsiveness
417
occurring when stimulation is applied at an intermediate hierarchical level. These effects arise from the progressive and
418
heterogeneous recruitment of network regions, thereby linking hierarchical-modular organization to stimulus-dependent
419
computation, in which functional specialization emerges naturally. This suggests that the brain’s dynamical
420
heterogeneity not only aligns with efficient information processing but actively supports robustness and computational
421
advantage. By distributing critical slowing down and stimulus sensitivity across modules, hierarchical organization can
422
provide multiple dynamical regimes within a single network, allowing different spatial scales of input to engage distinct
423
computational pathways. Consequently, rather than requiring precise tuning to a single critical point, complex brain
424
networks may leverage heterogeneity itself to achieve robust, flexible, and functionally specialized computation.

Methods
426
4.1
Construction of hierarchical-modular networks
427
HMNs can be described uniquely by a N × N adjacency matrix A, where the row and column indexes represent the
428
N neurons, with a nonzero entry for every connection. The coupling strength between neurons is then encoded by a
429
fixed parameter, p, for the whole network (see 4.4 for details), giving rise to the connectivity matrix C = pA. The
430
matrix C, then, not only encodes topology, but also the dynamics of the network.
431
In order to simulate the hierarchical functionality and structural capabilities of the brain connectome, we use the
432
model described initially by Moretti and Mu˜noz [12] and Geza ´Odor, et. al [20]. In it, the network is described by its
433
adjacency matrix A, presenting m modules (the innermost each with M0 nodes) and h hierarchies. Connections
434
depend only on the hierarchical level ℓ∈[0, h), such that each module is represented by a random network such that
435
the connection density within is given by ϕℓ= αϕh−ℓ, where α and ϕ are control variables. Higher values of ℓ
436
represent smaller probabilities (since ϕ < 1), implying narrower regions (in other words, the structure is coarser).
437
The parameter ϕ controls network sparsity: lower values produce sparser networks, while higher values yield denser
438
connectivity. A key measure of this connectivity is the network’s dimension, D, as defined by Daqing et al. [63]. This
439
dimension is determined by an iterative process similar to Dijkstra’s algorithm [78]: A random neuron is chosen as the
440
seed, and its neighbors are counted. The process then proceeds outward, counting the neighbors of those neighbors,
441
while ensuring that no neuron is counted more than once at each step. This continues for r steps, yielding the total
442
number of unique nodes reached, Nr. The network dimension D is then defined by the power-law relationship
443
Nr ∝rD (valid for finite dimension networks), loosely interpreted as the dimension of the fractal-like hypersphere of
444
dimension D, as the space occupied by the nodes scales accordingly [62]. Square and cube lattices serve as key
445
references for D = 2 and D = 3, respectively. By contrast, infinite-dimensional networks exhibit much faster growth
446
than the power law, whereas for D →0, Nr rapidly saturates at a threshold beyond which no new neurons can be
447
reached. Fig. 2 presents a comparison of these cases.
448
Only when ϕ is in the vicinity of 2−m, this model can generate networks with a finite topological dimension, when
449
the heterogeneous nature of the network is heightened. In this study, whenever we compare these infinite-dimensional
450
models (that is, when ϕ > 2−m) with their finite-dimensional counterparts, we ensure a fair comparison by using the
451
same underlying structure (the same M0, m, and h values) but different values for ϕ. Additionally, the parameter α
452
can be any positive real number, and its role is to determine the specific value of the network’s dimension while
453
keeping it finite. It is informative to picture these five network parameters in two categories (Fig. 2): the geometric
454
parameters (M0, m, and h), which influence the network’s modularity, and the connection parameters (ϕ and α),
455
which govern how the nodes connect.
456
Notably, HMNs are unique to this type of study by virtue of their very nature: modularity creates islands of
457
stability through topology, while the hierarchies ensure that these regions remain intertwined, implying distinct levels
458
of connectedness among them. The relationship between the modules and the hierarchies can be controlled via the
459
parameters ϕ and α, making them key to navigating through degrees of heterogeneity in the network.
460
4.2
Empirical connectomes
461
The empirical connectomes were binary networks of human, mouse, and C. elegans, identical to those used in [47],
462
with the C. elegans connectome obtained from [79] and the mouse connectome obtained from [28,80]. These networks
463
vary substantially in scale: a node in the human connectome represents a large brain region, whereas a node in the C.
464
elegans connectome represents an individual neuron. Despite these differences in scale and resolution, modular
465
organization and hierarchical core-periphery structure are recurring organizational principles across species [25,28,81].
466
4.3
Graph-theoretic properties
467
Graph-theoretic measures were computed for all directed binary networks using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox [82] in
468
MATLAB. Network density was defined as the ratio of the number of edges present in the network to the total number
469
19/24


of possible edges, i.e., the fraction of realized connections out of all pairwise connections that could exist. In-degree
470
was defined, for each node, as the number of incoming edges to that node (i.e., the number of other nodes with a
471
direct connection projecting onto it). Network efficiency was quantified by global efficiency [47,82], network clustering
472
by the mean clustering coefficient across all nodes [82,83], and modularity by the Q-index from a generalized Louvain
473
community detection algorithm [82]. The Q-index quantifies the extent to which a network is modular, with positive
474
values indicating greater modular organization than expected by chance. Finally, the convergence index κ was defined,
475
for each node, as the product of its in-degree and p, the probability of activity propagating along a connection to a
476
neighboring node; this measure was used to characterize the extent to which heterogeneity in in-degree across nodes
477
contributes to heterogeneity in mean activation (firing rate) across the network.
478
4.4
Temporal dynamics
479
For the simulation of neuronal behavior, we use the Kinouchi-Copelli model [48], where each neuron can possess one of
480
2 states, i.e., si = {0, 1}, cyclically traversed in a synchronous update, as in every cellular automaton. The state si = 0
481
implies rest, si = 1 is the excited state. A neuron can assume the value si = 1 in two ways: due to an external
482
stimulus, modeled as a Poisson process with rate r (that is, a probability η(r) = 1 −e−r); or with probability p (which
483
can be interpreted as a coupling strength between neurons), due to an excited neighbor at the previous step. After
484
si = 1, the automaton returns to state si = 0 at the next time step.
485
Defining ρt as the density of active sites, i.e., the fraction of sites i with input si = 1, we can obtain the average
486
activity F of the network: F = 1
T
PT
t=0 ρt. A common feature of the dynamics is the presence of a transient state
487
before reaching some better-defined behavior in F; this transient is excluded from the analysis in all results shown
488
here. When a network is stimulated via a Poisson process, the curve of F as a function of the stimulus rate r is a
489
response function, and it presents a region of optimized information output, defined in this context by Kinouchi &
490
Copelli [48] as the dynamic range of the network. This region corresponds to the range of external stimuli for which
491
the resulting neuronal activity is distinguishable, excluding stimuli that are too low, where small changes fail to
492

4.5
External stimulus and dynamic range
507
For the estimated response curves, 80 logarithmically spaced values of r were generated, spanning the interval
508
r ∈[r0 = 10−7, rf = 102]. Each system iteration was run for a total time T = 104ms, with an initial transient period
509
of ˜t = 100ms discarded prior to the computation of the mean value of F. To ensure statistical accuracy, 60 samples
510
were collected for each value of r, and the average of F was computed from these samples.
511
This procedure yields a sigmoidal curve of F (on a linear scale) as a function of r (on a logarithmic scale), allowing
512
the estimation of F0 ≡F(r →0) using a nonlinear fit, which explicitly accounts for the smoothing of F as r →0. In
513
this case, F is locally approximated by the functional form F ≈a · rb + c, and a nonlinear fit is performed over the
514
interval r ∈[r0, 10 · r0], yielding the coefficients a, b, and c, with F0 = c.
515
The nonlinear fitting approach proved to be the most faithful, as it accounts for both the fluctuations in F and its
516
power-law decay trend as r →0, particularly near the trivial critical point associated with second-order phase
517
transitions. Given both Fmax and F0, we obtain Fx = F0 + x(Fmax −F0). The values of r0.9 and r0.1 are then
518
obtained by interpolating the data points (F, r) that bound (F0.9, r0.9) and (F0.1, r0.1), respectively.

4.6
Modular coordinates
520
Due to the network’s hierarchy, modules inherently contain sub-modules, forming hierarchical layers. This means that,
521
using Fig. 2B as an example, the set of connections in X belongs to an outer module, which in turn belongs to
522
another one, and so on. Specifically, these outer regions are represented with a trailing sequence of zeros in the
523
modular coordinate set, as indicated by the modules Y and Z on Fig. 2B,C. This method thus provides a way to
524
sample specific modules independently.

As an example, we can use the network of m = 3, h = 4, seen on Fig. 2B,C. The n-tuple [1, 1, 1] is composed of 3

numbers, meaning that this module lies at ℓ= 3, the deepest one. This module is contained within [1, 1, 0], that is in

528
