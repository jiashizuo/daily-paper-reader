Abstract
Background: Accurate partial volume (PV) estimation, i.e., subvoxel tissue fractions,
from Magnetic Resonance Fingerprinting (MRF) remains challenging, as conventional meth-
ods such as dictionary matching or segmentation tools like FSL FAST rely on multi-stage
iterative pipelines, where inaccuracies in early steps (e.g., parametric map estimation) can
propagate and compromise the final tissue fraction maps.
Purpose: This study introduces DeepPV-MRF, a deep learning framework designed to
directly map raw MRF signals to voxel-wise tissue fraction maps. The goal is to provide
a more accurate and efficient alternative to existing iterative and dictionary-based PV
estimation methods.
Methods: DeepPV-MRF employs a cascaded spatial-temporal neural network trained
on subject-derived simulations generated through Bloch modeling with radial k-space un-
dersampling. The integrates voxel-wise temporal encoding with spatial U-Net refinement
to estimate CSF, GM, and WM fractions without intermediate T1/T2 maps. Performance
was evaluated against PV dictionary matching (PV-DM), and PV pseudoinverse (PV-Pinv),
using both simulated and in vivo data.

reconstructions and improving performance particularly in low-shot scenarios, the method
addresses critical weaknesses of existing approaches. These results support the practical
feasibility of DeepPV-MRF and motivate further validation before clinical deployment.

Introduction
Quantitative MRI (qMRI) is a non-invasive imaging framework that enables numerical charac-
terization of tissue microstructure1. Broadly, qMRI applications fall into two major categories:
parametric mapping and tissue composition estimation. The former focuses on extracting intrin-
sic tissue properties such as longitudinal relaxation time (T1) and transverse relaxation time
(T2), which serve as biophysical biomarkers for disease diagnosis. The latter aims to estimate
metrics such as volume or thickness of tissue such as gray matter (GM), white matter (WM),
and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the brain.
While parametric maps provide valuable insights into tissue biophysics, they do not directly
account for tissue mixing within a voxel2. Conventionally, tissue composition estimation assumes
that each voxel contains a single homogeneous tissue type3. However, this assumption fails in
regions where voxels span across anatomical boundaries. This limitation becomes particularly
important in clinical brain imaging, where partial volume (PV) effects are prevalent due to the
limited spatial resolution of MRF4,5,6. PV effects can substantially impair the accuracy of tissue
quantification2, lesion size estimations7, and brain atrophy assessment8.
To characterize sub-voxel tissue heterogeneity, voxel-wise tissue fraction estimation—i.e.,
quantifying the relative proportions of GM, WM, and CSF in each voxel—is essential. Traditional
approaches to PV estimation typically rely on regularized statistical models, such as Gaussian
mixture models (GMMs) combined with expectation-maximization (EM)9,10,11,12, often applied
to multi-contrast structural MRI. These methods assume that each tissue class exhibits a well-
defined intensity distribution across modalities and that voxel intensities result from a linear
mixture of pure tissue responses. While effective in structural imaging, these assumptions do
not hold in quantitative techniques like Magnetic Resonance Fingerprinting (MRF), where voxel
signals represent non-linear temporal evolutions rather than static intensities.
MRF13 signal representation captures multi-parametric information in a single scan, making it
an attractive candidate for direct tissue composition estimation. One early attempt to incorporate
PV modeling into MRF is the partial volume dictionary matching (PV-DM) method3,13, which
estimates tissue fractions through dictionary matching. While promising, this method inherits
several limitations from the underlying dictionary-based paradigm. First, dictionary-based
MRF reconstruction becomes computationally demanding as the size and resolution of the
dictionary increase, especially for applications involving partial volume modeling where dense
sampling of tissue parameter space may be necessary13,14,15,16. Second, PV-DM relies on a strong
“pure-tissue” assumption, requiring manually defined reference clusters with fixed relaxation
times for GM, WM, and CSF. This introduces subjectivity and operational complexity, making
it difficult to deploy in automated or clinical pipelines. These challenges motivate the need
for more flexible, data-driven approaches to sub-voxel tissue fraction estimation in the MRF
context.
Recent studies have explored deep learning as an alternative to dictionary-based MRF
reconstruction, demonstrating improved efficiency and scalability17,18,19,20,21,22,23,24.
These
methods typically train convolutional or transformer-based models to map MRF time-series
signals to voxel-wise tissue parameters such as T1 and T2. For example, DRONE17 first introduced
a fully connected neural network to directly regress quantitative maps from signal evolutions.
SCQ18 extended this approach using convolutional architectures. More recent transformer-based
models21 have shown further improvements in spatial consistency and generalization. However,
most existing models are designed with a single-compartment model and do not explicitly
estimate tissue fractions in partial volume voxels. While these models have demonstrated strong
performance in parametric map reconstruction, they are not designed to resolve sub-voxel tissue
mixing. As a result, partial volume effects remain unaddressed in most existing MRF-based deep
learning pipelines, limiting their utility in tasks that require accurate tissue composition analysis.
2


This limitation motivates the need for models capable of explicitly estimating sub-voxel tissue
fractions from MRF signals.
To this end, we present DeepPV-MRF, an end-to-end deep learning framework for directly
estimating voxel-wise tissue fraction maps from aliased MRF signal evolutions. The goal is
to estimate the relative fractions of CSF, GM, and WM within each voxel, without relying
on intermediate T1 or T2 maps. Extending the methodology proposed in MRF-Mixer24, we
construct a fully simulated training dataset in which each voxel is assigned reference tissue
fractions derived from high-resolution anatomical segmentations and corresponding pure-tissue
relaxometry estimated from in vivo acquisitions. Unlike conventional partial volume estimation
techniques such as partial volume pseudoinverse (PV-Pinv) and PV-DM3, which depend on
predefined pure tissue fingerprints and manually designed subdictionaries, our method learns
the mapping from MRF signals to tissue fractions directly from data, removing the need for
dictionary construction, signal matching, or parameter discretization.

Related Work
2.1
Magnetic Resonance Fingerprinting Parameter Mapping
MRF represents an approach to MRI data acquisition and analysis that enables simultaneous
quantification of multiple tissue parameters through a single abbreviated scan13. During the
dictionary matching (DM) process, the signal evolution observed in each voxel i, termed the
tissue fingerprint, is represented as si ∈C1×t, comprising t time points. This fingerprint is
systematically compared against a precomputed dictionary DMRF ∈Ct×n, where each column
dj ∈Ct represents a signal evolution simulated from a unique combination of tissue parameters
{T1, T2}. The best-matching dictionary entry di,MRF match is identified as the one that maximizes
the normalized dot product magnitude with the observed signal3. Mathematically, this matching
procedure can be expressed as:
di,MRF match = arg max
j=1,...,n |⟨si, dj⟩| .

The tissue parameters corresponding to the selected dictionary entry are then assigned to voxel
i. To accelerate the DM process, many approaches have been proposed14,15,25,26.Recently, deep
learning methodologies have been proposed to bypass the DM process altogether17,18,19,21,23,24.
However, these deep learning approaches typically do not address the partial volume estimation.

2.3
Integrating Partial Volume Estimation with MRF
Within the MRF framework, PV estimation has been approached by modeling each voxel’s
signal as a linear combination of pure tissue fingerprints. Let si denote the MRF signal at voxel
i, and let Dsub ∈Ct×m represent a subdictionary of signal evolutions for m tissue types. Then,
the signal can be modeled as:
si = Dsubwi,
(3)
where wi is the vector of tissue volume fractions. Based on this formulation, two representative
approaches have been developed. The first, partial volume pseudoinverse (PV-Pinv)3, uses a
pseudoinverse projection to estimate wi. The second, PV-DM3, constructs a dictionary of signal
evolutions corresponding to all possible tissue fraction combinations and applies conventional
dictionary matching to recover wi.
While both methods provide a mathematically grounded approach to PV estimation in MRF,
they depend heavily on predefined pure-tissue relaxation parameters and manual clustering for
ROI selection. These assumptions complicate robust application across large datasets containing
many subjects scanned on a variety of systems.

Methodology
As illustrated in Figure 1, DeepPV-MRF consists of two main modules: (1) a voxel-wise complex-
valued multilayer perceptron (cMLP) that compresses the temporal fingerprint at each voxel into
a low-dimensional latent representation, and (2) a spatial refinement module based on a U-Net
architecture that predicts tissue fraction maps. To train the network, we generated a synthetic
dataset using forward Bloch simulations based on subject-derived PV maps and estimated
pure-tissue relaxation parameters. The dataset incorporates radial-arm k-space undersampling
and complex-valued signal formation to closely mimic real-world MRF acquisitions. We validated
the proposed method using both simulated and in vivo test data.
The remainder of this section is organized as follows: Section 3.1 details the network structure,
Section 3.2 describes the synthetic data generation pipeline, Section 3.3 describes the training
data, Section 3.4 outlines the synthetic testing data and in vivo data acquisition process,
Section 3.5 covers the network training process, Section 3.6 presents the evaluation metrics, and
Section 3.7 describes the baseline comparison methods.
3.1
Network Architecture
The overall structure of DeepPV-MRF follows a two-stage design derived from the MRF-Mixer
framework24,28. The original MRF-Mixer was developed to jointly extract spatial and temporal
features from aliased MRF data. In this study, we adapted and extended that architecture to
explicitly target voxel-wise partial volume estimation. Figure 1 provides an overview of the
network, including the complex-valued temporal encoder and spatial tissue-fraction decoder.
The network consisted of two sequential modules: a voxel-wise temporal encoder and a spatial
refinement module. The encoder was implemented as a complex-valued multilayer perceptron
(cMLP) that independently processes each voxel’s 200-point MRF fingerprint obtained after
temporal compression via singular value decomposition (SVD). It comprised four complex-valued
Conv2D layers with kernel size set to 1 × 1, effectively functioning as point-wise MLPs that
reduce the temporal dimension to 16 latent channels. The real and imaginary parts of the
resulting features were then concatenated to form a 32-channel per-voxel embedding, which was
passed to the spatial decoder.

The refinement module adopted a modified U-Net to incorporate spatial context and regress
tissue fraction maps. The encoder comprised four downsampling blocks with increasing channel
widths [32, 64, 128, 256], each consisting of a 3 × 3 convolution, batch normalization, ReLU

convolutions for upsampling and skip connections for spatial resolution recovery. A SoftMax
activation was applied to the tissue fraction decoder output to enforce probabilistic constraints
across CSF, GM, and WM fractions.
For completeness, we also evaluated an auxiliary-head variant that added B0 and PD
prediction branches during training. These branches were introduced as an additional supervisory
signal to regularize the shared encoder, but, as shown in the ablation study, they did not improve
the primary PV estimation task under the current setting. Therefore, all main simulation and
in vivo results use the no-auxiliary-head model. Overall, DeepPV-MRF enabled direct inference
of PV maps from aliased MRF signals, without relying on intermediate parameter maps or
dictionary matching.

3.3
Synthetic Training Dataset
We applied the above procedure to ten subjects from the ME-MP2RAGE dataset30 for training.
All imaging procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed
consent was obtained.
To reduce memory usage and accelerate training, we applied temporal compression using
SVD14, reducing the original 1000 time points to 200 dominant components. This choice was
guided by prior MRF studies showing that, for bSSFP-based dictionaries, the first 200 singular
components retain nearly all dominant signal energy14. Accordingly, we used 200 components
as a robust and computationally efficient setting for evaluating the core feasibility of deep
learning-based partial volume estimation from undersampled MRF signals, rather than treating
SVD dimensionality itself as a primary optimization target in this study.
The SVD basis was computed once from the precomputed MRF dictionary rather than from
the training or testing image data. The same fixed temporal basis was then applied to the
training, synthetic testing, and in vivo datasets. Therefore, the temporal compression step was
7


independent of the image-level training/testing split and did not introduce information leakage
from the test subjects into model training.
The final training dataset contained 1200 paired samples of size 192 × 192, each consisting
of compressed aliased MRF signals as input and the corresponding CSF, WM, and GM tissue
fraction maps as labels. To enhance spatial diversity and improve generalization, we applied
random cropping during training, as illustrated in Figure 2(d).
Specifically, each original
sample was randomly cropped five times, resulting in a total of 30,000 training samples of size
64 × 64 × 200.
3.4
Testing Dataset
To evaluate model performance under controlled conditions, we first constructed a synthetic
test set using three subjects from the ME-MP2RAGE dataset30, which were not used during
training. For each subject, we selected slices primarily located in the central brain region to
ensure anatomical diversity. A total of 120 synthetic test samples were generated, each of
size 192 × 192. These samples were used exclusively during inference to provide quantitative
comparisons of reconstruction accuracy across different models.
In addition to the synthetic test data, in vivo datasets were acquired from three healthy
volunteers (sub 01, sub 02, sub 03). The sub 01 dataset was acquired on a 3 T Siemens Prisma
scanner, whereas the sub 02 and sub 03 datasets were acquired after the scanner system was
upgraded to a 3 T Siemens MAGNETOM Cima.X Fit platform. Each volunteer was scanned
using single-shot, three-shot, and six-shot MRF acquisitions, resulting in nine in vivo datasets
in total. Imaging was performed across 32 slices using a 1000-point IR-bSSFP sequence with
varying radial undersampling strategies, an in-plane resolution of 1 mm, and a slice thickness of
3 mm. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers prior to participation. The
trained models were directly applied to these datasets without retraining or fine-tuning, and
the results were used to evaluate cross-shot consistency and multi-volunteer in vivo robustness
across the expanded scanner/protocol setting.
3.5
Network Training
The DeepPV-MRF network was trained using the Adam optimizer on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX
5090 GPU for 100 epochs. All model parameters were initialized from a normal distribution with
a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 0.01, and the full training process required approximately
one hour under the current implementation settings. After training, computational profiling
of the final model showed that it contained 7.14 million trainable parameters and required
approximately 201.81 GFLOPs for one 192×192 slice. On the same GPU, the inference time was
3.06 ms per slice, with peak GPU memory usage of 0.243 GB allocated and 0.303 GB reserved.

3.6
Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate estimation performance, we employed voxel-wise metrics to reflect numerical accuracy.
These quantitative voxel-wise metrics were computed for the synthetic test data, where reference
tissue fraction maps were available. For the in vivo data, no voxel-wise reference PV maps
were available; therefore, metrics requiring reference tissue fractions were not computed in that
setting. Instead, an expert manually delineated the brain contour in ImageJ36 to generate a
brain mask, which was used to restrict the analysis and visualization to the brain region.

3.7
Comparison with Other Methods
To comprehensively assess the performance of the proposed method, we conducted comparative
analyses against several established approaches, including PV-Pinv3, and PV-DM3. Because
these competing methods operate on different signal representations and require different prior
information, we explicitly distinguish below between matched and unmatched comparison
conditions.
In the simulation experiments, DeepPV-MRF, PV-Pinv, and PV-DM were evaluated using
the same synthetic MRF inputs generated from the same underlying tissue composition and
relaxation parameter framework. For PV-Pinv and PV-DM, the required dictionaries Dsub and
DPV were constructed from the same reference tissue parameters used in the simulation setup.
Therefore, the simulation comparison among these MRF-based methods was performed under
closely matched input conditions and parameter assumptions.
For the in vivo experiment, some differences in preconditions were unavoidable because the
methods operate in different input domains. Following the original PV-DM study3, we first
generated T1 and T2 maps through dictionary matching and then applied k-means clustering
with k = 11 to select representative T1 and T2 values for constructing Dsub and DPV for
PV-Pinv and PV-DM. The value k = 11 was adopted from the original PV-DM implementation
rather than tuned in this study. The resulting cluster centers were used as representative
T1/T2 parameter candidates spanning the observed tissue distribution, not as eleven final tissue
classes. Candidate centers corresponding to CSF, GM, and WM were then used to construct
the three-component PV dictionary for tissue fraction estimation. In contrast, DeepPV-MRF
directly used aliased MRF time-series signals as input.
Accordingly, the comparison with PV-Pinv and PV-DM can be regarded as an MRF-based
methodological comparison under closely aligned simulation settings. We have made these
distinctions explicit to clarify both the fairness and the limitations of the comparative evaluation.

Figure 3 shows a qualitative comparison of partial volume estimation results from PV-DM,

single-shot case, where PV-DM and PV-Pinv showed clear structural errors, as was confirmed

DeepPV-MRF outperformed both PV-DM and PV-Pinv on all metrics. For example, under the
single-shot setting, DeepPV-MRF achieved lower MAE values (41.76×10−3 for CSF, 74.39×10−3
for WM, and 75.69×10−3 for GM) and higher SSIM scores (0.960, 0.916, and 0.922 respectively).
Figure 4 shows scatter plots of predicted PV values of DeepPV-MRF against the reference labels.
As the number of shots increases from 1 to 6, the data points become more concentrated around
the diagonal line, indicating better agreement with the reference labels. Correlation values
and trend line slopes also increased accordingly. For the 6-shot case, DeepPV-MRF achieved
MAE values of 22.93 × 10−3 (CSF), 43.16 × 10−3 (WM), and 43.96 × 10−3 (GM), with PSNR
values reaching 35.12 dB (CSF), 29.35 dB (WM), and 28.22 dB (GM). These results confirm
that DeepPV-MRF provides more accurate and stable partial volume estimation under different

4.3
Ablation Study
To quantify the contribution of the main architectural and loss design choices, we conducted
ablation experiments on the one-shot external evaluation set, including removal of the spatial
refinement module, addition of auxiliary B0/PD heads to the final model, removal of the spatial
gradient loss, variation of the latent dimension, and a sweep of the spatial regularization weight
λspatial. The results are summarized in Table 3.
Among the core ablations, removing the spatial refinement module led to the largest per-
formance drop, confirming that spatial context is essential for accurate PV estimation. The
temporal encoder alone increased the macro tissue-wise MAE from 63.95 to 89.54 and reduced
average PSNR/SSIM from 26.48/0.933 to 23.95/0.890, indicating that the main performance
gain arises from the joint temporal-spatial design rather than from voxel-wise temporal encoding
alone.

5
Discussion
This study presents DeepPV-MRF, a deep learning framework for direct partial volume estimation
from undersampled MRF signals. Unlike conventional methods such as PV-DM, PV-Pinv, and
FSL FAST, the proposed approach avoids strong reliance on hand-crafted priors and multi-step
intermediate processing. By directly mapping aliased MRF time-series signals to tissue fraction
maps, DeepPV-MRF simplifies the workflow and reduces the risk of error propagation from
intermediate reconstructions or model assumptions.
The performance gains of DeepPV-MRF appear to arise from two main factors. First, the
simulation-based training pipeline combines subject-derived tissue property maps and volumetric
fraction maps with realistic radial undersampling, allowing the network to learn tissue-specific
signal behavior under practical aliasing conditions. Second, the network architecture combines
voxel-wise temporal encoding with spatial refinement, which is particularly important in regions
with complex tissue mixing. The ablation study supports this interpretation: removing the
16


Figure 8: in vivo comparison of T1 and T2 maps from MRF-Mixer (left) and composite maps derived
from DeepPV-MRF (middle), with their absolute difference shown on the right. The composite maps
combine estimated tissue fractions with pure-tissue relaxation values. The difference maps confirm that
the largest discrepancies are concentrated at tissue boundaries, consistent with partial volume effects.
spatial refinement module caused the largest performance drop, whereas adding auxiliary B0/PD
heads and removing the spatial gradient loss had smaller effects on the final PV estimation
accuracy.
The simulation results demonstrated clear advantages of DeepPV-MRF over PV-DM and
PV-Pinv across all shot settings, with particularly strong performance under the more challenging
single-shot condition. The in vivo results further supported these findings, as DeepPV-MRF
produced tissue fraction maps with smoother transitions and better anatomical continuity,
especially in GM and WM regions.
The multi-volunteer in vivo cross-shot consistency analysis provided additional support for
this interpretation. Because voxel-wise in vivo tissue-fraction ground truth was unavailable, the
six-shot prediction was used only as an internal high-shot reference rather than as an accuracy
standard. Across all nine subject-tissue pairs, the three-shot estimates showed lower MAE
relative to the six-shot reference than the single-shot estimates, with an average MAE reduction
of 15.3%. This trend indicates that increasing the number of shots improved agreement with the
high-shot reference across subjects and tissues, while preserving the visually observed anatomical
structures in the estimated fraction maps.
The comparison between composite multi-component T1/T2 maps and single-component
MRF-Mixer estimates further highlights the value of explicit partial volume modeling. As shown
in Figure 8, the largest discrepancies were observed near tissue interfaces, where partial volume
effects are expected to be strongest. This suggests that mixed-tissue MRF voxels are not always
well represented by single-component relaxation estimates, and that explicit PV estimation may
support future frameworks for joint estimation of tissue fractions and intrinsic tissue parameters.
From a practical perspective, the inference benchmark reported in the Network Training
section indicates that DeepPV-MRF can generate tissue-fraction maps with low per-slice latency
and modest GPU memory usage. In addition, the three-volunteer in vivo noise perturbation
analysis showed monotonically decreasing prediction deviation as SNR increased, providing
preliminary evidence of robustness to moderate noise variation.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the in vivo evaluation included three
healthy volunteers acquired from two different scanner platforms (one from 3T Prisma and
two from 3T Cima.x Fit), the validation does not yet establish full generalizability across
sites, vendors, acquisition protocols, or pathological populations. In addition, the supervisory
tissue fraction maps were derived from FSL FAST on high-resolution anatomical images, so
they should be regarded as surrogate references rather than absolute ground truth, and some
FAST-related bias may therefore be inherited by the model. The synthetic signal model also
assumes linear combinations of pure-tissue fingerprints3, which may not fully capture effects
such as magnetization transfer, exchange, or other multi-compartment nonlinearities in real
tissues. Future work should therefore include broader validation across scanners, protocols, B1
17


conditions, motion levels, and pathological tissues, together with more independent reference
standards such as phantoms or expert-curated annotations.
6
Conclusion
In summary, we proposed a deep learning-based method for partial volume estimation that
directly maps MRF signals to tissue fraction maps in an end-to-end approach. By integrating
subject-specific simulation data with radial arm undersampling and employing a spatial-temporal
cascaded network architecture, the method achieves improved accuracy and robustness compared
to traditional approaches such as PV-DM and PV-Pinv. These advantages simplify the processing
pipeline and improve its suitability for practical applications.
Future work will focus on
validating the method across more diverse anatomical regions and clinical conditions, exploring
self-supervised or unsupervised training strategies, and extending the approach to additional
acquisition sequences.
