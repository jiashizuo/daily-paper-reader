I. INTRODUCTION
Euclidean geometry has long been the dominant represen-
tation space in machine learning [1]–[3], due to its strong cor-
respondence with human spatial intuition and well-established
mathematical foundations. In particular, graph representation
learning has predominantly relied on Euclidean embeddings
[4], [5]. However, an increasing body of evidence suggests
that Euclidean geometry may not align well with the latent
structures of many real-world datasets.
This mismatch is particularly evident in hierarchical or tree-
like data, which arise in a wide range of domains, including
concept taxonomies [6], [7], fine-grained image [8], RNA-seq
data [9], protein interaction data [10] and biological classi-
fication systems [11]. Such data typically exhibit exponential
branching patterns. In contrast, the volume of Euclidean space
grows only polynomially, leading to a fundamental geometric

mismatch. As a result, Euclidean embeddings are prone to the
so-called crowding phenomenon [12], where nodes at deeper
layers become increasingly close to each other.
Hyperbolic geometry provides a natural alternative. Due
to its exponential volume growth, hyperbolic space matches
the expansion of hierarchical structures and admits low-
distortion embeddings of tree-like data [7]. In addition, prior
studies [13]–[15] have suggested that hyperbolic space can
also serve as a natural latent space for graphs with scale-
free (power-law) structures and for heterogeneous information
networks that exhibit similar power-law properties. This obser-
vation has motivated a large body of work on deep hyperbolic
representation learning across computer vision [16]–[18], nat-
ural language processing [7], [19], and multi-modal learning
[20]. Beyond representation learning, hyperbolic geometry
has also been leveraged for graph generation. Most recently,
Bu et al. [21] proposed GGBall, a hyperbolic generative
framework operating in the Poincaré ball, which combines
a hyperbolic autoencoder with Riemannian flow to generate
hierarchical graphs in a one-shot manner. Their empirical
results demonstrate that hyperbolic latent spaces significantly
improve generation quality on tree-like and molecular graphs,
further confirming the practical advantages of hyperbolic
geometry. Despite these empirical successes, the mecha-
nisms underlying the advantages of hyperbolic geometry
and the intrinsic limitations of Euclidean geometry in
modeling hierarchical data remain underexplored. Existing
explanations are largely based on global distortion [22], [23]
or qualitative geometric intuition [7], and do not provide a
quantitative characterization of how Euclidean crowding arises
or how it affects representation learning.
In this work, we provide a geometric analysis of hierar-
chical graph embeddings in Euclidean spaces. Rather than
analyzing global metric distortion, we study the layer-wise
minimum separation between node embeddings, providing a
complementary perspective to the distortion-based theories of
hyperbolic embeddings. Our analysis shows that, under fixed
embedding dimensions, Euclidean representations of tree-like
graphs inevitably exhibit layer-wise crowding, where the sep-
aration between nodes within the same layer shrinks as depth
increases. Furthermore, we show that Euclidean hierarchical
embeddings induce an intrinsic geometric tension between
preserving parent–child distances and maintaining separation
between non-adjacent nodes. Enforcing separation between
non-adjacent nodes results in increased radial distances and
stretching parent–child edges, whereas preserving tree-path
distances inevitably compresses the separation between non-
adjacent nodes. This phenomenon gives rise to a fundamental

fully described by a single manifold such as hyperbolic space

with weaker hyperbolicity or less tree-like structures, where
imposing a fixed hyperbolic space may lead to performance
degradation. To address this issue, CurvGAD [25] proposes
a mixed-curvature graph autoencoder that explicitly models
geometric irregularities through curvature-aware encoding. In
particular, it incorporates Ollivier–Ricci curvature, along with
curvature reconstruction and Ricci-flow regularization to dis-
cern geometry-dependent patterns. While effective, such an
explicit curvature modeling strategy relies on curvature
estimation and curvature-driven reconstruction, which in-
troduces additional computational overhead. To address the
risk of geometric mismatch and implicitly capture structural
properties for mixed-manifold modeling, building upon the
GnnE2H mapping module, we further propose EH-Fusion, an
adaptive Euclidean–hyperbolic fusion framework that lever-
ages curvature via node-level neighborhood expansion patterns
to dynamically balance Euclidean and hyperbolic geometries
with low overhead and high portability.
Our method exploits the complementary roles of Euclidean
and hyperbolic geometries in modeling graphs with varying
degrees of hierarchy. Hyperbolic space provides a natural
inductive bias for tree-like graphs through its exponential
expansion property, whereas Euclidean space remains a re-
liable alternative when hierarchical structure is weak or less
pronounced. In contrast, positively curved spherical geometry
is more suitable for structures dominated by cycles or cliques,
which fall outside the flat-to-hierarchical regime considered
in this work. Therefore, we focus on Euclidean–hyperbolic
complementarity for hierarchy-aware graph learning.
Overall, this work provides a unified geometric and
learning-theoretic perspective on graph representation learning
for hierarchical data. By quantifying layer-wise crowding in
Euclidean embeddings for tree-like data and linking it to
margin-based generalization, we offer a principled explanation
for the advantages of hyperbolic representations and derive
models from these theoretical insights.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Deep Hyperbolic Representation Learning
Hyperbolic geometry has recently attracted increasing atten-
tion in deep representation learning for modeling hierarchical
structures. In visual representation learning, Yan et al. [16]
introduced hyperbolic metrics and hierarchical clustering to
capture multi-level semantic relations, addressing the difficulty
of modeling latent hierarchical structures in tree-like data.
In language representation learning, Nickel and Kiela [7]
proposed Poincaré embeddings, demonstrating that hyperbolic
geometry effectively encodes latent hierarchical structures with
enhanced representational capacity and structural fidelity. In
multimodal representation learning, Desai et al. [20] developed
a hyperbolic image–text contrastive framework, leveraging the
geometric properties of hyperbolic space to model multi-level
semantic organization in vision–language representations.
A common design paradigm in these approaches is to intro-
duce a hyperbolic realization layer on top of Euclidean back-
bone models to leverage the metric advantages of hyperbolic
space for hierarchical representation, while the underlying
architectures remain Euclidean. This paradigm suggests that
introducing a simple exponential mapping layer enables deep
Euclidean encoders to be effectively optimized under hyper-
bolic metrics, yielding high-quality hyperbolic embeddings.
These observations indicate that the effectiveness of hyperbolic
representations largely stems from the metric geometry itself.
B. Hyperbolic Graph Representation Learning
Recent advances in hyperbolic graph representation
learning have followed a progressively deepening geometric
modeling paradigm. HGNN [26] introduced a tangent-space
learning framework, where graph aggregation is performed
in the Euclidean tangent space, followed by projection onto
hyperbolic manifolds via exponential mappings. Building upon
this idea, HGCN [27] instantiated this framework with GCN-
style architectures while preserving inductive capability. Sub-
sequently, FHNN [28] proposed a fully hyperbolic framework,
where feature transformations, neighborhood aggregation, and
nonlinearities are defined entirely within hyperbolic mani-
folds. By performing all operations directly in hyperbolic
space, FHNN achieves stronger geometric consistency and
improved expressiveness for hierarchical graph structures.
Overall, hyperbolic graph representation learning has evolved
from tangent-space approximated GNNs to intrinsic hyper-
bolic graph operations, reflecting a transition from geometry-
assisted representations to fully geometry-consistent ones.
Taken together, existing studies reveal two distinct
paradigms of hyperbolic modeling. In deep representation
learning, hyperbolic geometry is introduced via a mapping
layer that lifts Euclidean representations onto hyperbolic space
and enables optimization under hyperbolic metrics. In graph
neural networks, recent studies have explored deeper integra-
tion of hyperbolic geometry throughout the entire network
architecture. This naturally raises the questions of what
fundamentally drives the effectiveness of hyperbolic rep-
resentations and what principles can guide our selection
among different pathways. In this work, through margin-
based generalization analysis and extensive experiments, we
show that much of this advantage stems from the underlying
metric geometry.
C. Geometric Perspective on Euclidean Crowding
Hyperbolic geometry is widely recognized as a natural
representation space for hierarchical or tree-like data. This
advantage is often attributed to the exponential volume growth
of hyperbolic space, which aligns with the exponential ex-
pansion of hierarchical structures. In contrast, the polyno-
mial volume growth of Euclidean space creates a geometric
mismatch, leading to the crowding phenomenon [7], [12],
[29], [30] among nodes in deeper layers. Several studies have
established the theoretical advantages of hyperbolic embed-
dings. In particular, the notion of δ-hyperbolicity introduced
by Gromov characterizes the tree-likeness of graphs, where
trees are 0-hyperbolic in the sense of Gromov and therefore
naturally align with hyperbolic geometry [31]. Moreover, tree
metrics admit embeddings into the hyperbolic plane with
only O(1) distortion, whereas Euclidean embeddings typi-
cally incur O(log n) distortion [23]. Nevertheless, existing
studies primarily focus on global distortion properties, while
the mechanisms underlying this geometric mismatch and
the resulting crowding phenomenon remain underexplored.
In particular, how Euclidean crowding arises in hierarchical
representations and how it affects learning performance has not
been quantitatively characterized. In this work, we provide a
complementary geometric perspective by analyzing the layer-
wise minimum separation between node embeddings. Inspired
by sphere packing arguments [32], [33],which study the max-
imal number of points that can be placed within a sphere
under a fixed minimum separation, we instead analyze the
opposite scenario, where all nodes are embedded within the


4
sphere and characterize how the minimum separation decays
with tree depth. We show that fixed-dimensional Euclidean
embeddings of tree-like graphs inevitably exhibit layer-wise
crowding. Furthermore, by relating geometric separation to
the functional margin of pairwise distance-based models, we
establish that such crowding leads to weaker margin-based
generalization guarantees, providing a theoretical perspective
on the advantages of hyperbolic representations for hierarchi-
cal data.

D. Gromov δ-Hyperbolicity
To quantify the hierarchical structure of graph data, we
adopt Gromov δ-hyperbolicity [31], [34] as a metric charac-
terization of tree-likeness. Unlike curvature, which describes
local bending behavior, δ-hyperbolicity captures how closely
the global distance structure resembles a tree metric. This
directly connects to our focus on hierarchical expansion and
the corresponding benefits of hyperbolic representations.
a) Gromov δ-Hyperbolicity: Let (X, d) be a metric
space. For any four points x, y, z, t ∈X, define the three
pairwise distance sums:
S1 = d(x, y) + d(z, t),
S2 = d(x, z) + d(y, t),
S3 = d(x, t) + d(y, z).
(8)
The metric space (X, d) is said to be δ-hyperbolic if, for every
quadruple of points, the two largest values among {S1, S2, S3}
differ by at most 2δ, i.e.,
max{S1, S2, S3} −mid{S1, S2, S3} ≤2δ,
(9)
where mid(·) denotes the second-largest value.
Intuitively, δ measures how close a metric space is to a tree
metric. A smaller δ indicates stronger hierarchical organization
and greater tree-likeness, favoring hyperbolic geometry as
an inductive bias. Conversely, a relatively large δ suggests
weaker tree-like structure, where Euclidean geometry becomes
a natural complement. In particular, trees are 0-hyperbolic.
b) Why δ-Hyperbolicity Instead of Curvature: Although
hyperbolic geometry is classically associated with negative
curvature, curvature and Gromov δ-hyperbolicity offer dif-
ferent geometric perspectives. Curvature characterizes local
geometric behavior, such as local bending, geodesic diver-
gence, or neighborhood connectivity patterns. In comparison,
δ-hyperbolicity provides a global geometric measure of tree-
likeness and hierarchical organization. Specifically, it charac-
terizes how closely the global distance structure of a metric
space resembles a tree metric, thereby reflecting the strength
of hierarchical structure in graph data. The advantage of
hyperbolic representations is closely related to the exponential
expansion property of hyperbolic space, which enables hier-
archical structures to be represented with improved separation
and reduced crowding. Therefore, our analysis focuses on
whether the overall graph geometry exhibits strong global
hierarchy and tree-like expansion, rather than relying only on
the curvature behavior of local regions. For this reason, we use
Gromov δ-hyperbolicity as a structural indicator for assessing
whether graph data are likely to benefit from hyperbolic
representation learning.

IV. GEOMETRIC CROWDING AND GENERALIZATION
In this section, we develop a geometric framework that
connects embedding geometry to generalization guarantees for
hierarchical data. We first demonstrate that fixed-dimensional
Euclidean embeddings
inevitably suffer
from layer-wise
crowding when representing tree-structured data. This phe-

margin of pairwise distance-based models, we further reveal
how Euclidean crowding weakens margin-based generalization
guarantees. In contrast, hyperbolic geometry preserves sepa-
ration more effectively and thus admit stronger generalization
guarantees for near-tree structured data.

V. FROM THEORY TO MODEL: THE EH-FUSION
FRAMEWORK
Motivated by the theoretical analysis in Section 1, we
propose a lightweight framework that exploits the metric
advantages of hyperbolic geometry while remaining the flex-
ibility of Euclidean graph encoders. Unlike fully hyperbolic
architectures that replace all Euclidean operations, our ap-
proach introduces a minimal geometric realization layer and
an adaptive mechanism for balancing between Euclidean and
hyperbolic geometries based on local structural patterns. The
proposed EH-Fusion framework consists of two main com-
ponents: (i) a multi-geometry embedding module that maps
shared Euclidean representations onto different manifolds via
lightweight exponential mapping layers, and (ii) a node-wise
adaptive fusion module that dynamically balances predictions
from multiple geometries according to local neighborhood
expansion patterns.

geometric structures, we further introduce a topology-aware
selection mechanism hat uses high-order degree statistics
to compute node-specific mixing weights, enabling adaptive
aggregation of predictions from different manifolds.

B. Node-wise Adaptive geometry Selection
Real-world graphs often exhibit mixed geometric structures:

others are better approximated by locally Euclidean geometry.

therefore suboptimal. To address this, we propose a data-driven
fusion mechanism that adaptively selects the most suitable
geometry for each node based on its local neighborhood
expansion pattern.

exponentially. This distinction manifests in graph-structured

better aligns with the node’s latent structure.

VI. EXPERIMENT
A. Experimental setup
1) Datasets: We evaluate the proposed method on five
graph learning benchmarks spanning citation networks, trans-
portation networks, disease propagation graphs, and large-
scale product co-purchase graphs. These datasets exhibit vary-
ing degrees of tree-likeness, which we quantify using Gromov
δ-hyperbolicity: smaller δ values indicate stronger hyperbolic
(tree-like) characteristics.
a) Cora [36]: Cora is a standard citation network dataset,
where nodes represent scientific publications and edges denote
citation relationships. Each node is associated with a high-
dimensional bag-of-words feature vector.
b) Citeseer [37]: The Citeseer dataset is another classic
citation network with similar characteristics to Cora. Nodes
represent scientific publications belonging to a set of distinct
classes, and edges correspond to citation relationships.
c) Airport [27]:
The Airport dataset models an air
transportation network, where nodes correspond to airports
and edges represent flight routes between airports. This graph
exhibits real-world topology and structural diversity.
d) Disease [27]: The Disease dataset represents a disease
propagation network, where nodes denote individuals or enti-
ties and edges reflect potential transmission paths of a disease
process.
e) Ele-Fashion [38]: Ele-Fashion is a node classification
dataset constructed from Amazon-Fashion, where each node
corresponds to a product image and edges indicate whether
two products are co-purchased. The graph contains 97,766
nodes and 199,602 edges.
Together, these datasets provide a diverse evaluation span-
ning different scales, domains, and levels of hyperbolicity,
enabling a comprehensive assessment of the proposed frame-
work.
2) Baselines: To comprehensively evaluate the effective-
ness of our method, we compare against representative graph
learning approaches spanning both Euclidean and hyperbolic
geometries.

fusion mitigates geometric mismatch and ensures consistent
performance under heterogeneous hyperbolicity levels.
D. Discussion and Future Work
This work provides a geometric and generalization-theoretic
perspective on the advantage of hyperbolic representations for
hierarchical data. By quantifying Euclidean crowding through
layer-wise minimum separation and linking it to margin-based
generalization, we explain why Euclidean embeddings strug-
gle with tree-like structures. Furthermore, to handle graphs
with mixed geometric structures, we propose EH-Fusion, a
lightweight geometry-adaptive framework that balances Eu-
clidean and hyperbolic metrics to leverage their complemen-
tary strengths, enabling improved geometric consistency.
Empirically, we observe that mixed-manifold models con-
sistently outperform their single-manifold counterparts. More-
over, the relative advantage of each geometry is strongly gov-
erned by the intrinsic structure of the data: hyperbolic models
are more effective for highly tree-like graphs with strong
hyperbolicity, whereas Euclidean models become increasingly
competitive as the degree of hyperbolicity decreases. These
findings reinforce the connection between structural separa-
bility, geometric crowding, and representation effectiveness.
Nevertheless, identifying a principled δ-hyperbolicity thresh-
old that can reliably guide the pre-selection of embedding
geometry remains an open and practically important direction.
Beyond δ-hyperbolicity, future work may explore more
directly computable separability measures. To account for
scale variations, one may consider a normalized variant of
the separation margin, ˜∆= 1 −d+/d−, which provides a
scale-invariant characterization of structural separability. For
computational convenience, crowding in hierarchical graphs
can be characterized by the ratio between the average sibling
distance and the average parent–child distance, which captures
intra-layer compression. This idea naturally extends to general
graphs by replacing sibling nodes with k-hop intra-layer
neighbors and parent nodes with predecessor nodes from the
previous layer.
These geometry-aware measures not only provide analytical
characterization of learned representations but also offer prac-
tical utility for downstream tasks. Among various applications,
we identify graph generation as the most promising direction.
Specifically, separation-based geometric measures such as ∆
and its induced crowding metrics can serve multiple roles in
graph generation: (1) as evaluation criteria for geometric
fidelity, by comparing structural separability between real and
generated graphs; (2) as regularization terms to suppress
overly crowded structures and enable controllable generation
of hierarchical patterns; and (3) as conditioning signals for
the generation process, where a generator is trained to produce
graphs whose ∆falls within a specified range, allowing fine-
grained control over branching patterns and hierarchical depth.
Overall, this perspective connects structural separability
and local crowding quantification to geometry-aware graph
generation. We hope these insights will inform the design
of future graph learning methods with stronger geometric
consistency.
