Abstract
Purpose: To evaluate the in vivo potential and cross-cohort generalization of
deep learning-based super-resolution for intracranial 4D Flow MRI, assessing to
what extent transfer learning improves regional accuracy across heterogeneous
neurological cohorts.
Materials and Methods: This mixed prospective-retrospective study included
12 prospectively enrolled participants (March 16–November 19, 2025) and 6 ret-
rospective healthy extracranial participants (August 26–September 24, 2024).
Eighteen participants (mean age, 35.4 years; range, 23-70 years; 8 women) were
analyzed: 12 healthy participants, 3 with moyamoya disease, and 3 with multiple
sclerosis. A convolutional neural network pretrained on synthetic cerebrovas-
cular data was fine-tuned using cohort-specific or multi-cohort data. Paired

1.2-mm and 0.6-mm 4D Flow MRI scans were acquired. High-resolution-derived
1.2-mm inputs were used for fine-tuning and voxel-wise evaluation; native
standard-resolution scans were used for angiographic and hemodynamic analy-
ses. Outcomes included velocity-normalized root mean square error (vNRMSE),
Bland–Altman agreement, precision–recall area under the curve (PR-AUC), and
hemodynamic measures.
Results: Synthetic pretraining reduced mean artery-wise vNRMSE versus inter-
polation (0.147 vs 0.166). Pooled transfer learning yielded the lowest mean
vNRMSE (0.119 vs 0.127 for cohort-specific models and 0.147 for synthetic
pretraining) and narrower 95% limits-of-agreement widths than synthetic pre-
training across all test cases (0.109–0.222 vs 0.154–0.278 m/s). Super-resolved
phase-contrast angiography had higher PR-AUC than native standard-resolution
imaging in all cohorts (0.848–0.896 vs 0.486–0.805).
Conclusion: Pooled multi-cohort transfer learning reduced reconstruction error
and narrowed voxel-wise limits of agreement relative to synthetic pretraining.
Super-resolution also improved vessel depiction relative to both standard- and
high-resolution imaging.

1 Introduction
Traditionally, neurovascular disease has been diagnosed from an anatomical paradigm,
with imaging techniques primarily used to qualitatively visualize intracranial vascular
morphology and pathology. Yet many clinical questions are fundamentally hemody-
namic, including whether flow is compromised distal to a stenosis, whether collateral
supply is sufficient, or how flow redistributes after revascularization. Quantitative
clinical intracranial hemodynamic assessment, however, remains challenging, with the
shielding cranium, limited interrogation windows, and tortuous vascular anatomy
restricting the use of routine tools used in other vascular compartments [1, 2]. In this
context, four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D Flow MRI) enables
non-invasive visualization and quantification of cerebrovascular blood flow across a
large vascular territory [3, 4]. However, despite translational advances in acquisi-
tion, correction, and quantification [5–9], a fundamental trade-off remains between
spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and scan time: high-resolution acquisitions
are lengthy and impractical, whereas standard-resolution acquisitions cannot capture
small branches, underestimate peak velocities, and reduce vessel conspicuity. These
challenges are particularly relevant in steno-occlusive disease such as moyamoya, where
hemodynamic assessment depends on resolving altered flow in small collateral vessels
and the downstream circulation [10, 11].
To address this, deep learning-based super-resolution has emerged as a practical
strategy to increase image resolution without extending scan time or modifying hard-
ware or pulse sequences, with the potential to sharpen vessel depiction and improve
2


hemodynamic quantification post-scan [12, 13]. However, existing intracranial super-
resolution models are predominantly trained on synthetic surrogate data, with in vivo
validation generally limited to small cohorts or indirect reference settings [12–14].
Specifically, it remains unclear whether synthetic training generalizes sufficiently to
in vivo scans - where noise characteristics, anatomy, and disease-related flow patterns
differ - or whether additional adaptation is required. In this context, transfer learn-
ing offers a potential mechanism for adapting synthetic-pretrained models to in vivo
data, supported by extensive evidence from other imaging domains [15]. However, the
benefit of such fine-tuning for intracranial super-resolution 4D Flow MRI, particularly
across heterogeneous clinical cohorts, has not been established.
The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the in vivo performance and cross-
cohort generalization of a synthetic-pretrained super-resolution model for intracranial
4D Flow MRI using paired standard- and high-resolution acquisitions, and to deter-
mine whether transfer learning provides additional benefit across clinically relevant
neurological cohorts. We hypothesized that transfer learning would improve perfor-
mance on acquired in vivo data, reflected by reduced reconstruction error relative
to synthetic pretraining alone. We further evaluated angiographic vessel depic-
tion and quantitative hemodynamic performance to assess the clinical potential of
super-resolution across diverse cerebrovascular settings.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Study Design and Participants
This mixed prospective-retrospective study evaluated the in vivo generalization of a
synthetic-pretrained super-resolution model and whether transfer learning improved
performance across heterogeneous cohorts. The study included 18 participants (8
women, 10 men; mean age, 35.4 years; range, 23–70 years) in four cohorts: healthy
intracranial (n = 6), healthy extracranial (n = 6), neurovascular (moyamoya, n = 3),
and neuroinflammatory (multiple sclerosis, n = 3). An overview of the study and eval-
uation workflow is provided in Figure 1. The intracranial cohorts were prospectively
enrolled between March 16, 2025 and November 19, 2025. The extracranial cohort
was retrospectively selected from examinations acquired between August 26, 2024 and
September 24, 2024. Healthy participants were recruited as volunteers. Eligibility for
the patient cohorts required a clinical diagnosis of moyamoya disease or multiple scle-
rosis, respectively, and no contraindications for MRI. Participants were enrolled based
on availability during the study period, and all datasets meeting the study criteria
were included. No formal power calculation was performed; the sample size was based
on the available paired acquisitions and the feasibility of participant-level training and
held-out test partitions.
The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (EPM, 2023-
07667-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled participants. Data
were pseudonymized before analysis, with direct personal identifiers removed and par-
ticipants represented by study-specific identifiers. No participant overlapped with prior
publications. Participant characteristics, including sex-stratified age summaries, are
provided in Table 1.

2.2 MRI Acquisition and Image Processing
All participants underwent dual-velocity-encoding, kt-GRAPPA-accelerated 4D Flow
MRI at 3 T (acceleration factor R = 5), with paired standard-resolution (LR; 1.2-mm
isotropic) and high-resolution (HR; 0.6-mm isotropic) acquisitions and 3D time-of-
flight (TOF) MR angiography. For the intracranial cohorts, the imaging field of view
was centered on the Circle of Willis, whereas the extracranial cohort was acquired with
the field of view centered on the cervical carotid arteries. Intracranial and extracra-
nial imaging were performed on two scanner platforms at separate sites. Acquisition
parameters are summarized in Table 2.
4D Flow MRI was processed using a standardized semiautomatic workflow in
MATLAB (MathWorks), as described previously [16–18]. A single TOF-derived ves-
sel segmentation was generated per participant and rigidly registered (SPM12 [19])
separately to the native LR and HR acquisitions. Data preprocessing, vessel label-
ing, centerline extraction, analysis-plane placement, and direction-consistency mask
refinement are detailed in the Supplemental Material, Section S1.

2.4 Outcome Measures and Evaluation
The primary model-performance outcome was artery-wise voxel-wise reconstruction
error between super-resolved velocities and the matched HR reference, quantified
using velocity-normalized root mean square error (vNRMSE). Secondary outcomes
included Bland–Altman agreement at peak systole, phase-contrast MR angiography
(PCMRA) vessel depiction relative to the co-registered TOF-derived anatomical ref-
erence using precision–recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) and maximum Dice
coefficient (Dicemax), and comparisons against native-acquisition hemodynamic mea-
sures, including flow rate (Q), 95th-percentile velocity (vp95), voxel-wise velocity
distributions, and mean absolute divergence. The publicly available cerebrovascu-
lar 4DFlowNet model was applied to the same held-out data as an external model
benchmark.
Metric definitions and benchmark details are provided in Supplemental S4–S5, with
complementary linear regression described in Supplemental S6. Because one partici-
pant per cohort was held out for testing and voxel- and plane-level observations were
nested within participants, analyses were descriptive. No inferential hypothesis tests
were performed and no P-value threshold was defined.
3 Results
All 18 enrolled participants were included in the analysis, with the four prede-
fined cohorts each contributing one participant-disjoint test case. The remaining
participants were assigned to training and validation partitions as described in
Supplemental S3.

3.2 Super-resolution improves angiographic vessel delineation
In Figure 4, PCMRA visualizations derived from LR, HR, and super-resolved
reconstructions across all four cohorts are compared with the corresponding TOF
angiography as anatomical reference. Consistent with the background-suppression
objective incorporated during training, all three super-resolution models showed
reduced background signal relative to LR and HR, with visually clearer delineation of
major arterial branches. In the neuroinflammatory case, the M2 branches were faint
on LR and HR PCMRA but more clearly visualized on the super-resolved images.
In the neurovascular case, the carotid siphon and downstream branches were likewise
more apparent after super-resolution. Smaller vessels surrounding the affected prox-
imal M1 were also more apparent on super-resolved PCMRA than on LR and HR,
although their depiction remained incomplete relative to TOF.
The qualitative findings were supported by quantitative comparison with the TOF-
derived vessel masks. PR-AUC was higher for every super-resolved model than for
native LR or HR in all four test cases (LR: 0.486–0.805; HR: 0.206–0.457; CFD: 0.816–
0.883; COHORT: 0.845–0.893; POOL: 0.848–0.896). Corresponding Dicemax values
were 0.585–0.756 for LR, 0.414–0.639 for HR, 0.727–0.830 for CFD, 0.780–0.813 for
COHORT, and 0.779–0.813 for POOL.
3.3 Super-resolution preserved quantitative hemodynamics
Figure 5 summarizes plane-based hemodynamic comparisons across all four test cases.
Visual inspection of the velocity-field panels showed that the super-resolved recon-
structions had more spatially continuous velocity patterns than LR, particularly within
higher-velocity flow regions. In the neurovascular case, TOF-derived segmentation
incompletely captured several smaller vessels within the moyamoya-associated region.
Four such vessels were therefore selected for exploratory analysis and labeled MMV-
1–MMV-4, with super-resolved PCMRA-derived masks used to define these regions
consistently across LR, HR, CFD, and POOL.
The super-resolved flow-rate (Q) and vp95 curves broadly followed the temporal
profiles observed in the acquired LR and HR data. Both super-resolution models gen-
erally shifted plane-based vp95 toward the HR reference, with estimates falling between
LR and HR across most displayed plane groups. For flow rate, POOL produced the
highest mean peak-systolic estimate across the 10 displayed plane groups (4.54 mL/s
vs 4.08 mL/s for LR, 3.82 mL/s for HR, and 4.02 mL/s for CFD).
Table 3 provides artery-level peak-systolic flow estimates across all analyzed ves-
sel segments. POOL yielded the highest median peak-systolic flow (Qpeak) in 33 of
36 arteries. Within the smaller intracranial vessels (MCA, PCA, ACA, PCOM, and
moyamoya-associated vessels), CFD exceeded LR in 23 of 23 arteries and HR exceeded
LR in 21 of 23, while CFD exceeded HR in 16 of 23. In the larger proximal vessels,
differences between LR, HR, and CFD were more mixed. In the extracranial case,
LR peak flow was generally higher than HR, consistent with different sampling of the
systolic peak in the separately acquired scans.

of the systolic peak between the separately acquired scans.

CFD reconstructions showed a pronounced accumulation of near-zero velocities
in several arteries, resulting in more strongly skewed distributions. POOL showed
less near-zero accumulation and generally higher median velocities than CFD across
intracranial territories, although this pattern was not consistent in all arteries, par-
ticularly extracranially. Voxel-wise vp95 was slightly higher for CFD than POOL

4 Discussion
In this study, we performed a preliminary multi-cohort in vivo evaluation of super-
resolution for intracranial 4D Flow MRI, focusing on synthetic-to-in vivo generaliza-
tion and the effect of transfer learning across neurological cohorts and acquisition
settings. The synthetic-pretrained model generalized across all four test cohorts,
while transfer learning provided additional improvements in reconstruction error and
voxel-wise agreement. Pooled multi-cohort fine-tuning showed the most consistent
overall performance compared with cohort-specific adaptation. Super-resolution also
improved angiographic vessel depiction and enabled exploratory hemodynamic assess-
ment in vessel segments that were poorly depicted at native resolution. Together, these
findings support super-resolution 4D Flow MRI as a proof-of-principle approach to
higher-resolution cerebrovascular flow assessment.
The synthetic-to-in vivo performance of the CFD-pretrained model supports the
use of acquisition-mimicking synthetic data for pretraining [20]. Previous intracranial
4D Flow MRI super-resolution studies have largely relied on synthetic training data
generated through simpler downsampling or k-space cropping procedures, with in vivo
evaluation often limited to small cohorts or indirect reference settings [12, 13, 21]. In
contrast, our pipeline produces a synthetic domain sufficiently representative of clinical
image data to support evaluation without in vivo adaptation, consistently yielding
higher voxel-wise agreement as compared to the reference 4DFlowNet network [12],
and serving as a solid pretrained foundation for subsequent transfer learning.
Continuing, transfer learning incrementally improved reconstruction accuracy,
revealing a practical observation: diversity in fine-tuning data may be preferred over
narrowly targeted tuning. While cohort-specific models performed well within their
own domain, they showed reduced cross-cohort performance under domain shift,
most notably when transferring from extracranial carotid vessels into intracranial
distal arteries. In contrast, pooled multi-cohort fine-tuning consistently exceeded
cohort-specific performance, even when compared to domain-specific networks. While
extension into even more distal vessels remains undetermined, these observations sup-
port further exploration of broadly adapted models trained on heterogeneous clinical
cases, rather than relying exclusively on separate models for each clinical subgroup.
Beyond voxel-wise reconstruction metrics, super-resolution improved vessel depic-
tion on derived PCMRA, with reduced background signal and clearer delineation of
major arterial branches – improvements of possible relevance to downstream image
15


analysis tasks including segmentation, centerline extraction, and standardized place-
ment of analysis planes. In the moyamoya case, smaller vessels surrounding the affected
proximal M1 region appeared more conspicuous after super-resolution and were
incompletely captured by the TOF-derived arterial labels. This enabled exploratory
visualization and quantification using super-resolved PCMRA masks. However, these
vessels are small, tortuous, and close to or below the effective spatial resolution of the
acquired data, and the high-resolution reference remains uncertain in this territory.
Disease-specific conclusions therefore require validation in larger moyamoya cohorts
and against independent angiographic or physiologic references.
Compared with previous cerebrovascular super-resolution studies, which have often
relied on synthetic or indirect reference settings or limited in vivo validation, the
present study directly evaluated synthetic-pretrained and transfer-learned models
against acquired high-resolution data across heterogeneous cohorts [12, 13, 21]. By
combining voxel-wise reconstruction metrics with complementary angiographic and
hemodynamic evaluation, the findings provide early evidence for the translational
potential of intracranial super-resolution 4D Flow MRI. Although the approximately
9-minute standard-resolution protocol substantially reduced scan time relative to the
high-resolution reference, it remains lengthy for routine clinical use. These findings
should therefore be viewed as an intermediate step toward clinically feasible intracra-
nial 4D Flow MRI, alongside continued work on acquisition acceleration, motion
correction, reproducibility, and disease-specific hemodynamic validation [4, 7–9, 22–
25].
This study has a few notable limitations. First, cohort sizes were modest, particu-
larly for the disease cohorts, and only one participant per cohort was held out for final
testing. The analyses were therefore descriptive and cannot establish participant-level
statistical significance or disease-specific effects. The cohorts were also not demograph-
ically matched and differed in scanner platform and acquisition characteristics, which
may confound direct cross-cohort comparisons, although this heterogeneity also pro-
vided a pragmatic test of domain shift. Second, voxel-wise fine-tuning and evaluation
relied on HR-derived LR inputs, ensuring exact spatiotemporal correspondence; these
inputs may not reproduce all artifacts present in independently acquired standard-
resolution scans. Native standard-resolution acquisitions were therefore retained for
angiographic and hemodynamic analyses. Third, the high-resolution scans were them-
selves noisy, particularly near vessel boundaries, and TOF-derived reference masks
were subject to registration and thresholding uncertainty. Finally, no independent
external test dataset or independent physiologic reference, such as flow-phantom or
invasive flow measurements, was available. External multi-center validation and inde-
pendent reference measurements will therefore be required before clinical performance
can be established.
In conclusion, synthetic-pretrained deep learning-based super-resolution general-
ized to heterogeneous in vivo 4D Flow MRI data, with transfer learning providing
additional gains and pooled multi-cohort fine-tuning showing the most consistent
overall performance. Super-resolution also improved vessel depiction and supported
exploratory hemodynamic assessment in small cerebrovascular territories. These find-
ings support further multi-center validation of broadly adapted super-resolution
models for intracranial 4D Flow MRI.

S1 Image preprocessing, vessel labeling, analysis
plane placement, and direction-consistency mask
refinement
This section provides implementation details for (i) four-dimensional flow magnetic
resonance imaging (4D Flow MRI) preprocessing, (ii) voxel-wise artery labeling,
(iii) centerline-based analysis plane placement, and (iv) direction-consistency mask
refinement used prior to voxel-wise and hemodynamic analyses.
Four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D Flow MRI) preprocessing
followed the correction and vessel-analysis workflow described in previous work [16–18],
including eddy-current correction, co-registration of time-of-flight (TOF) angiography,
TOF segmentation threshold refinement, centerline extraction from the TOF-derived
vessel masks, and subsequent vessel analysis. Extracranial datasets were processed by
an investigator with more than 10 years of experience in neurovascular 4D Flow MRI.
Intracranial datasets were processed by a doctoral researcher who had been trained in
the workflow by the experienced investigator and subsequently performed processing
independently according to the same protocol.
TOF angiography served as a common anatomical reference across LR, HR, and
super-resolved reconstructions. Although TOF-based segmentation may be affected
by signal dropout and registration error, use of the same TOF-derived segmentation
reduced the potential for reconstruction-dependent segmentation differences to bias
comparisons.
For intracranial datasets, a U-Net-based model [26] generated initial voxel-wise
artery labels for the Circle of Willis, which were visually reviewed and corrected when
needed by the doctoral researcher using ITK-SNAP [27]. Extracranial vessel territories
were delineated manually. The following artery labels were used for reporting: inter-
nal carotid arteries (ICAs), middle cerebral arteries (MCAs), anterior cerebral arteries
(ACAs), posterior cerebral arteries (PCAs), basilar artery (BA), and posterior commu-
nicating arteries (PCOMs) when clearly identifiable. For the extracranial cohort, labels
comprised the ICAs and vertebral arteries (VAs), manually labeled by the doctoral
researcher. No formal inter- or intrarater variability analysis was performed.
For plane-based hemodynamic quantification, analysis planes were placed along
each labeled vessel segment using the corresponding TOF-derived centerlines. Planes
were generated perpendicular to the local centerline tangent and placed at equidis-
tant spacing of 0.5 mm along the centerline. For the moyamoya-associated vessels
(MMV-1–MMV-4), a spacing of 0.25 mm was used to account for their smaller size.
Each plane group and artery reported was visually quality-controlled to exclude
planes at intersecting bifurcations, and to ensure consistent lumen coverage across
reconstructions.
To improve robustness to isolated spurious velocity voxels - particularly prevalent
near the vessel boundary - a direction-consistency refinement step was applied within
the TOF-derived lumen mask prior to voxel-wise comparisons and hemodynamic anal-
yses. Specifically, voxels were removed if their velocity direction deviated by more than

S2 Network architecture, loss functions, and
implementation details
Synthetic pretraining data were generated from four patient-specific cerebrovas-
cular computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations previously used in related
intracranial super-resolution studies [12, 20]. The simulations comprised time-resolved
3D velocity fields derived from patient-specific cerebrovascular geometries under
physiologic boundary conditions and included anatomies with varying degrees of
steno-occlusive disease. Each simulated velocity field was paired with a co-registered
magnitude volume derived from clinical magnitude data and mapped to the CFD
anatomy. Paired synthetic high- and low-resolution 4D Flow MRI were then generated
using an acquisition-mimicking pipeline incorporating k-space truncation, noise injec-
tion matched to clinically observed levels, and dual-velocity-encoding reconstruction.
This provided inherently co-registered high- and low-resolution pairs for supervised
pretraining before in vivo fine-tuning. Further details are provided in [20].
We implemented a 3D residual convolutional neural network (CNN) based on
the 4DFlowNet-inspired [12] generator design in [20], using residual-in-residual dense
blocks (RRDBs) [28] as the main feature extractor. The network takes as input a
low-resolution velocity patch v = [u, v, w] and magnitude information, which are first
encoded by two 3D convolution layers per branch. Velocity and magnitude features
are concatenated and fused by an additional 3D convolution to form a shared low-
resolution feature representation. This representation is then processed by a stack of
four RRDBs in the low-resolution space. Spatial upsampling to the target grid is per-
formed using trilinear interpolation. In the high-resolution space, features are refined
by an additional stack of two RRDBs. Finally, the network branches into three output
22


heads with two 3D convolutions each to predict the super-resolved velocity compo-
nents ˆu, ˆv, and ˆw, which are concatenated to form the final 3-channel output. All
convolution layers use symmetric padding.

S3 Data partitions and training details
The baseline model was pretrained on the full set of n = 4 synthetic computational
fluid dynamics (CFD)-derived velocity fields used in previous works [12, 20]. In total,
the synthetic training set comprised 123,500 aligned HR/LR patch pairs, generated by
sampling 10 patches per CFD time frame and applying rigid rotation augmentation
to each patch.
For in vivo transfer learning, cohort-specific and pooled fine-tuning used the
participant-level partitions summarized in Table S1. One participant per cohort was
held out for final testing. For the healthy intracranial and extracranial cohorts, four
participants were used for training and one separate participant for validation. For the
neurovascular and neuroinflammatory cohorts, two participants were used for train-
ing, with model-optimization patches resampled from one training participant because
no additional participant was available. Final test partitions were participant-disjoint
in all cohorts and were not used during training or model selection.
The held-out test subjects were: intracranial healthy (male, 28 years), extracranial
healthy (female, 23 years), neuroinflammatory (male, 39 years, multiple sclerosis), and
neurovascular (female, 31 years, with left-sided moyamoya vasculopathy involving the
proximal M1 segment).
To emphasize the impact of data diversity rather than the number of samples, we
adjusted the sampling rate across cohorts to account for differences in the number of
subjects and cardiac frames. Specifically, cohort-specific fine-tuning used 40 patches
per time frame for the intracranial and extracranial cohort models and 80 patches per
time frame for the two patient cohort models, which after augmentation corresponded
to 10,000 (intracranial), 15,200 (extracranial), and 8,000 patches each (neurovascular
and neuroinflammatory). The multi-cohort model sampled 10 patches per time frame
from intracranial and extracranial cohorts and 20 patches per time frame from each

S5 4DFlowNet benchmark implementation
As an external benchmark, we evaluated the cerebrovascular 4DFlowNet [12] using
the publicly available pretrained model weights from the authors’ repository (https://
github.com/EdwardFerdian/4DFlowNet). The model was not retrained on the present
synthetic or in vivo datasets. For voxel-wise comparison, 4DFlowNet was applied to
the same HR-derived LR inputs used for evaluation of the present models, and outputs
26


were evaluated against the same HR references using the same TOF-derived vessel
masks.
While complete network architecture and training details are provided in [12], the
main differences relative to the present baseline model are summarized here. First,
while the same set of n = 4 cerebrovascular flow simulations was utilized, the pub-
licly available cerebrovascular 4DFlowNet weights were trained using the data split
and preprocessing strategy described in [12]. Here, two CFD-derived cases were used
for training, one for validation, and one for testing. In contrast, the present baseline
model was pretrained using all four available CFD-derived cerebrovascular cases, with
subsequent evaluation performed only on the held-out in vivo test subjects described
in the main manuscript. Second, the synthetic image-generation pipelines differed. In
4DFlowNet, low-resolution inputs were generated using the original cerebrovascular
4DFlowNet downsampling procedure, designed to mimic spatial-resolution degrada-
tion and noise in 4D Flow MRI. In the present work, synthetic pretraining used a
related but updated simulation-to-MRI pipeline described in [20], which extends this
framework by incorporating a more acquisition-mimicking reconstruction procedure,
including dual-velocity-encoding reconstruction.
These differences were not modified or harmonized, because the purpose of the
benchmark was to compare the present synthetic-pretrained and transfer-learned
models against an established, previously published cerebrovascular super-resolution
framework. Consequently, differences in performance between 4DFlowNet and the
present models may reflect not only network architecture, but also differences in
training-data usage and synthetic data generation.
This comparison represents benchmarking against an externally developed model,
not validation on an independent external dataset.
