Abstract 

Early identification of neurodegenerative disorders using structural T1-weighted MRI is essential for timely clinical intervention, yet supervised deep learning models often struggle to generalize due to limited annotations and scanner-induced distribution shift. Self-supervised learning offers a promis-ing alternative; however, existing approaches are largely adapted from natural image paradigms, relying on contrastive objectives or generic masked reconstruction with voxel-wise losses, which may not fully capture the distributed morphometric patterns associated with neurodegeneration. We introduce SYMBA (SYstematic Morphometric Brain Autoencoding), a self-supervised pretraining framework specifically designed for structural brain MRI. SYMBA integrates anatomically con-strained masking, a multi-scale structural similarity (MS-SSIM) reconstruction objective, and a progressive curriculum that transitions from local anatomical detail to global brain morphology. These components explicitly incorporate neuroanatomical structure into the self-supervised objec-tive rather than relying on generic image priors. Pretrained on 6,953 T1-weighted MRI scans from nine heterogeneous cohorts, SYMBA is evaluated using frozen linear probing on two independent unseen datasets, enabling a strict assessment of out-of-distribution generalization. Across multiple clinical tasks, SYMBA consistently outperforms recent neuroimaging foundation models, achieving 0.930 AUROC for Alzheimer’s disease classification, 0.772 for mild cognitive impairment conver-sion prediction, and strong performance in amyloid status prediction. In low-label regimes, SYMBA demonstrates high sample efficiency, reaching approximately 0.90 AUROC for AD/CN classification

1. Introduction 

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in neuroimaging, en-abling the extraction of robust, transferable anatomical representations directly from large col-lections of unlabeled brain scans Li et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2026). A primary motivation for self-supervised representation learning in medical imaging is its ability to train effective down-stream models and transfer them to very small patient populations, which is a critical bottleneck in clinical domains where expert annotations are labor-intensive, expensive, and scarce Moradi et al. (2014). In this context, high-dimensional modalities like structural T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide rich volumetric depictions of brain anatomy, making them ideal for learn-ing transferable 3D morphological representations. Neurodegeneration is an extensively studied application domain and an excellent testbed for evaluating these methodological advances. Be-cause neurodegenerative conditions manifest as subtle, progressive structural changes distributed across interconnected brain networks Jack et al. (2010), testing representation learning frameworks in this domain rigorously assesses their capacity to capture complex morphometric patterns under low-sample regimes and population heterogeneity. Despite their performance on curated research cohorts Wen et al. (2020), supervised DL models for neuroimaging remain difficult to translate into routine clinical practice Teo et al. (2026). Their reliance on large, expertly annotated datasets limits scalability, as obtaining reliable diagnostic labels is both labor-intensive and expensive. This challenge is particularly pronounced for rare or highly heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorders, where pathological variability further reduces the availability of representative training samples. In addition, supervised models are often sensitive to distribution shifts arising from differences in scanner manufacturers, acquisition protocols, and patient populations, limiting their ability to generalize across clinical sites Nooner et al.; Guo et al. (2026). Recent advances in SSL, by contrast, have shown remarkable success in downstream neuroimag-ing tasks Chen et al. (2026) such as brain segmentation, image registration, and anatomical cor-respondence, where learned representations can transfer across datasets and imaging protocols. Representative examples include BrainSegFounder Cox et al. (2024) and BrainMorph Wang et al. (2025), which leverage large-scale pretraining to improve segmentation and registration perfor-mance. However, comparatively few self-supervised pretraining frameworks have been specifically designed for neurodegenerative disease analysis, where the learning objective fundamentally differs from these structural tasks and requires capturing subtle, distributed morphometric alterations associated with disease progression rather than precise anatomical localization. Although structural image segmentation and registration rely primarily on the accurate local-ization of anatomical boundaries, representation learning for neurodegenerative disease analysis requires capturing subtle, spatially distributed morphometric alterations that progressively affect large-scale brain networks rather than isolated brain regions Akan et al. (2025); Huang and Zhang 2(2022). Clinically relevant biomarkers in structural MRIs, such as cortical atrophy, ventricular en-largement, or hippocampal atrophy, manifest as gradual macroscopic changes that, while initially localized, progressively extend across multiple interconnected anatomical regions as the disease evolves Huang and Zhang (2022). Consequently, effective self-supervised pretraining for neurode-generation should encourage representations that model global brain morphology and long-range anatomical relationships instead of focusing solely on local structural patterns. Recent studies have begun extending SSL to neurodegenerative disease analysis. For example, BrainIAC Tak et al. (2024) and AnatCL Barbano et al. (2026) demonstrate that contrastive pre-training can improve downstream diagnostic and prognostic classification tasks by learning transfer-able anatomical representations from large-scale MRI collections. These studies provide encouraging evidence that self-supervised representation learning is a promising direction for neurodegeneration. However, they also highlight that the design of effective pretraining objectives remains largely un-explored, with current approaches relying predominantly on contrastive learning paradigms whose relative advantages over generative objectives have yet to be systematically investigated. Despite these encouraging results, several fundamental questions remain unanswered. First, al-though contrastive learning has become the dominant paradigm in self-supervised neuroimaging, the relative advantages of contrastive and generative pretraining objectives remain largely unexplored for neurodegenerative disease representation learning Wu et al. (2025). Second, little consensus exists regarding the most appropriate architectural choices, pretraining datasets, or evaluation pro-tocols for assessing representation quality. Existing studies predominantly rely on end-to-end fine-tuning, making it difficult to disentangle the contribution of the learned representations from that of downstream optimization Munk et al. (2026); Moradi et al. (2015). Finally, current generative approaches largely inherit masking strategies and reconstruction objectives originally developed for natural images. Regular cubic masking patterns and voxel-wise reconstruction losses such as Mean Squared Error (MSE) do not explicitly account for the hierarchical and spatially correlated orga-nization of brain anatomy, potentially limiting their ability to learn morphometrically meaningful representations suitable for neurodegenerative disease analysis. We introduce SYMBA ( SY stematic Morphometric Brain Autoencoding), a self-supervised pre-training framework for learning morphometric brain representations tailored to neurodegenerative disease analysis. Rather than directly adopting masked autoencoding strategies developed for nat-ural images, SYMBA introduces an anatomically constrained masking operator that generates geo-metrically diverse, anatomically coherent occlusions over 3D brain volumes. The proposed masking strategy is coupled with a structural reconstruction objective based on the Multi-Scale Structural Similarity (MS-SSIM) loss Wang et al. (2003) and a progressive masking curriculum that gradu-ally shifts the learning task from recovering local anatomical details to modeling large-scale brain morphology. Together, these components encourage the encoder to learn biologically meaningful latent representations of brain morphology that are transferable to downstream neurodegenerative disease analysis. The resulting model is pretrained predominantly on cognitively healthy subjects from multiple large-scale cohorts, enabling learning transferable morphometric representations without relying on disease-specific supervision. We evaluate SYMBA under a challenging transfer learning setting, where representations pre-trained on healthy control populations are transferred to two independent, previously unseen neu-rodegenerative cohorts, including Alzheimer’s disease. Performance is compared against recent self-supervised neuroimaging pretraining methods using strict frozen-representation protocols and low-label regimes, providing a controlled assessment of representation quality, transferability, and 3robustness under distribution shift.

Reconstruction Objective 

Unlike conventional reconstruction objectives, which evaluate similarity over the entire image, SYMBA computes the reconstruction loss exclusively over the voxels removed by the masking operator. This prevents the model from receiving trivial supervision from already visible voxels and large homogeneous background regions, forcing it instead to recover the missing anatomical structures. Unlike voxel-wise losses such as Mean Squared Error, MS-SSIM jointly evaluates luminance, contrast, and structural similarity across multiple spatial resolutions, making it particularly suitable for preserving anatomical morphology in structural MRI. 2.4. Downstream Tasks 

After self-supervised pretraining, the decoder is discarded and only the pretrained encoder fθ

is retained as a generic feature extractor. Given an input sMRI volume, the encoder produces a fixed-dimensional latent representation that serves as a compact description of the underlying brain morphology. These embeddings can subsequently be transferred to a variety of downstream tasks without requiring further representation learning. To evaluate the quality of the learned representations, we adopt a frozen linear probing protocol. Specifically, the encoder parameters remain fixed, and a linear logistic regression classifier is trained on the extracted embeddings for each downstream task. Compared with end-to-end fine-tuning, linear probing isolates the discriminative power of the latent representation from the capacity of the downstream model, providing a more direct assessment of the information captured during self-supervised pretraining. Moreover, its simplicity reduces the influence of optimization strategies and hyperparameter choices, allowing differences in downstream performance to be more confidently attributed to the quality of the learned representation. 7Table 1: Number of samples, country of origin, nature of the diagnosis and metadata of the different databases used

3.2. Experimental setup 

SYMBA was implemented in PyTorch. The encoder fθ was instantiated as a 3D ResNet-50 comprising [3,4,6,3] bottleneck blocks and producing a 2048-dimensional global embedding. The decoder gϕ consisted of five transposed convolutional layers followed by a Sigmoid activation. SYMBA was pre-trained on the healthy-control cohorts described in Section 3.1 together with OASIS, explicitly excluding both ADNI and AIBL from pretraining. Pretraining was performed for 600 epochs, increasing complexity through a curriculum learning technique. The masking ratio increased progressively from 30% to 75%, while the characteristic scale transitioned from s(t) = 16 

during the first 300 epochs to s(t) = 32 during the remaining 300 epochs. The network was optimized using AdamW Loshchilov (2017) with a weight decay of 0.05. We employed a Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts scheduler with a peak learning rate of 1x10 −4,with restarts synchronized with each change in curriculum complexity as done by Yoon and Kang (2025). Training used a batch size of 4, implemented via gradient accumulation. Reconstruction was optimized using the masked-region 3D MS-SSIM loss proposed by Mallick et al. (2024), computed over four scales. Only masked brain regions satisfying ρ ≥ 0.20 were considered, and background voxels were excluded from the loss. We implemented a nested stratified cross-validation for each task and dataset. To provide the final performance measures we defined a 5-fold partition (outer loop), whereas for the hyperparame-ter tuning of the linear probe we defined a 3-folds partition (inner loop) exclusively over the training fold. The inner loop was used to tune the L2 regularization hyperparameter ( C ∈ [10 −4, 10 2]) of a Logistic Regression classifier optimized via the L-BFGS algorithm. Performance was reported as the mean AUROC ± standard deviation of the outer loop. The code of both the proposed SSL approach and the experimental setup is available at https://github.com/nuribal123/SYMBA. However, the datasets used in this study were not hosted 10 in the public repository due to the specific licensing constraints and terms of use associated with each database. 

3.3. Performance evaluation 

To evaluate the transferability of the learnt representations, we compared SYMBA against rep-resentative state-of-the-art large-scale, self-supervised, pre-trained models for sMRI, spanning the main SSL paradigms currently adopted in neuroimaging. Specifically, we evaluated (1) BrainMorph Wang et al. (2025), a geometric representation learning approach based on anatomical keypoint pre-diction for image registration, (2) BrainSegFounder Cox et al. (2024), which performed two-stage pretraining using healthy and tumor MRIs for downstream segmentation, (3) AnatCL Barbano et al. (2026), a contrastive framework exploiting region-of-interest information and (4) BrainIAC Tak et al. (2024), which explored a SimCLR objective. To ensure a fair comparison, all external models were treated as frozen feature extractors and evaluated using the same linear probing pro-tocol. Note that BrainMorph, AnatCL, and BrainIAC were originally pre-trained using ADNI as part of their pretraining datasets, whereas BrainSegFounder and SYMBA were pre-trained with-out any ADNI images. Consequently, while the ADNI evaluation provided a direct comparison with prior work under a common protocol, the AIBL cohort offered the strictest assessment of out-of-distribution generalisation, as none of the evaluated models were pre-trained on this dataset. Low-data regime evaluation 

To evaluate the sample efficiency of the learned representations, we progressively reduced the amount of labelled training data available for downstream learning. For each task, the training set was randomly subsampled to 1 %, 2 %, 8 %, 25 %, 50 %, 75 %, and 100 % of the available participants, while the test partition remained fixed. Each subsampling was repeated 10 times per fold to account for both data subsampling and optimization variability. We compared SYMBA against two categories of baselines. The first was the same backbone trained end-to-end under full supervision from random initialization (no pretraining), following Tak et al. (2026). The second was the set of four publicly available pre-trained neuroimaging models (AnatCL, BrainIAC, BrainMorph, and BrainSegFounder) evaluated under the same frozen-encoder, linear-probe protocol as SYMBA. This second comparison isolated whether SYMBA’s advantage came from pretraining in general or from the specific self-supervised objective proposed here. Figure 3(a) shows that on AD vs CN, Sex, and Amyloid classification, SYMBA consistently outperformed both the supervised baseline and all four alternative large pre-trained models on the ADNI dataset, with the advantage being most notable when only a handful of labelled participants were available. Strikingly, with only five to six labelled samples, SYMBA already outperformed all four large pre-trained models on AD vs CN classification, despite three of them having encountered ADNI itself during their own pretraining. As more labels were added, the supervised baseline catched up, and the two methods converged by the time the full training set was used, whereas the alternative pre-trained models plateaued at a lower level throughout, never closing the gap to either SYMBA or the fully trained supervised model. The sMCI vs pMCI task departed from this pattern. SYMBA still provided the clearest advan-tage in the extreme low-data regime and remained ahead of all four alternative foundation models throughout. 3.5. Ablation study 

To quantify the contribution of each design choice in SYMBA, we performed a controlled abla-tion study along three key axes of the proposed framework: (i) the autoencoder architecture, (ii) the pre-training paradigm, and (iii) the reconstruction objective. In each experiment, we isolated one design axis while keeping the remaining training configuration fixed, enabling us to assess its individual impact on downstream performance. This setup provided a systematic evaluation of how architectural and training choices affect the quality and transferability of the learned representa-tions. 

3.5.1. Autoencoder architecture

While ResNet M used a 3D-ResNet34 architecture with BasicBlock3D layers, ResNet L scaled up to a RD-ResNet50 backbone featuring Bottleneck3D layers. This comparison allowed us to disentangle the effect of network size from that of skip connections, which may facilitate low-level information transfer between encoder and decoder but can also reduce the pressure on the latent representation to capture semantically meaningful features.

18 4. Discussion 

This study investigated whether incorporating anatomical and morphological priors into self-supervised pretraining can improve the transferability of structural brain MRI representations for neurodegenerative disease analysis. The results suggest that the design of the pretraining objective plays an important role in determining the information retained in the learned representations. In particular, the performance of SYMBA across independent cohorts, heterogeneous downstream tasks, and limited-label settings supports the value of reconstruction objectives that encourage the modeling of spatial relationships and global brain morphology. Beyond the overall improvements in predictive performance, our findings provide insights into the relationship between self-supervised objectives, anatomical inductive biases, and downstream representation quality. 

4.1. Learning Morphological Representations for Neurodegenerative Disease Analysis 

An important question in self-supervised neuroimaging is which anatomical properties a rep-resentation should preserve to support subsequent clinical analysis. Unlike tasks such as image registration or segmentation, which emphasize accurate spatial correspondence or local boundary delineation, neurodegenerative disease classification requires identifying structural variations that may be subtle, distributed, and spatially interdependent. This distinction has implications for the design of self-supervised objectives, since representations optimized for one anatomical task may not necessarily retain the information most relevant to another. The differences observed between SYMBA and the evaluated pretrained models are consistent with this interpretation. In contrast to objectives emphasizing instance discrimination, anatomical correspondence, or segmentation, SYMBA requires reconstructing missing tissue from the sur-rounding anatomical context. This encourages the encoder to retain information that supports the recovery of spatial relationships between brain structures, rather than optimizing exclusively for local features or similarity between augmented views. The progressive masking curriculum further introduces reconstruction problems at different spatial scales, potentially promoting representations that capture both localized anatomical variations and larger morphological patterns. The differences across downstream tasks also provide insight into the information captured by these representations. While SYMBA performed well on established diagnostic categories, improve-ments were less pronounced for MCI progression and amyloid status prediction. This distinction is relevant because sMRI provides an indirect characterization of the underlying pathological pro-cesses. Clinical progression and molecular pathology cannot necessarily be inferred from macro-scopic morphology with the same reliability as established disease-related anatomical differences. Consequently, the benefits of improved structural representation learning may be constrained by the information intrinsically available in the imaging modality. 

4.2. Transferability and Label Efficiency 

A central motivation for SSL in neuroimaging is the possibility of learning representations from heterogeneous unlabeled populations and transferring them to clinical cohorts where annotated data are limited. SYMBA was pretrained without access to either ADNI or AIBL, yet its representations supported multiple downstream classification tasks using frozen encoders and linear classifiers. This suggests that the pretrained latent space contains anatomical information that remains useful across populations and acquisition settings without requiring extensive task-specific adaptation. The use of frozen linear probing is particularly relevant to this interpretation. The evaluation reduces the extent to which differences in performance can be attributed to task-specific represen-tation adaptation due to the restriction of downstream learning to a linear classifier. The resulting 19 improvements therefore support the usefulness of the anatomical information encoded during pre-training. Nevertheless, this protocol evaluates linear accessibility of downstream information rather than the full potential of each architecture after fine-tuning. The low-data experiments provide further evidence of this property. SYMBA’s advantage was most pronounced when very few labeled examples were available, suggesting that much of the information needed for downstream discrimination was already accessible in the pretrained feature space. From a practical perspective, this is particularly relevant to neuroimaging applications where obtaining reliable labels requires expensive biomarker measurements, specialist assessments, or prolonged clinical follow-up. However, the different data efficiency curves reveal that the value of self-supervised pretraining is not uniform across tasks. For several ADNI classification problems, supervised models progressively approached the performance of SYMBA as more labeled examples became available. For MCI progression prediction, the supervised baseline eventually surpassed the frozen representations. One possible explanation is that pretraining captures broadly useful anatomical characteristics, whereas distinguishing stable from progressive MCI may benefit from learning more specific combinations of structural features that are not linearly accessible in the pretrained space. These observations suggest a complementary relationship between general-purpose pretraining and task-specific optimization. Frozen representations may be particularly attractive in severely data-constrained settings, whereas fine-tuning could become beneficial when larger annotated co-horts are available or when the prediction target requires more specialized information. Investigat-ing this trade-off would help determine when the additional complexity of end-to-end adaptation is justified. 

4.3. Anatomical Inductive Biases and Representation Quality 

The ablation experiments offer additional insight into how architectural and methodological decisions influence representation learning. An important observation is that improvements do not arise uniformly from increasing architectural complexity or imposing additional anatomical constraints. Instead, the findings suggest a trade-off between preserving detailed spatial information and encouraging the encoder to learn compact representations of global brain morphology. This trade-off is illustrated by the comparison between residual autoencoders and U-Net archi-tectures. Although skip connections can facilitate reconstruction by transferring spatial information directly between encoder and decoder, they also provide pathways that bypass the latent bottle-neck. In a representation-learning setting, this may reduce the need for the encoder to retain all the information required for reconstruction. The stronger performance of residual architectures on several disease-related tasks is consistent with this hypothesis, although the competitive perfor-mance of U-Net on other endpoints indicates that preserving fine-grained spatial detail can also be beneficial. Importantly, these findings do not establish that skip connections are universally detrimental, but rather suggest that reconstruction fidelity and downstream representation quality are not necessarily aligned. The masking ablations reinforce the importance of the reconstruction problem presented to the encoder. The benefits associated with geometrically structured masking suggest that the spatial organization of missing information influences which anatomical dependencies the network learns. However, the less consistent contribution of additional masking constraints indicates that anatomi-cal priors should not be assumed to be beneficial simply because they encode biological knowledge. Their usefulness depends on whether they encourage the model to preserve information relevant to 20 the downstream task without unnecessarily restricting the variability encountered during pretrain-ing. A similar interpretation applies to the reconstruction objective. Whereas voxel-wise losses pri-oritize local intensity agreement, structural similarity objectives evaluate relationships involving local contrast and spatial organization. The overall performance of MS-SSIM is consistent with the hypothesis that multiscale structural reconstruction provides useful supervision for morphometric representation learning. Nevertheless, the competitive results obtained with MSE and single-scale SSIM on certain tasks indicate that different reconstruction criteria may favor different aspects of anatomical information. This raises the possibility that reconstruction objectives could be adapted to the characteristics of the intended downstream application rather than selected solely according to reconstruction accuracy. Taken together, these findings suggest that effective self-supervised learning for structural neu-roimaging requires balancing anatomical detail, spatial context, and latent compression. Rather than maximizing architectural capacity or reconstruction fidelity independently, the objective should be to encourage representations that retain informative morphological variation while remaining transferable across datasets and tasks. 

4.4. Limitations and Future Directions 

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although the evaluation included two independent cohorts excluded from SYMBA pretraining, the experiments do not explicitly isolate the effects of scanner characteristics, acquisition protocols, or population differences. Furthermore, some competing models were pretrained using data from the evaluation cohorts, complicating direct comparisons of their exposure to the target distributions. Additional experiments involving site-stratified evaluation or controlled acquisition shifts would be necessary to characterize robustness to these factors more precisely. Second, downstream evaluation relied primarily on frozen linear probing. While this provides a controlled means of assessing representation quality, it does not establish the maximum predictive performance achievable through adaptation. The results for MCI progression prediction particularly motivate investigating whether partial fine-tuning or parameter-efficient adaptation can complement the pretrained representations. Third, the present framework is restricted to structural gray matter maps derived from T1-weighted MRI. Although these images provide valuable information about neurodegeneration, they cannot capture all aspects of disease pathology. Integrating complementary imaging modalities or clinical information could help address prediction problems in which morphological information alone is insufficient, particularly molecular biomarker estimation and early disease progression. Finally, the analysis uses baseline structural images, without explicitly modeling within-subject changes over time. Although the proposed framework learns representations associated with subse-quent clinical progression, it does not directly characterize longitudinal neurodegenerative trajec-tories. Extending anatomical self-supervision to repeated observations could provide a more direct means of learning how structural changes evolve, provided suitable longitudinal data are available. Overall, this work supports anatomically informed reconstruction as a promising strategy for self-supervised structural MRI representation learning. More broadly, the findings emphasize that pretraining objectives should be designed around the anatomical information relevant to the in-tended downstream applications, while recognizing the trade-offs between general-purpose trans-ferability and task-specific adaptation. 21 5. Conclusions 

In this work, we presented SYMBA, a self-supervised pretraining framework systematically optimized for structural brain MRI analysis, demonstrating how neuroanatomical inductive biases yield highly generalizable representations tailored for neurodegenerative disease analysis. Through a rigorous evaluation pipeline, we demonstrate that anatomically-informed generative pretraining produces more transferable representations than existing SSL approaches for sMRI. Rather than adapting objectives originally developed for natural images, SYMBA incorporates neuroanatomical priors into the masking strategy, reconstruction objective, and training curriculum, encouraging the encoder to model structural brain organization across multiple spatial scales. The deliberate methodological choice of using linear probing instead of fine-tuning allowed for a rigorous evaluation of the intrinsic quality of the learned latent space, removing the confound-ing variables and artifacts typically introduced by complex fine-tuning schedulers. Despite this conservative evaluation protocol, SYMBA demonstrated superior performance and out-of-domain generalizability across a diverse array of clinical tasks when evaluated on two independent and unseen external datasets, ADNI and AIBL. The clinical applicability of this approach is more notable in our low-data regime evaluations. In extremely low-data settings, SYMBA achieved an AUROC close to 0.90 for Alzheimer’s disease classification using only six training samples (1% of the training data), drastically outperforming fully supervised baselines trained from scratch. This result indicates that much of the disease-discriminative information is already encoded during pretraining, substantially reducing the depen-dence on large annotated cohorts. Our findings also suggest that for structural MRI the explicit adaptation of the SSL objective to the spatial organization of brain morphology is critical. Future work will investigate parameter-efficient adaptation strategies, multimodal downstream tasks, and transfer to rarer neurological disorders where labeled data remain extremely limited.
