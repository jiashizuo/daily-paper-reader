# How Reliable Is Individualized Functional-Network Attribution in Resting-State fMRI Cognitive Prediction in ADNI? 

## A Scan–Rescan Evaluation

Abstract. Individualized attribution estimates which functional networks drive a model prediction for a given subject and is often proposed as an interpretable resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) biomarker, but its test–retest re-liability is rarely quantified. We evaluate per-subject network-ablation at-tribution for a frozen resting-state functional connectivity (FC) predictor (Schaefer-200 FC predicting MMSE/ADAS-Cog13) trained on 𝑁 =875 sub-jects from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), using (i) within-session split-half, (ii) scan–rescan repeat visits (74 subjects, 205 baseline–repeat pairs; median inter-visit interval 24.4 months), and (iii) a subject-level permutation null. Split-half reliability is near-ceiling (Spear-man 𝜌 =0.948/0 .950) but drops on scan–rescan ( 𝜌 =0.609/0 .616). Under the mismatched-subject null, correlations remain substantial ( 𝜌 ≈0.46–0 .47), in-dicating strong population-shared structure, yet matched pairs exceed this null ( 𝑧 ≈6.6–6 .9, 𝑝 ≤ 10 −4). Thus, in ADNI, individualized network attribu-tion contains a modest but non-zero person-specific component atop a strong population-shared pattern. We recommend reporting scan–rescan reliability together with a permutation-null control, rather than within-session proxies alone, to avoid overstating individual specificity. 

## 1 Introduction 

Models that map resting-state functional connectivity (FC) to cognition and clinical phenotypes are increasingly used in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) neuroimaging [1, 2]. Beyond group-level accuracy, a common objective is individualized attribution : for a given subject, estimating which functional networks drive that subject’s prediction. Such per-subject attribution is only meaningful if it is reproducible. Reliability is a central constraint. Large-scale analyses suggest that stable brain– behavior effects often require much larger samples than historically used [3], and arXiv:2610.37472v1 [cs.CV] 27 Oct 2026 2 Bhat et al. 

FC test–retest reliability is frequently modest (pooled mean edge-level Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC ≈ 0.29) [4]. The ICC describes how reproducibly a single connectivity edge is measured. Prior work on subject-specific parcellations reports reproducibility across short-interval repeat sessions [5, 6], but does not quantify test–retest reliability of model-derived attribution signatures over the multi-year intervals typical of aging cohorts. An additional aspect is attribution stability : feature-importance maps can vary substantially under resampling, noise, and other perturbations even when predictive accuracy is similar [7, 8]. In FC prediction, however, attribution work has largely em-phasized group-averaged importance. For connectomes rather than attributions, dif-ferential identifiability formalizes the person-specific, test–retest-identifiable compo-nent [9], and permutation-null models are standard for separating population-shared from person-specific structure [10, 11]. Neither has been applied to per-subject attribution signatures. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work combines per-subject attribution of an FC-based cognitive predictor with a quantitative test–retest evaluation on independent repeat scans of the same individuals. We address this gap using a frozen FC-based multilayer perceptron (FC-MLP) trained to jointly predict MMSE and ADAS-Cog13 from Schaefer-200 FC on 𝑁 =875 ADNI subjects (Sect. 2). Instead of edge-level ICC, we assess test–retest consistency of each subject’s network-attribution ranking. We compute per-subject network-ablation signatures and evaluate reproducibility with three checks: within-session split-half, scan–rescan across independent repeat visits, and a cluster-aware subject-level permutation null. The main contributions in this study are: 1. Quantify how split-half overestimates scan–rescan reliability of per-subject net-work attribution (74 subjects; 205 session pairs). 2. Use a subject-level permutation null to estimate the mismatched-subject baseline and the residual person-specific component. 3. Provide reporting recommendations to avoid overstating individual specificity.

## 3 Experimental details 

3.1 Data description 

ADNI is a multi-site, longitudinal study of aging and Alzheimer’s disease with multi-modal neuroimaging [13]. We analyzed data from 𝑁 =875 unique ADNI participants who had both T1-weighted MRI and rs-fMRI scans. Within the 𝑁 =875 cohort, diag-noses are CN ( 𝑛 =464), MCI ( 𝑛 =315), Dementia ( 𝑛 =94), and unknown ( 𝑛 =2). Age is 72 .6 ± 7.9 years (range 50–97); 489 subjects are female and 386 male. Subjects were enrolled in ADNI1 ( 𝑛 =37), ADNI-GO ( 𝑛 =38), ADNI2 ( 𝑛 =256), ADNI3 ( 𝑛 =474), and ADNI4 ( 𝑛 =68); 2 unknown. rs-fMRI scans were acquired at 3.0 T across 53 sites with heterogeneous protocols (TR 0.607–6.02 s; 96–972 volumes after removal of initial volumes). For the upstream prediction model, the cohort is split once at the subject level into fixed train/validation/test partitions of 611/132/132 stratified by diagnosis. We evaluate attribution and reliability only on the held-out 132-subject test split; scan–rescan further restricts to test subjects with repeat-visit rs-fMRI. 

3.2 Implementation details 

We analyze a trained FC-MLP and keep its weights fixed for all attribution and reliability analyses. 

Input features. rs-fMRI preprocessing includes motion correction, HD-BET brain extraction [14], rigid rs-fMRI →T1 registration, detrending, and nuisance regression of mean white-matter (WM) and cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF) signals plus 5 anatomical CompCor (aCompCor) components [15] from a combined WM/CSF mask, without global signal regression. Time series are band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz, using each 6 Bhat et al. 

subject’s TR), 𝑧 -scored, and scrubbed at framewise displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm after filtering; FD was computed from translation magnitude only rather than the full six-parameter formulation [16], as rotation parameters were not retained upstream. No spatial smoothing or slice-timing correction is applied. We compute Schaefer-200 [17] FC as a Fisher-𝑧 transformed 200 × 200 Pearson correlation matrix and vectorize the upper triangle (19,900 edges). An edge-coverage mask is concatenated, yielding a 39,800-dimensional input. 

Model and training (frozen). The FC-MLP encoder maps 39 ,800 → 256 → 128 with LayerNorm/GELU/dropout (0.3), and the decoder maps 128 → 64 → 2 with GELU/dropout (0.3). The model is trained with Huber loss ( 𝛿 =1.0) using Adam (lr =

10 −4, weight decay 10 −5; no schedule/warmup), batch size 16, and up to 100 epochs. Early stopping uses patience 15 on validation loss; we keep the single best checkpoint (lowest validation loss). During training only, an augmentation zeros 5 randomly chosen parcels per sample. Targets are 𝑧 -scored on train only and inverse-transformed for reporting. Three independent runs with different random initializations share the same fixed split. ComBat harmonization [18] uses the TR group as batch variable (a proxy for acquisition protocol) and protects diagnosis, whose composition differs across TR groups ( 𝜒 2=35 .66, 𝑝 < 0.001); no other covariates are included. It is fit on the training split only, never refit, and applied to validation/test subjects and split-half halves, but not to repeat-visit sessions (both sides of scan–rescan use pre-ComBat features). 

3.3 Results 

The frozen predictor is weak: on the 𝑛 =113 test subjects with both targets, 𝑅 2=0.053 ±

0.034 for MMSE and 0 .087 ± 0.037 for ADAS-Cog13 (mean ± SD over runs). As a diagnostic check, the predicted ADAS-Cog13 score separates CN from Dementia with AUC 0 .780 (95 % bootstrap CI 0.611–0.911; 𝑛 = 72 of the 113 subjects with both targets: 58 CN, 14 Dementia). Within-session split-half reliability is near-ceiling for both targets (mean

## 4 Discussion 

Per-subject attribution reliability depends strongly on protocol: split-half is near-ceiling ( 𝜌 ≈0.95), but drops on scan–rescan across independent repeat visits ( 𝜌 ≈0.61– 0.62), consistent with additional between-visit variability beyond within-session sampling noise. The permutation null suggests that much of this structure is population-shared, with only a modest person-specific excess; robustness to other cohorts, preprocessing, parcellations, attribution operators, and models remains untested. The coexistence of shared and person-specific structure mirrors FC itself, where connectomes are identifiable across sessions [9, 10] yet test–retest reliability is mod-est [4]. The strong shared profile likely has two sources. First, the ablations overlap by construction: every between-network edge (𝑖, 𝑗 ) with 𝑖 ∈ 𝑛 , 𝑗 ∈ 𝑚 is zeroed in both 𝑥 \𝑛 and 𝑥 \𝑚 , coupling 𝐼 (𝑡 ) 

> 𝑛

and 𝐼 (𝑡 ) 

> 𝑚

; within-network-only ablation would remove this overlap. Second, the weak predictor may respond mainly to group-level FC structure. Because 𝜌 is bounded above, a high shared baseline leaves little room for an individual excess, so the person-specific component may be underestimated. Limitations include the modest repeat-visit sample (74 subjects; 205 session pairs) and the weak, run-variable predictor (Sect. 3). Repeat-visit sessions were not ComBat-harmonized, so part of the split-half to scan–rescan gap may reflect this mismatch. Inter-visit intervals are long and variable (median 24.4 months, IQR 12.2–48.4) and are not modeled. We therefore recommend treating within-session proxies as expected upper bounds, reporting scan–rescan estimates when repeat visits exist, and including a permutation-null (or equivalent) control; these recommendations should be validated in further aging/AD cohorts such as OASIS-3 and AIBL. In summary, per-subject network attributions are far less reproducible across visits than within-session esti-mates suggest, and most of their apparent stability reflects a pattern shared across the cohort, so individual specificity should be demonstrated against an explicit pop-ulation baseline before attributions are interpreted as personal biomarkers.
