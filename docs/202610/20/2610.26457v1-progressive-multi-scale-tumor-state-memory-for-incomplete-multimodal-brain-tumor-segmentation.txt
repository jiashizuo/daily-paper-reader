# Progressive multi-scale tumor-state memory for incomplete multimodal brain tumor segmentation 

Milad Rostamian a, Mohammadreza Mohammadi a, Mohsen Soryani a, ∗

> aSchool of Computer Engineering, Iran University of Science and Technology (IUST), Hengam Street, Resalat Square, 13114-16846, Tehran, Iran

Abstract 

Brain tumor segmentation uses complementary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences, but some sequences may be unavailable in practice. Their absence changes the information available for segmenting different tumor re-gions. Fusion methods can support different input subsets. However, fusion at several scales does not by itself specify how tumor evidence is retained and updated to guide fusion at later stages. We propose a progressive multi-scale tumor-state memory network (PMTM) for segmentation from any non-empty subset of T1ce, T1, FLAIR, and T2. PMTM maintains a compact state for the current subject across the encoder stages. At each stage, we use assign-ment maps to pool spatial features into ordered evidence vectors for each available modality. We average these vectors across the available modalities, and a learned gate combines this evidence with the previous state. The up-dated state guides residual fusion at that stage and is used in the state update at the next stage. A modality-token mixer also models interactions among the available modalities at each stage. We apply semantic supervision to the assignment maps at all four stages. We evaluate PMTM on BraTS2018, BraTS2020, and BraTS2023 GLI across all 15 non-empty modality subsets. Averaged over the three tumor regions and the 15 subsets, PMTM achieves Dice scores of 75.31%, 77.54%, and 80.47% on BraTS2018, BraTS2020, and BraTS2023 GLI, respectively. These scores are higher than those of Robust-Seg, RFNet, and mmFormer in our experiments on each dataset. Component ablations and internal state analysis are performed on BraTS2020. 

Keywords: Brain tumor segmentation, Incomplete multimodal learning, Missing MRI modalities, Multimodal fusion, Tumor-state memory

1. Introduction 

Glioma segmentation on MRI supports diagnosis and treatment planning [1]. The tumor regions considered here are the whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET) [2, 3]. To identify these regions, multimodal brain MRI commonly combines contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1ce), T1-weighted (T1), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and T2-weighted (T2) images [2, 3]. These sequences provide complementary information about the tumor regions. T1ce is especially informative for TC and ET, while FLAIR and T2 provide information about edema and WT [4, 5]. Many multimodal segmentation models are designed to receive a com-plete set of MRI sequences [6]. However, differences in acquisition protocols, image corruption, resource constraints, and restrictions on contrast admin-istration can leave one or more sequences unavailable [6]. The resulting loss of information affects tumor regions differently [4, 5]. Segmentation from incomplete inputs therefore requires a fusion mechanism that works with the available subset and accounts for the information supplied by each remaining sequence. Existing approaches address this problem in different ways. Latent aggre-gation constructs a common representation from different modality subsets [7, 8]. Region-aware fusion accounts for how each sequence contributes to in-dividual tumor regions [4]. Transformer modules learn interactions between modality representations [9, 10]. Our earlier method, TG-BWSA, exchanges modality features between encoder stages through a shared adapter [11]. These mechanisms determine how the available features are combined. Arelated question is how evidence about tumor regions can be accumulated across encoder stages and used to guide fusion at later stages. Memory mechanisms reuse information during feature extraction and de-coding [12, 13]. In multimodal brain tumor segmentation, MSM-Seg uses memory to model relationships between modalities and across slices [14]. These studies motivate the use of retained context. The role of memory de-pends on what is stored and where the network uses it. Here, we investigate a tumor state built from the available MRI sequences that accumulates tumor evidence across encoder stages and directly guides modality fusion. We propose PMTM, a progressive multi-scale tumor-state memory net-work. PMTM maintains a tumor state, an ordered set of feature vectors called state slots. At each encoder stage, assignment maps pool features 2from each available modality into evidence vectors. We average correspond-ing evidence vectors across the available modalities and use them to update the state. The updated state guides fusion at the current stage and is used in the state update at the next stage. Fusion uses the current modality features together with the state accumulated from preceding stages. At the begin-ning of each forward pass, the state is initialized from a learned tensor. State updates use evidence from the available sequences of the current subject. The contributions of this work are summarized below. 1. We introduce a progressive tumor-state path for segmentation from in-complete multimodal MRI. Ordered state slots accumulate evidence from the available MRI sequences across all four encoder stages. 2. We apply semantic supervision to the assignment maps at all four encoder stages. 3. We introduce residual fusion guided by the updated tumor state. Amodality-token mixer also models interactions among the available modal-ities at each encoder stage. 4. We evaluate PMTM on BraTS2018, BraTS2020, and BraTS2023 GLI across all 15 non-empty modality subsets. Component ablations on BraTS2020 assess the tumor-state path, cross-stage state propagation, semantic supervision, and modality-token interaction. Internal analyses examine the learned assignment maps and state slots. 

2. Related work 

2.1. Fusion in complete multimodal brain tumor segmentation 

Methods for complete inputs differ in when MRI sequences are combined. Pereira et al. [15] processed the four sequences jointly with a patch-based CNN. Separate encoders allow fusion to adjust modality contributions after feature extraction. Zhou et al. [16] use attention and tumor-region context constraints, while Tri-Attention [17] weights modality and spatial features and models correlations between sequences. Transformer fusion models dependencies between modality representa-tions through attention. NestedFormer [18] uses intra-modality and cross-modality attention at higher levels and modality-sensitive gating at lower scales. Zhou et al. [19] combine modality-wise, spatial, and channel-wise 3feature fusion. These designs adjust how features are combined for com-plete inputs. With missing sequences, fusion must also accept different input subsets. 

2.2. Incomplete multimodal brain tumor segmentation 

Supporting different subsets first requires a representation that can be constructed from the available inputs. HeMIS [7] aggregates modality-specific features through their mean and variance. U-HVED [8] instead combines distributions inferred from the available modalities in a probabilis-tic latent space for joint segmentation and modality completion. Beyond accepting different subsets, fusion can also adjust the contribution of the remaining modalities. RobustSeg [20] separates shared and modality-specific representations and applies gated fusion. RFNet [4] makes fusion region-aware, accounting for differences in the information each sequence provides about each tumor region. D 2-Net [21] models modality and region relationships through dual disentanglement. Training can provide additional information for incomplete inputs. ACN [22] couples complete and incomplete configurations through adversarial co-training and representation alignment. LCKD [23] selects teacher modalities according to task performance, so supervision can differ between targets. W. Liu et al. [24] use complete-input predictions to guide partial-input pre-dictions during post-training. Self-supervised learning (SSL) addresses feature learning before super-vised segmentation. M3AE [25] reconstructs masked modalities and image patches during pretraining. It also uses model inversion for modality com-pletion and self-distillation during segmentation training. M3AE combines pretraining with knowledge transfer. UniME [26] combines a shared ViT pretrained through masked image modeling with multi-scale features from modality-specific CNN encoders. The pretrained representation provides global context, while the CNN encoders supply features at higher spatial resolutions. Within the network, interaction modules determine how features ex-change information. mmFormer [9] uses intra-modal and inter-modal trans-formers, while M 2FTrans [10] incorporates modality masking into trans-former fusion to handle different missing patterns. Feature interaction is also modeled with state-space blocks. LS 3M [27] combines differentiable se-quence reordering with state-space blocks for local and long-range dependen-cies. CDMS [28] uses learnable consistency features and fusion at multiple 4scales. LS 3M focuses on spatial ordering, while CDMS incorporates consis-tency information into fusion. Fusion can also receive conditioning information. MIFPN [29] de-rives prompts from modality-invariant features. MPDF-UNET [5] combines learned modality priors with input-dependent features and changes modality-combination sampling during training. MGML [30] uses meta-parameterized fusion to construct soft targets from available modalities, together with consistency regularization. TG-BWSA combines compressed modality features in a shared adapter and returns adapted features to each encoder stream [11]. It learns two scalar weights per modality and stage to combine the original and adapted features. These scalar weights remain fixed across test inputs. PMTM instead updates a tumor state from the available modalities and uses that state to guide fusion. Alongside these fusion mechanisms, we examine how an explicit tumor state can accumulate evidence across encoder stages and guide fusion for different available modality subsets. This connects incomplete multimodal fusion to memory mechanisms in segmentation. 

2.3. Memory mechanisms in medical image segmentation 

Memory provides context whose role depends on what is stored and where it is reused. Memory-Net [12] propagates cell memories through hi-erarchically stacked gated convolutional units and across PET/CT encoder branches. HMDM-Net [13] retains deep semantic representations across de-coder resolutions. Memory-Net reuses intermediate information during fea-ture extraction, while HMDM-Net combines deep and shallow features during decoding. Memory can also retain information across examples. Sim-MPNet [31] updates a prototype bank of category features associated with lesions or or-gans during training. The selective memory network [32] stores and retrieves representative global features across samples for 3D MRI segmentation. Both provide category or global context beyond the features of the current input. In multimodal brain tumor segmentation, MSM-Seg [14] organizes memory around modality and slice relationships. Its memory attention models corre-lations between modalities and dependencies across slices. The method also uses category-agnostic prompting and a modality-adaptive fusion decoder. PMTM builds an ordered tumor state from the available sequences of the current subject, updates it across encoder stages, and uses it to guide 5modality fusion at each stage. 

3. Method 

In this work, we develop PMTM for brain tumor segmentation from in-complete multimodal MRI. We first describe the overall network architecture, followed by the progressive tumor-state memory and state-guided fusion with modality-token interaction. We then present the modality sampling proce-dure and training objectives. 

3.1. Network overview 

Figure 1 shows the overall architecture. PMTM has four modality-specific encoders and a shared segmentation decoder. The encoders have the same architecture but independent parameters. Each encoder has four stages that produce feature maps at different spatial scales. For the current subject, PMTM maintains a tumor state that stores evi-dence from the available MRI sequences. At each encoder stage, the available modality features update this state. Each stage has a fusion module with state-guided fusion and a modality-token mixer. The updated state guides feature fusion, and the mixer models interactions among the available modal-ities. The shared decoder combines the fused features from all four stages and predicts four segmentation classes. During training, each modality branch also has an auxiliary decoder with the same architecture as the shared decoder but independent parameters. It takes the deepest features of its own encoder as input and uses the shal-lower scales through skip connections. Its prediction supervises that modality branch directly. The auxiliary decoders are discarded at inference. Let M = (T1ce , T1 , FLAIR , T2) denote the ordered set of M = 4MRI modalities, and let S ⊆ M denote the non-empty subset avail-able for a subject. 4. Experiments 

We evaluate PMTM on BraTS2018, BraTS2020, and BraTS2023 GLI across all 15 non-empty modality subsets. We first describe the datasets, evaluation protocol, and implementation details. We then compare PMTM with RobustSeg, RFNet, and mmFormer trained in this study and with pub-lished BraTS2020 and BraTS2023 results [27, 30]. We also report component ablations, analyses of assignment maps and tumor states, and segmentation maps under complete and incomplete inputs. 14 4.1. Datasets and evaluation protocol 

We use BraTS2018 and BraTS2020 [2, 3, 35], and the adult glioma task of the BraTS 2023 Challenge (BraTS2023 GLI) [36–38]. The train-ing/validation/test splits contain 205/23/57, 219/50/100, and 875/125/251 subjects, respectively. For BraTS2020, we use the public subject lists from the official M 2FTrans repository [10]; for BraTS2023 GLI, we use the public IM-Fuse subject lists [39]. Within each dataset, all methods trained in this study and component ablations use the same fixed subject-level partitions. Published results retain the training and evaluation protocols reported in their source studies. We evaluate Dice for WT, TC, and ET under each of the 15 non-empty subsets of T1ce, T1, FLAIR, and T2. When the predicted and ground-truth regions are both empty, Dice is set to one. When only one is empty, Dice is set to zero. The overall score is Dice averaged equally over test subjects, the three tumor regions, and all 15 modality subsets. Dice scores are reported as percentages, and differences between scores are reported in percentage points. 

4.2. Implementation details 

Each volume is cropped to a common foreground bounding box defined by the nonzero voxels of the four MRI sequences. The box is expanded to at least 128 voxels along each axis when necessary. Each sequence is standardized over its foreground voxels, with background values kept at zero. PMTM training uses random 128 3 crops, rotations up to 10 ◦, intensity scaling for each modality in [0 .9, 1.1], and intensity shifts in [ −0.1, 0.1]. Each spatial axis is independently flipped with probability 0.5. Rotations use linear interpolation for MRI volumes and nearest-neighbor interpolation for segmentation labels. The four encoder stages have channel widths of 16, 32, 64, and 128. Each stage contains three 3 × 3 × 3 convolutional blocks with instance normal-ization and LeakyReLU. The first block of stages 2–4 uses stride two. The decoder uses trilinear upsampling and skip concatenation. Its convolutional refinement layers have channel widths of 64, 32, and 16. A 1 × 1 × 1 convolu-tion maps the final 16 feature channels to four class logits. Softmax converts these logits to voxel-wise class probabilities. The auxiliary decoders use the same architecture, as described in Section 3. The assignment branch Θ s uses a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution, instance nor-malization, and LeakyReLU, followed by a second 1 × 1 × 1 convolution 15 that produces K logits. The evidence branch Ψ s uses a 1 × 1 × 1 convolu-tion followed by instance normalization and LeakyReLU to produce d = 64 evidence channels. The modality-token mixer uses a single transformer en-coder block with four attention heads, a feed-forward width of max(2 Cs, 64), GELU activation, pre-layer normalization, and zero dropout. Layer normal-ization is applied before the attention and feed-forward sublayers (Pre-LN). All LeakyReLU activations have a negative slope of 0.2. PMTM is implemented in PyTorch and trained using Adam with AMS-Grad. The initial learning rate is 10 −4 and follows polynomial decay with power 0.9. Weight decay is 10 −4, and the effective batch size is two. PMTM experiments use one NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48 GB memory. In the experiments conducted in this study, all four methods are trained for 1000 epochs on BraTS2018 and BraTS2020 and for 200 epochs on BraTS2023 GLI. We select the checkpoint with the highest average validation Dice across all 15 modality subsets. The comparisons use method-specific crop sizes of 80 3 for RobustSeg and RFNet and 128 3 for mmFormer and PMTM. The sizes for the comparison methods follow their original input configurations. For the objective in Section 3.4, we use λregion = 0 .35 and λstate = 0 .05. The region weights ( ωWT , ω TC , ω ET ) are (0 .20 , 0.30 , 0.50). Focal Tversky loss uses αFT = 0 .3 for false positives, βFT = 0 .7 for false negatives, and γFT =0.75. The loss is (1 − TI) γFT , where TI is the soft Tversky index. The auxiliary region multiplier is ρaux = 0 .15, so ρaux λregion = 0 .0525. The semantic class weights are (0 .25 , 1, 1, 1) for BG, NCR/NET, ED, and ET. The auxiliary coefficient is 0.5 during the first 30% of training. It decreases linearly to 0.2 by 70% and then decreases linearly to 0.1 at the final epoch. PMTM inference uses 128 3 sliding windows with 50% overlap. The tu-mor state is initialized from T 0 separately for each sliding window. Class probabilities are averaged in overlapping regions before applying argmax at each voxel. No test-time augmentation is applied.

4.5. Semantic assignment and tumor-state dynamics 

The ablations measure changes in segmentation performance when com-ponents are removed. We next examine the assignment maps and state slots to describe the representations learned by PMTM across encoder stages. 

4.5.1. Analysis protocol 

We use the selected checkpoint to perform the internal state analysis on all 50 subjects in the BraTS2020 validation partition. The analysis includes complete input, FLAIR-only input, and T2-only input. State statistics are 24 computed only for sliding windows containing at least one ground-truth tu-mor voxel. At each of the four stages, we upsample the assignment maps using trilin-ear interpolation. We then average them over the overlapping windows used for segmentation. Applying argmax at each voxel gives hard assignment la-bels. WT combines NCR/NET, ED, and ET. TC combines NCR/NET and ET, while ET uses the enhancing class. We compute Dice for these three re-gions. We also measure the mean activation of each assignment channel for voxels belonging to each ground-truth class. Normalized entropy and mean maximum assignment probability describe how concentrated the assignments are. We measure mean cosine similarity between different state slots within each stage. To examine slot correspondence across stages, we use stage 1 as the reference for stages 2–4. We report diagonal matching and a similarity margin. This margin is the similarity to the corresponding reference slot minus the largest similarity to a different reference slot. We also measure the relative magnitude of state updates. Finally, we compare corresponding slots obtained with complete and incomplete input using cosine similarity.

5. Discussion 

In our experiments, the gains over mmFormer are larger for TC and ET than for WT. However, segmentation performance still depends on the available MRI sequences. In BraTS2020, removing T1ce from the complete input mainly reduces TC and ET Dice. Removing FLAIR mainly reduces WT Dice. These observations are consistent with the different information supplied by the sequences [4, 5]. The memory can combine evidence from the remaining inputs, but the results do not establish that it recovers all information from a missing sequence. The local state memory ablation retains state construction and state-guided fusion but removes propagation between stages. Its lower Dice sug-gests that evidence accumulated at earlier stages contributes to segmenta-tion within this architecture. Removing semantic assignment supervision produces a larger decrease than using local state memory. Together, these comparisons support progressive state updates and supervised assignments in the evaluated BraTS2020 configuration. They do not establish how these components would interact in other architectures or under different training settings. The assignment channels show preferences for their intended classes, but the NCR/NET and ET responses overlap. This overlap limits their inter-pretation as exclusive representations of individual tissue classes. Preserved slot correspondence across stages is consistent with an ordered state repre-sentation. The high cosine similarity between states obtained with complete input and with FLAIR-only or T2-only input indicates similar vector direc-tions. It does not establish equivalent information content or segmentation performance. 

5.1. Limitations 

We evaluate PMTM using fixed subject-level splits within three BraTS cohorts. The experiments do not measure sensitivity to alternative splits or establish performance on external imaging protocols. We do not report 28 variability across training seeds. Our experiments compare PMTM with RobustSeg, RFNet, and mmFormer. Additional methods are compared using published scores; these methods were not retrained in our experimental setup. RobustSeg and RFNet use 80 3 crops, while mmFormer and PMTM use 128 3 crops. The comparisons with RobustSeg and RFNet therefore do not separate differences in architecture from differences in input crop size. The comparison with mmFormer uses the same crop size. Internal state analysis includes all 50 BraTS2020 validation subjects un-der complete, FLAIR-only, and T2-only input. The observed assignment preferences and slot correspondence should be interpreted within these set-tings. The experiments evaluate missing-modality subsets. They do not es-tablish performance under other acquisition failures or on other tumor types. 

6. Conclusion 

We developed PMTM for brain tumor segmentation from incomplete mul-timodal MRI. The method accumulates tumor evidence across encoder stages and uses the updated state to guide modality fusion. We evaluated PMTM on BraTS2018, BraTS2020, and BraTS2023 GLI across all 15 non-empty modal-ity subsets. Dice averaged over the three tumor regions and all 15 subsets is 75.31% on BraTS2018, 77.54% on BraTS2020, and 80.47% on BraTS2023 GLI. These averages are higher than those of RobustSeg, RFNet, and mm-Former in our experiments on each dataset. The BraTS2020 ablations support the contributions of the tumor-state path, cross-stage propagation, and semantic assignment supervision within the evaluated configuration. Removing modality-token interaction gives the smallest average Dice decrease among the tested variants, at 0.26 percent-age points. Internal analysis shows that assignments become more concen-trated across stages and that state slots retain their correspondence. The NCR/NET and ET assignment responses still overlap.
