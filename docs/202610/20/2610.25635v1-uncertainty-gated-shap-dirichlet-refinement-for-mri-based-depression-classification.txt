## Abstract 

Structural MRI-based classification of major depressive disorder (MDD) remains challenging due to subtle, heterogeneous anatomi-cal changes. We propose uncertainty-gated SHAP-Dirichlet refine-ment, a post-hoc reliability layer that improves predictions without retraining the classifier. The method retrieves similar labeled SHAP explanations, constructs Dirichlet class evidence, and selectively refines uncertain predictions through probabilistic fusion, while allowing unreliable cases to be deferred for expert review. We eval-uate five classifiers on 504 SRPBS subjects (252 controls and 252 MDD patients) using 159 anatomical features and repeated nested cross-validation. Refinement improved accuracy across all 8 repeti-tions for TabM and ResNet, yielding mean gains of +1.04 and +0.45 percentage points (sign test 𝑝 = 0.008), while modifying at most 8.3% of predictions. Gains were concentrated among low-confidence cases, with significant improvements in calibration. The method also benefited an RBF support vector machine but not logistic re-gression or random forests. Ablations confirmed the importance of uncertainty gating and soft fusion. Dirichlet evidence identi-fied baseline errors (AUROC 0.62), while deferring 50% of cases increased retained-case accuracy to approximately 75%. These find-ings support selective explanation-guided refinement as a practical approach to improving prediction reliability and facilitating expert triage in MDD classification.

## 1 Introduction 

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous psychiatric disorder whose diagnosis is based on clinical symptoms rather than a single biological marker [31]. Structural magnetic resonance imag-ing (MRI) provides non-invasive measurements of brain morphol-ogy, and large consortia have reported MDD-related differences

in cortical thickness, surface area, and subcortical volume [38]. These effects are small and heterogeneous, and they are sensitive to acquisition, preprocessing, site composition, and diagnostic la-beling [15, 45]. As a result, machine learning studies of depression report a wide range of accuracies [26, 41], and small samples tend to overestimate accuracy [14]. With a few hundred subjects, cross-validated accuracy also has large error bars [43], so a single split can overstate or understate the effect of any modeling choice. For clinical decision support, average accuracy is not sufficient. A classifier may assign a confident label to a subject whose local feature pattern is unstable, weakly supported by similar cases, or inconsistent with the evidence the model uses [3, 7, 18, 27]. Uncer-tainty quantification methods such as Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles, evidential learning, calibration, and selective prediction address part of this problem [16, 17, 22, 28, 34, 39], but they of-ten require retraining, architectural changes, or repeated inference. For a classifier that is already trained, a post-hoc reliability layer is attractive. Model confidence alone, however, does not indicate whether a prediction is supported by subjects whose explanations look alike. We propose Uncertainty-gated SHAP-Dirichlet Refinement , a post-hoc layer for a fixed probabilistic classifier. For each query subject, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [30] give a local explana-tion vector, which is compared with a bank of labeled reference explanations. The most similar references are converted into class-level Dirichlet evidence [39]. An uncertainty gate, by default local explanation instability under small feature perturbations measured by relative input and output stability (RIS and ROS) [1], decides which predictions may change. Unstable predictions are refined by fusing the classifier probability with the explanation-derived evidence, and the least reliable subjects can be routed to expert review. The layer never changes the classifier, and every parameter is selected on validation data. We evaluate the layer on 504 subjects of the SRPBS dataset [42] with five classifiers: the tabular deep learning models TabM [19] and ResNet [20], and logistic regression, an RBF support vector machine [9], and a random forest [5]. Because single-split results on this task are unstable, we use repeated nested cross-validation with pre-specified statistical tests. Our contributions are as follows: arXiv:2610.25635v1 [eess.IV] 19 Oct 2026 Nguyen et al. 

1. We formulate a post-hoc, reliability-aware decision layer that retains, refines, or defers the predictions of a fixed classifier by com-bining explanation retrieval, Dirichlet evidence, and an uncertainty gate (Sect. 3). 

2. We describe an exact batched implementation of KernelSHAP with common random numbers, which makes explanation-stability estimates comparable across perturbations, and a fix for a batch-dependent preprocessing step in a popular tabular deep learning library (Sect. 3.5). 

3. In repeated nested cross-validation with five classifiers, the layer with a training-plus-validation reference bank improved ac-curacy in every TabM and ResNet repetition, concentrated its gains on low-confidence subjects, and improved calibration. Controlled ablations show that gating and soft fusion keep post-hoc changes safe, and that the size of the reference bank matters more than the choice of gate (Sects. 5.1–5.4). 

4. SHAP-space retrieval yields more label-consistent references than raw-feature retrieval, SHAP-Dirichlet evidence identifies base-line errors above chance and adds to model confidence, and a de-ferral policy built on both raises the accuracy of automatically han-dled subjects to about 75% when half of the subjects are reviewed (Sects. 5.5–5.6). 

## 2 Related Work 

Machine learning for MDD from structural MRI. Morphometric features from FreeSurfer or FastSurfer [13, 25], typically summa-rized on the Desikan-Killiany atlas [11], are a common input for MDD classification. Group-level analyses of large consortia show consistent but small cortical and subcortical differences [38]. A meta-analysis of multivariate pattern recognition studies found heterogeneous accuracies across studies and modalities [26]. In community cohorts, a cross-validated accuracy of 75% in a small sample dropped to 53.7% in a larger independent sample [41], and a systematic study showed that small samples overestimate per-formance [14]. Cross-validation in small samples has large error bars [43], and methodological pitfalls such as test-set reuse remain common in medical imaging [44]. These findings motivate our use of repeated nested cross-validation and our focus on reliability rather than on a single accuracy number. 

Uncertainty, calibration, and selective prediction. Monte Carlo dropout [16], deep ensembles [28], and evidential deep learning [39] estimate predictive uncertainty but change how the model is trained or queried. Calibration methods [22] and calibration metrics such as the expected calibration error (ECE) [35] and the Brier score [6] assess whether probabilities match observed frequencies. Selective prediction abstains on uncertain inputs [8, 17], with the maximum class probability as a strong baseline [24], and learning-to-defer methods train a classifier together with a deferral policy [32]. In medical imaging, referring uncertain cases to experts improves the accuracy of the remaining automatic decisions [18, 29], and commu-nicating uncertainty is considered essential for clinical use [3, 27]. Our layer is post hoc, keeps the classifier fixed, and combines de-ferral with a correction step for unstable predictions. 

Neighbors and explanations as reliability signals. Nearest-neighbor agreement is a known reliability signal. The trust score compares the distance to the predicted class with the distance to other classes, and deep 𝑘 -nearest neighbors measure label agreement in learned representations [36]. We instead retrieve neighbors in the explana-tion space, where distances reflect how the model uses each feature, and convert them into Dirichlet evidence. SHAP values [30, 40] are commonly estimated with KernelSHAP [10, 30]. The stability of explanations under small input changes has been studied as a robustness property [1, 2], and toolkits such as Quantus implement RIS and ROS [23]. We use RIS and ROS not to evaluate explana-tion methods, but to gate which predictions a post-hoc layer may change. 

Tabular classifiers. Deep tabular models such as ResNet-style networks [20] and the parameter-efficient ensemble TabM [19] are strong general-purpose baselines, while tree-based models remain competitive with deep models on typical tabular data [21]. We there-fore evaluate the layer on both neural and classical classifiers [37].

## 3.5 Implementation 

Exact batched KernelSHAP. We explain the MDD probability with KernelSHAP [30], using 20 𝑘 -means centroids of the training split as background and 800 coalition samples. We reseed the sampler before every explanation, so a query and its perturbations share the same coalitions (common random numbers). Differences between their explanations then reflect the model rather than Monte Carlo noise of KernelSHAP, which matters because RIS and ROS divide explanation differences by small input changes. With fixed coali-tions, the KernelSHAP estimate is a fixed linear map of the coalition values. We therefore take the masks and kernel weights from the reference implementation and evaluate all perturbations of a query in one batched model call. On every outer fold, the batched esti-mate matched the reference implementation up to floating-point precision. 

Batch-independent preprocessing. Both neural backbones use piece-wise linear encoding (PLE) of the features. The PLE transform of the library we used clamps its last bin edge to the maximum of the current batch, so the prediction for a subject depends on the other subjects in the batch. This matters for post-hoc analysis, be-cause perturbed copies of one subject are evaluated in batches of very different composition. We removed the clamp and compiled the per-feature encoding into one vectorized GPU computation. Training inputs are unaffected, because the training maximum al-ways exceeds the last split, and the fixed transform reproduced the reported baseline of a previous single-split experiment exactly. 

## 4 Experimental Setup 

Data. We used T1-weighted MRI scans from the SRPBS dataset [42] and formed a balanced cohort of 504 subjects, with 252 healthy controls and 252 patients with MDD. Preprocessing followed the FastSurfer/FreeSurfer-based protocol [13, 25] of Beliveau et al. [4]. Scans were processed automatically, followed by a quality check that excluded large segmentation failures, without manual editing. Each subject was represented by 159 features: 70 cortical thickness and 70 surface-area features (34 Desikan-Killiany regions [11] per hemisphere plus one hemisphere average), 18 bilateral volumes of the accumbens, amygdala, caudate, cerebellar cortex, hippocam-pus, pallidum, putamen, thalamus, and lateral ventricles, and the estimated total intracranial volume. 

Classifiers. TabM [19] is an MLP with parameter-efficient im-plicit ensembling (32 members), and ResNet [20] is a residual feed-forward network. Both use PLE with 20 bins and are trained for up to 200 epochs with early stopping (patience 20) and batch size 32. Each has a grid of 72 configurations over width or depth, dropout, learning rate, and weight decay. The classical classifiers are L2-regularized logistic regression, an RBF support vector machine with Platt-scaled probabilities, and a random forest with 300 trees, each on standardized features with a small grid (Appendix A). 

Protocol. We used stratified 5-fold outer cross-validation, so ev-ery subject is a test subject exactly once per repetition. Within each outer fold, a 3-fold inner GridSearch selected the hyperparameters by F1-score. The selected model was refit on two thirds of the outer-training data (about 270 subjects), and the remaining third (about 135 subjects) served as the validation set for early stopping and for all refinement choices. The whole protocol was repeated with four split seeds for TabM and ResNet (20 outer folds each) and with two split seeds for the classical classifiers, whose KernelSHAP runs were the most expensive per subject. No test subject was used for training, model selection, reference construction, or rule selection. 

Refinement settings. We used 𝐾 = 5, 𝑀 = 10 multiplicative per-turbations 𝑥 𝑞 ⊙ ( 1 + 𝑢 ) with 𝑢 ∼ U [− 0.02 , 0.02 ]𝑑 , the ℓ2 norm in RIS and ROS, and log-probabilities as the logit surrogate. The grids were 𝜋 ∈ { 50 , 60 , 70 , 80 , 90 }, 𝜆 ∈ { 0, 0.05 , . . . , 0.95 }, and 𝜏 𝐸 ∈{0.50 , 0.55 , . . . , 0.75 }. Unless stated otherwise, the layer uses soft fu-sion with the gate measure and the similarity selected on validation. We report two reference banks. The pre-specified bank contains the validation subjects (about 135). The enlarged bank adds the training subjects (about 400 in total). We noted the enlarged bank as the most promising design choice after the first two repetitions, before the remaining two repetitions were analyzed, and we report those two as a confirmation. 

Comparisons. Each ablation changes one component and keeps every other choice validation-selected: hard instead of soft correc-tion, gates based on no gating, a random subset of the same size, low baseline confidence, or Monte Carlo dropout variance, retrieval in the standardized raw feature space, larger reference banks, and other values of 𝐾 . We also report decision-threshold tuning on validation data as a simple post-hoc alternative. 

Metrics and statistics. Within a repetition, metrics are computed on the 504 pooled out-of-fold predictions. We report accuracy, bal-anced accuracy, macro F1-score, ROC-AUC, the Brier score [6], the expected calibration error (ECE, 10 bins) [35], and the negative log-likelihood (NLL). The paired change in correctness is tested with an exact McNemar test per repetition [12]. Across repetitions, we use a sign test over repetition-level accuracy changes and the corrected resampled 𝑡 -test [33] over all fold-level changes, which accounts for the overlap between training sets. Calibration changes are reported with 95% paired bootstrap intervals. Error detection is measured by the AUROC of separating incorrect from correct base-line decisions, after mapping each score to its validation percentile within the fold. ## 5 Results 5.1 Refinement Across Classifiers 

Table 1 summarizes the main comparison. The fixed baselines reached between 63.74% (ResNet) and 66.27% (random forest) accu-racy, which is in the range reported for structural MRI in comparable samples [14, 26]. Fold-level accuracy varied widely, from 56% to 73% for TabM (Appendix A), so single-split results on this task are not reliable. With the pre-specified validation bank, the layer changed ac-curacy by +0.05 points for TabM and +0.50 points for ResNet, with gains in 2 and 3 of 4 repetitions. With about 135 references, the Dirichlet evidence is noisy, and the first two repetitions sug-gested that a larger bank helps (Table 2). With the enlarged train-ing+validation bank, accuracy increased in all 8 neural backbone repetitions (sign test 𝑝 = 0.008), from 64.24% to 65.28% for TabM and from 63.74% to 64.19% for ResNet (Fig. 2). The two confirma-tion repetitions gave +1.98, +0.99 points for TabM and +0.40, +0.20 points for ResNet. Balanced accuracy and macro F1 moved by the same amount, and ROC-AUC changed little because soft fusion only reorders the scores of gated subjects. The layer changed few decisions, at most 8.3% of the subjects in any repetition. Fold-level changes remain noisy, so the corrected resampled 𝑡 -test was not significant for either backbone ( 𝑝 = 0.38 and 𝑝 = 0.68), and the gains should be read as small but consistent. For the classical classifiers, the effect depended on the model. With the enlarged bank, refinement changed the accuracy of the RBF-SVM by +1.39 points (improved in both repetitions), of logistic regression by −0.10 points, and of the random forest by −0.50 points. The two classifiers that did not benefit had the most compressed probabilities: 34% (logistic regression) and 23% (random forest) of the test subjects had |2𝑝 𝑞 − 1| < 0.1, compared with 12% and 10% for TabM and ResNet. Near 0 .5, a small shift toward the Dirichlet estimate flips decisions in both directions. Over all five classifiers, accuracy increased in 11 of 14 repetitions. 

## 5.2 Layer Acts Localization 

We now analyze the layer with the enlarged bank. The gate passed 35.3% (TabM) and 31.5% (ResNet) of the test subjects to the correc-tion rule, and it selected harder subjects. Their baseline accuracy was 60.4% for TabM and 60.9% for ResNet, compared with 66.3% and 65.0% for the subjects that were not gated. Among gated subjects, refinement increased accuracy to 63.3% and 62.4%. Fig. 4 splits the test subjects by baseline confidence. The gains are concentrated in the least confident third, where accuracy increased from 51.5% to 54.0% for TabM and from 53.0% to 54.2% for ResNet, while only 0.1% (TabM) and 0.1% (ResNet) of the most confident decisions were changed. This is the intended behavior of a post-hoc layer: it leaves confident predictions alone and uses the explanation evi-dence where the classifier is close to the decision boundary. 

## 5.3 Ablation Study 

Table 2 changes one component of the pre-specified layer at a time. Hard flipping lowered accuracy for both backbones ( −0.45 and −0.35 points), while soft fusion did not. Without a gate, soft fusion acted on every subject and changed accuracy by −0.74 and −0.20 points, because it also altered many correct decisions. Every gate was better than no gate. Monte Carlo dropout variance gave the largest gain for ResNet (+0.94 points) and a gain of +0.60 points for TabM, and the explanation-stability gates were competitive. The choice of the uncertainty measure therefore mattered less than having a gate. The reference bank was the most influential choice. Adding the training subjects gave +1.04 and +0.45 points and improved accuracy in every repetition, while retrieval in the raw feature space gave +0.64 and +0.20 points. The default 𝐾 = 5 was the best value for ResNet, and no value of 𝐾 changed TabM accuracy by more than 0.3 points. Decision-threshold tuning alone gave −0.30 and +0.45 points. 

## 5.4 Calibration 

Refinement also improved the probabilities themselves (Table 3). For both neural backbones, the Brier score and the NLL decreased, and the 95% intervals exclude zero (TabM −3.6 and −10.4, ResNet 

−5.1 and −16.2, both ×10 −3). The ECE of ResNet dropped from 11.4% to 9.0%, and that of TabM from 7.6% to 6.2%. Logistic regression also gained in Brier score, while the SVM and the random forest were unchanged. Fig. 5 shows the reason. ResNet was overconfident for high predicted MDD probabilities, and fusing these probabilities with the Dirichlet estimate, which its prior shrinks toward 0 .5, moved them closer to the diagonal. Calibration gains therefore come together with the accuracy gains, and they matter for downstream uses of the probability, such as risk communication and deferral thresholds. 

## 5.5 Explanation Space 

Table 4 compares retrieval in SHAP space with retrieval in the standardized raw feature space. SHAP space with the Euclidean dis-tance gave the most label-consistent references for both backbones (58.9% vs. at most 57.6% in feature space), and the Dirichlet estimate reached 62.2% accuracy for TabM, compared with at most 61.3% for any feature-space metric. Explanations remove features that the model ignores and weight the remaining ones by their effect, so neighbors in SHAP space share the evidence the model actually used. A 5-nearest-neighbor classifier on the test subjects of the first TabM repetition reached 63.3% accuracy in SHAP space and 61.7% in feature space (Appendix B, Fig. 8). The explanations were also consistent across architectures. The rank correlation of the mean absolute SHAP values of TabM and ResNet over the 159 features was 0.94. The most important features (Fig. 3) include the surface area of the left caudal anterior cingulate, the thickness of the rostral anterior cingulate, the caudal middle frontal and precentral cortex, the accumbens volume, and the lin-gual surface area. The cingulate features overlap with the regions in which large consortia report cortical differences in adult MDD [38], and the caudal anterior cingulate, lingual, and precentral features overlap with the variables most often selected by a decision tree for MDD classification in an independent cohort [41]. Importance at the level of single folds was less stable, which is expected for small effects estimated from about 270 training subjects.

densely the bank covers explanation space. With about 135 valida-tion subjects, retrieved neighbors are often far from the query, and the evidence is noisy. Adding the training subjects triples the bank. Their explanations come from the same fixed model, and their la-bels are known, so the enlarged bank uses no test information. The consistent gain in all 8 neural repetitions suggests that the layer will benefit from more labeled reference subjects in deployment, for example from other sites. 

Why the accuracy gains are modest. The baselines are weak, in line with other structural MRI studies of depression [14, 41], and evidence retrieved from a few hundred subjects cannot be much more accurate than the classifier itself. The Dirichlet estimate alone reached about 62% accuracy. Each rule is also selected on a small validation set, so the selection is noisy. We therefore present ac-curacy as one benefit among several, together with calibration, low-confidence correction, and expert triage. 

The role of RIS and ROS. RIS and ROS did not separate correct from incorrect decisions on their own, so the gate does not work as an error detector. It works as a selector of subjects whose ex-planation is sensitive to small, measurement-scale feature changes, which makes the correction conservative. The gated subjects were also less accurate than the rest, so the gate and the evidence act on the same subjects. Monte Carlo dropout variance was an even better gate in our experiments, and the framework accepts any uncertainty score. 

Classical classifiers. The layer helped the RBF-SVM but not lo-gistic regression or the random forest. Both of the latter produce probabilities close to 0 .5 for many subjects, and for logistic regres-sion the SHAP vectors are rescaled feature vectors, so explanation retrieval adds little beyond the features themselves. The effect of the layer on a given classifier should therefore be confirmed on independent folds before deployment, as in our protocol. 

Lessons for evaluation. Single test folds are too small to rank post-hoc rules on this task. In the first two repetitions, the accuracy change of the twelve fixed rule variants on single folds ranged from 

−4.95 to +5.0 points, and the variant with the largest single-fold gain changed accuracy by −0.59 points on average over all folds. Selecting a rule by its test-fold gain would therefore have reported a misleading improvement. Repeated nested cross-validation with pre-specified tests and a separate confirmation step is necessary for post-hoc methods on small neuroimaging datasets [43, 44]. 

Limitations. We used one dataset with site effects that were not harmonized [15], and we did not test transfer to external sites. The enlarged reference bank was chosen after two repetitions and con-firmed on two further repetitions of the same dataset, which is an internal rather than an external confirmation. The validation set serves for early stopping and for rule selection, which makes validation estimates optimistic, although the test folds remain un-touched. KernelSHAP for perturbed copies is expensive, especially for large ensembles. Finally, the expert-review path was evaluated by simulation and not with clinicians. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented uncertainty-gated SHAP-Dirichlet refinement, a post-hoc layer that retains, refines, or defers the predictions of a fixed MRI-based MDD classifier. In repeated nested cross-validation on 504 SRPBS subjects, the layer with a training-plus-validation refer-ence bank improved accuracy in every TabM and ResNet repetition, concentrated these gains on low-confidence subjects, and improved calibration. Gating and soft fusion were essential for these proper-ties, and the effect carried over to an RBF-SVM but not to logistic regression or a random forest. SHAP-space retrieval produced more label-consistent references than raw-feature retrieval, and the re-sulting evidence supported a deferral policy that raised the accuracy of automatically handled subjects to about 75% at 50% coverage. A natural next step is to validate the review pathway with clinicians, to enlarge the reference bank across sites, and to test the layer under external site and cohort shifts.
