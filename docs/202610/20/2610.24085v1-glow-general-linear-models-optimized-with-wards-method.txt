Keywords: general linear model, MANCOVA, permutation inference, family-wise error rate, Ward’s method, hierarchical segmentation 

Abstract: Identifying brain regions whose imaging features correlate with variables of interest (e.g. diagnosis, age) is a central goal of neuroimaging, usually pursued with the General Linear Model (GLM). Region-Based Analysis dilutes or splits any effect which does not fill exactly one atlas region. Voxel-Based Analysis (VBA), with Cluster Extent Thresholding (CET) and Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) built on it, fits a GLM per voxel and groups adjacent significant voxels. But neighboring voxels can share a statistic without sharing a GLM, and a per-voxel statistic cannot tell them apart. GLOW (General Linear Models Optimized with Ward’s method) instead builds a hierarchy of nested regions by greedily minimizing GLM error, which we show is a special case of Ward’s clustering. Maximum-statistic (Westfall-Young) permutation controls family-wise error over all regions. Significance spreads through the hierarchy, to an effect’s nested fragments and the regions containing it. GLOW’s final step seeks to prune away these distractions to output whole, pure effect extent estimates. We validate GLOW by planting synthetic effects of known extent and varying strength in diffusion images from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) and in white noise. Pruning delivers wholeness on spatially consistent effects: GLOW returns one as a single region on 57% of HCP trials against the best baseline’s 35% ; the baselines recover comparable volume, but in many pure pieces. The cost is positive predictive value (PPV): GLOW is more sensitive (by up to 0.29 ), but its regions overrun the effect, mostly because no region of the hierarchy isolates the effect rather than because of the pruning. On HCP, GLOW’s average Dice gains 0.059 over the best baseline at weaker effects and gives back 0.005 at stronger ones. On white noise, GLOW trails severely, as much as 0.61 : its hierarchy holds the effect, but its permutation test does not certify it. Its runtime grows roughly linearly with voxel count, reaching about four and a half times that of the voxel-wise methods on the full HCP volume. # 1 Introduction 

A fundamental aim of neuroimaging research is to identify how independent variables (e.g. age, diagnosis, or behavioral measures) influence brain structure (Ashburner & Friston, 2000; Friston et al., 1995). For example, white-matter microstructure, as measured by fractional anisotropy (FA) or mean diffusivity (MD), may differ between clinical groups or correlate with behavioral scores (Smith et al., 2006). Such a study images a set of individuals whose independent variable varies, then registers those images into a common anatomical space (Avants et al., 2011; Jenkinson et al., 2002). Throughout this work, we assume that registration has already been done. The General Linear Model (GLM) provides a unified framework for testing whether image intensities correlate with independent variables after controlling for confounds (Friston et al., 1995; Worsley et al., 2002). By encoding hypotheses in a design-matrix/contrast framework, the GLM supports t-tests, ANOVA, multiple regression, and factorial designs, with nuisance regressors explicitly adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, or acquisition site. Before applying a GLM, one must decide how to group voxels for analysis. Region-Based Analyses (RBA) define regions a priori, typically from anatomical atlases (Desikan et al., 2006; Glasser et al., 2016; Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002). Each region’s GLM tests whether an effect exists uniformly across its voxels. Statistical power suffers in two complementary ways: including non-effect voxels in a region dilutes the signal, while splitting a single effect across atlas boundaries fragments it (Eickhoff et al., 2018). Both errors are to be expected: atlas boundaries track anatomy, not the unknown extent of some effect. Voxel-Based Analyses (VBA) sidestep atlas dependence by fitting a GLM at every voxel and gluing adjacent, significant voxels together post hoc (Ashburner & Friston, 2000; Friston et al., 1995). A central challenge for VBA is incorporating spatial scale: a lone island of significance is more likely to be a false positive than a large, contiguous continent of significant voxels. Cluster Extent Thresholding (CET) (Forman et al., 1995; Friston et al., 1994) and Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) (Smith & Nichols, 2009) act as spatial regularization steps on top of VBA, favoring continents over islands. CET thresholds the statistic map and keeps those clusters of suprathreshold voxels whose extent is unlikely under the null. TFCE sidesteps the choice of any one threshold by using all of them: each voxel’s statistic is enhanced in proportion to the extent of the suprathreshold clusters containing it, accumulated over every threshold. Because each voxel is tested on its own, the resulting map must be corrected for a massive number of comparisons. The family-wise error rate (FWER) (T. Nichols & Hayasaka, 2003) is controlled either parametrically, via Random Field Theory (Worsley, Marrett, Neelin, Vandal, et al., 1996), or nonparametrically, via permutation (T. E. Nichols & Holmes, 2002; Winkler et al., 2014). Multivariate data fit the same voxel-wise template: PALM tests several imaging features jointly at each voxel, via either classical multivariate statistics or non-parametric combination (Winkler et al., 2016), still leaving the single voxel as the unit of inference. VBA’s first aggregation across voxels comes before any test: each image is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel, so every voxel’s statistic already averages its neighbors. By the matched filter theorem (Turin, 31960), smoothing best detects a signal when the kernel matches the signal’s own extent: wide effects want a wide kernel, narrow effects a narrow one (Friston, 2004; Worsley, Marrett, Neelin, & Evans, 1996). That extent is not known a priori, so the analyst fixes one width in advance for the whole image, and conclusions can turn on the choice: kernels of 0 to 16 mm led one diffusion data set to four different conclusions (Jones et al., 2005). We smooth each voxel-wise baseline at the width which serves it best on our data (Appendix D). VBA and the cluster methods built on it share a clear advantage over RBA: their output regions can mold to the contours of effects present in the data. But pooling per-voxel statistics, as CET and TFCE do, or blurring the images each voxel’s model is fit to, as smoothing does, is not the same as fitting one model across voxels. A per-voxel statistic is all VBA ever sees of a voxel’s GLM, and there is plenty of wiggle room among GLMs which share an F -statistic (Fig 1). F = 100

Consider the three neighboring voxels of Fig 1, whose effects A, B, and C are clearly distinct yet produce an identical per-voxel statistic. Seeing three identical statistics, VBA is encouraged to glue the voxels together into a single output. The statistic is a bottleneck: whatever passes through it cannot tell VBA whether neighboring voxels agree on a model, so VBA can neither reward that agreement nor penalize its absence. The bottleneck costs detection power, not only interpretation. Under the null, where imaging features carry no correlation with the independent variables, n contiguous voxels which each reach F = 100 under one shared effect A are rarer than n contiguous voxels which each reach F = 100 under whatever model they please; the first event is contained in the second. A per-voxel test draws its null from the second, looser event, so the permuted images are free to cobble together unrelated GLMs and compete with the observed statistic. Asking the voxels to agree on a model narrows what the null can offer, which is the power GLOW is after. Cluster-wise inference has known weaknesses: Woo et al. (2014) and Eklund et al. (2016) show that it 4 2 PROBLEM & ANALYSIS 

can inflate false-positive rates and pool heterogeneous signals. A more principled remedy comes from All-Resolutions Inference (ARI) and its true-discovery-proportion descendants, which report a lower bound on the fraction of truly active voxels holding simultaneously for any region the analyst selects (Blain et al., 2022; Rosenblatt et al., 2018). Yet the bound is a proportion, not a partition: for whatever region the analyst queries it reports how many voxels are active, never which ones. Data-driven parcellation approaches, including spatially constrained clustering (Craddock et al., 2012; Michel et al., 2012; Schaefer et al., 2018; Thirion et al., 2014), address the atlas-dependence problem but do not optimize regions specifically for GLM fit (Eickhoff et al., 2018). 1 Our objective is to construct regions whose voxels follow one spatially homogeneous GLM. GLOW (General Linear Models Optimized with Ward’s method) does so by building a hierarchical segmentation which greedily minimizes each region’s GLM mean-squared error, via Ward’s agglomerative clustering (Ward Jr, 1963); in practice it measures that error only along the variables of interest (Section 3.2). The significance of all regions is evaluated with the same permutation machinery used for voxel-wise inference: Freedman–Lane residualization (Freedman & Lane, 1983) and Westfall-Young max-statistic FWER (Westfall & Young, 1993), as set out by Winkler et al. (2014). The only change is that this machinery is applied to GLOW’s data-derived regions rather than to fixed voxels or threshold-formed clusters. GLOW is multivariate as well: it analyzes several imaging features jointly (e.g. fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity) within the GLM/MANCOVA framework (Rencher & Christensen, 2012), as PALM’s multivariate tests do at the voxel level (Winkler et al., 2016). We validate GLOW by imposing synthetic effects onto image sets of known ground truth and comparing detection performance against VBA, CET and TFCE. One image set honors our statistical assumptions (white Gaussian noise); the other carries the spatial correlations of real data (diffusion images from the Human Connectome Project (Van Essen et al., 2013)). In the remainder of the paper we analyze the GLM in the special case here where every voxel shares one design matrix (Section 2), describe GLOW’s method (Section 3), present experiments and results (Sections 4 and 5), and discuss how spatial dependence and region selection limit GLOW (Section 6).

To that end GLOW uses agglomerative clustering, repeatedly merging the neighboring pair of regions whose GLMs are most alike. Starting from one voxel per region, it merges until a single region remains. The result is a hierarchy of nested regions in which every multi-voxel region splits into exactly two subregions (see the first panel of Fig 2). ## 3.3 Significance Testing 

GLOW’s inference follows the framework of Winkler et al. (2014), reusing that machinery (Freedman–Lane residual permutation and Westfall-Young max-statistic FWER) essentially unchanged. Two issues arise when applying Westfall-Young in GLOW’s context. First, GLOW tailors its regions to the effects present in the unpermuted images. Reusing those same regions on the permuted image sets, for which they were never optimized, would hand the observed statistics an advantage the null draws never had. Selecting regions on the observed data and then testing them there is circular (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009), and it breaks the exchangeability on which the FWER bound rests (Hemerik & Goeman, 2018). GLOW restores exchangeability by building a separate hierarchical segmentation for each permuted image set, then collecting the maximum statistic of each hierarchy into the comparison set. The second issue concerns how each region’s statistic is prepared before the regions compete for the maximum: raw statistics are not comparable across regions of different sizes. ## 3.4 Region Pruning 

If all has gone well, GLOW has flagged a region matching each true effect’s extent as significant. It has almost certainly flagged that region’s relatives in the hierarchy too: a strong effect lends its significance to the smaller fragments nested inside it and to the larger regions which contain it. These overlapping regions are redundant descriptions of one effect, and GLOW’s final step prunes them away, leaving output regions which capture all, and only, the voxels of a single effect’s extent. Let B denote the set of bloomed regions, those output to the user; every other significant region is 

pruned (Fig 4). A valid B must meet two requirements. First, its regions must be pairwise disjoint: homogeneous effects cannot meaningfully overlap, so neither should the regions we report. Second, every significant region must intersect some member of B; otherwise we would silently discard a significant result. Together these make B a tree-cut (similar in spirit to Michel et al. (2012)). One natural choice of B maximizes the likelihood of the observed images. Under this model each voxel follows the βr of whichever bloomed region contains it, while a voxel in no member of B follows the reduced model’s βr, contributing a convenient LLR of zero. Spatial independence of the residuals makes the objective tractable: the log-likelihood ratio over all significant voxels is just the sum of the per-region ratios, P

Imposing an effect also puts its character under our control: turning the effect’s LLR knob up, we watch detection performance change as effects grow from subtle to obvious. We test on two image sets: White Gaussian Noise (WGN), which satisfies our modeling assumptions (independent and identically distributed Gaussian residuals), and diffusion MRI maps from the Human Connectome Project (HCP), which carry the realistic spatial structure of a brain. For either image set we first sample the effect’s extent, the set of voxels it will occupy. Starting from a single seed voxel chosen uniformly at random, we grow the extent to a target size, at each step annexing whichever neighboring voxel minimizes the resulting region’s spatial variance Tr( S) (Fig 5). We call it the MinVar extent. On HCP the MinVar extent follows tissue, so its voxels share a mean and its spatial variance S stays small; on WGN, which has no tissue to follow, it is a one-voxel-thick dendrite spread through the crop. We also plant a shape which ignores the images: a ball of the same size centered in the crop, grown by repeated face-connected dilation (an octahedron, the ball of the voxel grid’s own metric). Each sphere is planted on the same images as its MinVar twin. On HCP a sphere typically straddles tissue boundaries, so its voxel means differ and S is large, and the offset which brings the whole region to its target LLR must be larger than a MinVar extent of the same size would need. Such a sphere is arguably not one effect but several: the voxels on either side of an anatomical boundary share the planted offset but not a common ¯Y , and so not one GLM in the sense of Section 2.1. Each source (WGN or HCP) is cropped to a single connected sphere of 25 ,000 voxels (about a tenth of a full-brain volume at HCP resolution), with N = 100 subjects. We set effect strength with LLR-per-voxel, which is closer to |r|-invariant than raw LLR, sweeping eleven logarithmically spaced values from 0.003 to 0.3.Experiments which do not sweep effect strength use that grid’s midpoint, 0.03 . Both sources sit on a 

2 mm isotropic grid, so a kernel width in millimeters means the same on each. The voxel-wise baselines smooth every image with a 2 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel (Appendix D); GLOW does not smooth. GLOW uses ninner = 250 inner permutations. Each configuration runs 50 random seeds. Significance testing for all methods bounds FWER at α = 0 .05 using nfwer = 500 outer (Westfall-Young) permutations. That is fewer permutations than a single published study ought to use, but it buys us comprehensive testing 5.1 Tuning: Segmentation Objective 17 across many contexts here. All performance comparisons report Dice, sensitivity, and positive predictive value (precision) against the planted ground-truth extent, together with the homogeneity and completeness of Section 2.1. We report PPV rather than specificity because effects cover only a small fraction of the volume: specificity is then dominated by the large true-negative pool and stays near one even for a method which flags many false positives (Maier-Hein et al., 2024). With a single planted effect, homogeneity and completeness each reduce to a simpler question. Com-pleteness asks whether the detection comes back as one region, and is nearly binary. A trial that returns one region leaves no uncertainty in R for T to resolve, so H(R) = 0 and the convention scores it 1.A trial that returns several regions scores near 0, however cleanly those regions tile the effect: both scores see detected voxels alone, so as detection gets cleaner nearly every detected voxel carries the same label T = 1 , and H(T ) vanishes, taking the numerator I(R, T ) ≤ H(T ) with it. Mean completeness is therefore close to the proportion of trials which return a single region. Homogeneity asks whether the region boundaries separate effect voxels from false positives. It credits only what the split into regions tells a reader about T beyond the detected volume taken as a whole, so a single mixed region scores 

0 however much of it is effect, while many small pure regions score near 1. Put plainly, completeness rewards handing the detection back in one piece, homogeneity rewards pieces that are each all effect or all false positive, and neither counts how much of the effect was found, which Dice and sensitivity measure. 

## 5.1 Tuning: Segmentation Objective 

We compare the three Ward’s objectives of Table 1. Ideally a segmentation produces some region which matches the effect’s extent, so we score it by oracle Dice: the largest Dice overlap with the ground truth extent taken across every region of the hierarchy (Fig 7). Of course, oracle Dice cannot be computed in practice; we use it here to isolate segmentation quality. Comfortingly, every objective’s oracle Dice climbs toward one as the effect strengthens. On WGN data, discarding the GLM residuals before segmentation (as both GLM Error and Focus do) consistently improves Dice. On realistic images (HCP), Focus performs admirably at high LLR, suggesting that the reduced model’s GLM error may indeed be a distraction. We suspect its relative underperformance at low LLR is an artifact of how we sample effect extents: they are grown to minimize spatial variance, which is exactly what Naive Ward’s minimizes. GLM Error, whose Q retains that critical bias column ⃗ 1 in the span of Q0,inherits much of the same advantage. Focus is left adrift here because it strips each voxel’s across-image mean (see the Focus discussion of Section 3.2). The GLM Error and Naive objectives are, we would argue, “cheating” here: they reproduce the very mechanism which sampled the effect extent. ## 5.3 Type I Error Control 

Before evaluating detection performance, we verify that GLOW controls the FWER. Under the null hypothesis (no imposed effect) we run 1,000 seeds of HCP and WGN images. We report the percentage of experiments which declared any region significant, the empirical FWER, against the nominal rate (Fig 9). On both sources the empirical rejection rate tracks the nominal α across its whole range, the 95% band bracketing the ideal line ( 4.8% on HCP and 5.1% on WGN at α = 0 .05 ), so at these 1,000 seeds GLOW’s FWER under the global null is indistinguishable from nominal. That is weak control. Strong control asks more: in an image which does hold an effect, the chance of rejecting any region containing none of it must still not exceed α. The detection experiments of Section 5.4 measure this directly, since an output region without a single planted voxel is a false rejection of a true null. Table 2 counts the trials which return one. On HCP GLOW’s count never exceeds 5 of 50, indistinguishable from nominal (one-sided binomial p ≥ 0.10 ), though suggestive. On WGN the count holds at or near zero through the weaker effects, then climbs with strength to as many as 25 of 50, well above α. That climb is the risk anticipated in Section 3.3: the effect itself raises the null regions’ chance of reaching significance. ## 5.5 Computation 

As described thus far, GLOW is sorely in need of computational optimization. The central challenge is computing log-likelihood ratios for a hierarchical segmentation quickly. A region’s LLR needs its E and 

H matrices (13, 14), which depend only on |r|, Y ′T Y ′ and ¯Yr. Given these sufficient statistics for two regions, those of their union follow immediately. We may therefore walk the tree in topological order, from single voxels up to the whole image, at a per-region cost which no longer grows with region size |r|.26 5 RESULTS 

Better yet, re-indexing the voxels in the tree’s depth-first order leaves every region spanning a contiguous range of voxel indices. Each of |r|, Y ′T Y ′ and ¯Yr is then the difference between two entries of a single cumulative sum over the voxels, the standard prefix-sum trick. Even so, the inner calibration of Section 3.3 multiplies this workload: every region of every outer (FWER) permutation needs its own ninner inner permutations to estimate the mean and standard deviation that studentize its LLR (19). A fit therefore pays for nfwer tree builds, each carrying ninner +1 LLR computations per region. This multiplier, not the clustering, separates GLOW’s wall time from a voxel-wise test’s. To characterize compute time, we timed each method’s fit on HCP images at analysis-crop sizes ranging from 1,000 to 224,619 voxels (the full connected HCP support), with a single imaging feature ( b = 1 ). A moderate effect (per-voxel LLR of 0.03 over 10% of the crop) was imposed so the pruning step had a genuine effect to process. Each fit ran nfwer = 500 outer (Westfall-Young) permutations, GLOW additionally drawing ninner = 250 inner permutations per region. Every method parallelized its permutation walk across all cores of one workstation (Intel i9-14900K, 24 cores / 32 threads) with a single consumer-grade NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU; the reported wall time is what a user on such a machine waits for. All methods were tuned, to the author’s limited capability, to their best on that machine. GLOW’s wall time was bound by the GPU computing its LLRs: just 10 threads feeding it hierarchical segmentations were enough to saturate its bandwidth. At the full support GLOW takes about four and a half times as long as the voxel-wise methods. 10 3 10 4 10 5

# 6 Discussion 

GLOW’s advantage is concentrated where an effect is hard to see, and on real images: on HCP it leads on Dice below LLR /|r| = 0 .03 and ties above it, while on white noise it trails from moderate strengths up. Part of that lead on subtle effects may come from the baselines’ kernel width: each runs at the single width that maximizes its mean Dice pooled over effect strengths, while subtle effects favor a wider one (Appendix D). A wider kernel would narrow GLOW’s lead on subtle effects, but would cost the baselines more Dice on strong effects than it gains on weak effects. GLOW, by contrast, has no kernel to tune, so it 28 6 DISCUSSION 

never needs a guess at the effect’s strength or geometry in advance. Compared to voxel-wise baselines, it does a better job handing a spatially consistent effect’s whole extent back to the user (the completeness gap of Section 5.4), at the cost of bundling more tagalong false positive voxels along with it. GLOW’s keystone ingredient is that it reads imaging features across voxels when judging which GLMs are viable. VBA does great work with per-voxel statistics, but a per-voxel statistic can only characterize so much (Fig 1). Where an effect is strong enough to be noticed in each of its voxels alone, VBA does a great job. Where it is subtle enough to need repeated observation across voxels, GLOW shines. Detection aside, per-voxel statistics make it hard to construct regions holding all, and only, the voxels of one consistent GLM. Adjacency among significant voxels may be too permissive a criterion for the job. Much has been written about a cluster’s poor spatial specificity, that its extent licenses no claim about which of its voxels carry the effect (Rosenblatt et al., 2018; Woo et al., 2014). There is a second difficulty: should two distinct effects happen to lie side by side, adjacency alone will not tell them apart. A sphere planted across a tissue boundary is such a case: a per-voxel test, fitting each voxel its own mean, cannot see the boundary, while GLOW’s region LLR can, since pooling the two sides adds their difference in means to E (24). The Focus objective, which strips each voxel’s mean, does not segment along it, however. A hierarchical segmentation may offer a fruitful vantage point on the problem. The pruning step presented here takes the common statistical approach of maximizing likelihood, and many of GLOW’s troubles trace back to the model generating that likelihood (independent and identically distributed Gaussian residuals): 

LLR = N |r|

2 log |E + H||E| (21) The N |r| factor encapsulates the repeated measurements of a GLM’s deviation from H0; it is the row count of the stacked regression of Y ′ on X′ (8). Given another N images of unrelated subjects showing the same GLM, we would rightly double the LLR. It is concerning that adding |r| consistent voxels has the same impact. Correlation across space explains away part of the repeated measure: the extra voxels may only be mimicking their neighbors rather than independently pointing at one underlying GLM. This raises the essential question: how many independent observations does a region of size |r| over N

images actually provide? TFCE answers implicitly through its e = 0 .5 and h = 2 exponents, trading a signal’s spatial extent against its height. CET does the same with its cluster-forming threshold: higher initial thresholds favor localized effects. VBA’s initial smoothing is clumsy here, requiring one kernel width be chosen a priori even though the optimal width depends on the effect’s strength (Appendix D) and geometry (compact effects prefer wider kernels than branching ones). Compounding the difficulty, any answer must depend on the image’s resolution and spatial autocorrelation, which varies across it, as some anatomy is more consistent than the rest (Hayasaka et al., 2004). Regardless, GLOW is the most naive of all, taking the N |r| count at face value. At some point a method must decide which is less consistent under H0: an intense GLM observed in a handful of voxels or a subtler GLM observed across many more. Let us follow this question, and the 29 spatial independence assumption, through GLOW’s three steps. (1) In practice the segmentation caps GLOW’s performance on real imaging data: at weak effects on HCP, most of the PPV GLOW leaves on the table lies beyond any pruning rule’s reach, because no node of the tree isolates the effect’s extent (Fig 13). Modeling spatial autocorrelation might raise that ceiling. (2) The studentization of (19) allows the hypothesis testing to largely sidestep this question. Observed regions are compared to permuted versions of themselves which, critically, have the same size and spatial autocorrelation (thanks to block-exchangeability). (3) Spatial independence has profound consequences for the pruning method. It makes the likelihood additive across disjoint regions, so no preference is afforded to an intense, localized effect over a diffuse one carrying the same total. Dynamic Programming feels this as (20), where a region’s LLR never exceeds the sum over any partition of it, so the global optimum always fragments; greedy feels it as a raw 

LLR which grows with |r|, so its argmax overshoots the extent. GLOW backs away from the clean global optimum in favor of the greedy placeholder only because the second failure is the more forgiving of the two (Section 5.2). A model which captures spatial dependence, such as a conditional autoregressive (CAR) prior (Besag, 1974), may improve the segmentation and lift these limits. An LLR which priced a region’s own spatial dependence might also be comparable across region sizes on its own, retiring the inner permutation loop which dominates GLOW’s runtime (Section 5.5).

All maps are resampled to a common MNI template, registered with ANTs (Avants et al., 2011, 2025). The software also includes a dashboard-style viewer, which shows several regions of the hierarchical segmentation at once alongside their GLM models, their extents overlaid on the original images (glow-viewer.web.app).

# D Tuning the Voxel-wise Baselines 

Each voxel-wise baseline (VBA, VBA-TFCE and CET) carries two choices, its statistic and its smoothing kernel, which we tune jointly: picking one at a time would assume the other’s answer. 

Statistic. Four classical MANCOVA statistics generalize the univariate F -statistic: Wilks’ Lambda (entered as 1 − Λ, so it grows with evidence against H0), Pillai’s Trace, Hotelling–Lawley Trace and Roy’s Largest Root (Rencher & Christensen, 2012). The fifth candidate is GLOW’s LLR (7) on a single-voxel region. Each also has a z-scored variant, which standardizes every voxel’s statistic by its mean and standard deviation across the nfwer + 1 permutation rows, the unpermuted row included (Phipson & Smyth, 2010), before the max-statistic null; this puts HCP’s spatially heterogeneous voxels on a common scale. 

Kernel. Before the statistic is computed, every image is smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian of FWHM 

0, 2, 4, 8 or 12 mm ( 0, 1, 2, 4 or 6 voxels). The kernel reads beyond the analyzed crop, and the smoothed images are divided by the identically smoothed brain mask so voxels at the brain’s edge are not dimmed. 

Selection. Each baseline takes the best mean Dice of its 50 variants (5 statistics × 2 scalings × 5kernels) over HCP experiments at b = 1 : five effect strengths ( 0.003 , 0.0075 , 0.03 , 0.12 and 0.3) crossed with 10 seeds, other settings at the defaults of Section 5.
