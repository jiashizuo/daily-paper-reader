Abstract 

Functional connectivity changes across adult aging, but most evidence is cross-sectional, and covariance-based connectivity depends on blood oxygena-tion level dependent (BOLD) fluctuation amplitude. Covariance decline can reflect changing fluctuation amplitude, and its interpretation may depend on whether normalization uses signal amplitude or includes estimated noise. It is also unclear which longitudinal changes recur across brain states. We analyzed 852 adults (baseline age 36–89 years) from the Aging Adult Brain Connectome with repeated functional MRI (fMRI) during rest, inhibitory control, associative memory, and visual–motor processing (2,220 sessions; median follow-up 5.5 years). Baseline age and elapsed follow-up were modeled separately. Ten cortical networks were tested jointly across states, and covari-ance was normalized either by released signal amplitude or by a candidate total amplitude reconstructed from released signal and noise amplitudes. Native common covariance declined by 7–11% per 5 years, while signal pair amplitude declined by 9% on average across states, significantly more than estimated noise pair amplitude. 1. Introduction 

Large-scale functional networks change across the adult lifespan, but the organization of that change remains incompletely understood. Cross-sectional studies have commonly reported weaker within-network organization, stronger interaction among networks, and reduced system segregation with increasing age (Chan et al., 2014; Deery et al., 2023). Longitudinal resting-state work has provided within-person evidence for declining segregation in associative systems, including default, frontoparietal control, and salience networks (Malagurski et al., 2020). At the same time, functional architecture is not fixed across brain states. Age differences in network organization can differ between rest and task, and older adults may show less consistent network organization across states (Hughes et al., 2020; Varangis et al., 2021). These observations raise a basic longitudinal question: does adult aging produce a systems-level reconfiguration that recurs across distinct functional states, or are longitudinal changes primarily state specific? A second issue is measurement scale. Covariance-based functional connec-tivity preserves both temporal coupling and blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) fluctuation amplitude, whereas correlation-based measures remove amplitude by standardization. This distinction matters in aging: healthy aging is associated with a broadly distributed reduction in resting-state BOLD amplitude, such that covariance-based connectivity decreases with age while correlation-based connectivity is comparatively preserved (Strain et al., 2022). Standardization itself involves a choice. Correlation normalizes covariance by measured fluctuation amplitudes, which include residual noise. Normalization 2by estimated signal amplitudes uses a different denominator, and the two can yield different age trajectories when signal and noise components change differently. Relative network comparisons, in turn, remove a multiplicative change shared across all network covariances while remaining sensitive to differences among networks. Whether these components can be separated within individuals over time, and which conclusions depend on how amplitude is normalized, remains unclear. This distinction also matters for network-specific aging effects. Asso-ciative networks, including frontoparietal systems, have repeatedly been implicated in age-related changes in functional segregation and task organi-zation (Malagurski et al., 2020; Hughes et al., 2020). A network’s relative position can change because its own covariance changes, because other net-works change, or both. We therefore evaluated the complete network profile before characterizing individual networks within it. Here we examined within-person change in network covariance over a median of 5.5 years in 852 adults from the Aging Adult Brain Connectome, with fMRI during rest and three tasks: inhibitory control, associative memory, and visual–motor processing. We separated baseline age from elapsed follow-up, tested ten cortical networks jointly across states, and compared covariance normalized by released signal amplitude with covariance normalized by a candidate total amplitude reconstructed from the released signal and noise estimates. Native covariance declined alongside released signal amplitude, and whether a common decline remained after normalization depended on the amplitude denominator. Beneath this overall change, the relative network profile reconfigured in a state-dependent manner under both normalizations, and frontoparietal covariance rose relative to the other networks in rest and all three tasks. 2.2. Imaging-derived measures 

The released fMRI imaging-derived phenotypes were generated from time series processed through the HCP pipelines, including MSMAll registration, spatial-ICA/FIX cleanup, and temporal-ICA cleanup (Glasser et al., 2013, 2016; Salimi-Khorshidi et al., 2014; Glasser et al., 2018). According to the release documentation, structured artifacts related to head motion and physiology were removed before the connectivity products were estimated, and images were intensity normalized (Human Connectome Project, 2026). The available release documentation does not specify whether task-evoked responses were regressed out before covariance estimation. Task-state covari-ance may therefore include shared task-evoked responses. Run-level motion, temporal signal-to-noise ratio, and usable-frame summaries were unavailable in the resources used for this analysis and could not be used as covariates. We mapped the 360 HCP-MMP1.0 cortical parcels (Glasser et al., 2016) to canonical functional networks using the assignment of Ji et al. (2019). A network was retained if it contained at least two parcels and, in every state, had finite within-network covariance in at least 90% of eligible parent observations and positive covariance in at least 99% of finite observations. Ten networks met these criteria: Visual1, Visual2, Somatomotor, Cingulo-Opercular, Dorsal-attention, Language, Frontoparietal (FP), Auditory, Default, and Posterior-Multimodal. 5For each participant, visit, and state, the within-network covariance Ck

2.4. Longitudinal models and inference 

Baseline age and elapsed follow-up were represented separately so that cross-sectional age differences did not substitute for within-person change. Baseline age was centered at the cohort mean, and both baseline age and elapsed time from the first eligible visit were scaled in 5-year units. The fixed-effects design included baseline age, elapsed time, state, baseline-age ×state, elapsed-time ×state, sex, study, site–scanner, elapsed-time ×site, and elapsed-time ×site ×state terms. State and site interactions were sum coded. Fixed effects were estimated by generalized least squares (GLS), and uncertainty was estimated with a sandwich covariance aggregated at the pedigree level, so that related participants formed one cluster. The centered profile has nine independent dimensions. We represented it in nine orthonormal Helmert coordinates, which form an isometric log-ratio basis (Egozcue et al., 2003), and fitted these jointly with the same design and an unrestricted residual covariance across coordinates. Unless stated otherwise, intervals for state-specific slopes are Bonferroni simultaneous 95% intervals across the four states, and tests are two-sided with α = .05 .

2.8. Site, robustness, structure, and cognition 

Site dependence of the profile was evaluated with site ×time ×network and site ×time ×state ×network tests. From the pooled joint model, we extracted model-implied equal-state profiles for each site and correlated each with the mean profile of the other three, with simultaneous 95% intervals on the Fisher z scale. For the FP-relative contrast, we tested site ×time and site ×time ×state terms, estimated site-specific equal-state slopes, refitted the model omitting each site in turn, recomputed the contrast after omitting each reference network, and compared released partial covariance with native full covariance fitted to the same rows (848 participants, 2,210 sessions, 6,143 session–state rows), using FP-relative contrasts derived from the ten-network profile models for both. Network cortical thickness was the surface-area-weighted mean of parcel thickness (at least 95% parcel coverage), and FP-relative thickness was the FP value minus the mean of the other nine networks. Network volume and surface area were summed across parcels and log transformed, with volume expressed relative to estimated intracranial volume. FP-relative values were defined analogously. Structural trajectories were fitted with baseline age, elapsed time, sex, study, site–scanner, and site ×time terms and a participant random-intercept working covariance. To assess whether structure accounted for the fMRI trajectory, each participant’s FP-relative thickness was split into between-person (participant mean) and within-person (deviation) components. These were added, with their state interactions, to the FP-relative fMRI model. Adjusted and unadjusted four-state slope vectors were compared on identical rows with a Wald test of their difference, and the change was summarized as the percentage change in the Euclidean norm of the four slopes. The thickness-adjusted model was also refitted in the 822 participants scanned on a single scanner. For cognition, we used the Fluid Cognition Composite (FluidIQ). Visits were included when all four FP-relative fMRI values and FluidIQ were available at the same event, and participants were required to have at least two such 10 visits (746 participants, 1,881 visits). For each state, the FP-relative value was split into a participant mean and a within-person deviation. FluidIQ was regressed simultaneously on the four between-person and four within-person terms, adjusting for baseline age, elapsed time, sex, study, site–scanner, and site ×time, with a participant random-intercept working covariance and pedigree-robust inference. The primary test was the 4-df joint test of the within-person coefficients; between-person coefficients were tested jointly as context. Within-person coefficients were also expressed as standardized regression coefficients using the observed within-person SDs. Moderation by baseline age was assessed by adding baseline-age ×elapsed-time and baseline-age ×elapsed-time ×state terms (4 df for the FP-relative contrast; 9 df for the equal-state profile). 

3. Results 

3.1. Overall covariance and signal amplitude declined over follow-up 

The primary cohort included 852 adults with a mean baseline age of 60.96 years (SD 15.12; range 36–89), of whom 492 (57.7%) were women (Table 1). Participants contributed 2,220 imaging sessions and 8,727 analyzed session–state rows. Median follow-up was 5.50 years (IQR 3.10–6.33), and 431 participants had at least three sessions. Native common within-network covariance declined in all four states (Fig. 2A). Slopes were −0.105 log units per 5 years in REST, −0.068 in CARIT, −0.085 in FaceName, and −0.116 in visual–motor, corresponding to declines of approximately 7–11% per 5 years ( χ24 = 47 .69 , p = 1 .10 × 10 −9). −0.101 , and −0.110 log units per 5 years; χ24 = 26 .09 , Holm-adjusted p =9.1 × 10 −5; Fig. 2B), an equal-state decline of 9.4% per 5 years (simultaneous 95% interval 1.9–16.4%). The estimated noise pair-amplitude slope was small (equal-state −0.7% per 5 years, simultaneous 95% interval −5.0% to +3 .8% ;

χ24 = 2 .34 , p = .673 ). 3.2. Network-relative covariance reconfigured, with a recurrent frontoparietal-relative shift 

Network-level analyses used the signal-normalized (amplitude-standardized) relative profile RSTD . Section 3.3 examines which conclusions persist under an alternative normalization. The relative balance among the ten networks changed over follow-up ( χ29 = 43 .90 , p = 1 .47 × 10 −6), and the pattern of change differed across states ( χ227 = 112 .00 , p = 2 .55 × 10 −12 ; Fig. 3A). Averaged across states, FP had the most positive relative slope ( +0 .058 log units per 5 years), followed by Language ( +0 .037 ); Auditory ( −0.045 ) and Visual1 ( −0.042 ) had the most negative. Five of the 40 network–state slopes had Bonferroni simultaneous intervals excluding zero: FP in REST, CARIT, and visual–motor, and Auditory in CARIT and FaceName. The fitted profile also contracted modestly over 5 years (equal-state ratio of across-network SD 0.926, 95% multiplier interval 0.896–0.960; Table S4B and Fig. S2). The direct contrast between FP and the mean of the other nine networks was positive in every state: +0 .081 log units per 5 years in REST, +0 .063 

in CARIT, +0 .045 in FaceName, and +0 .070 in visual–motor (simultaneous 95% intervals 0.044–0.118, 0.023–0.102, 0.004–0.085, and 0.025–0.116), or approximately 5–8% per 5 years ( χ24 = 37 .08 , p = 1 .73 × 10 −7). This subset was younger than participants outside it (mean baseline age 60.1 versus 67.0 years) and had longer imaging follow-up (Table S13 and Fig. S9). 16 4. Discussion 

Within individuals followed for a median of 5.5 years, the relative balance of covariance among large-scale cortical networks changed over adult aging, and the frontoparietal network rose relative to the others in rest and in all three task states. This reconfiguration occurred alongside a decline in native covariance of roughly 7–11% per 5 years, which paralleled a decline in released signal amplitude. Estimated noise amplitude changed much less. Whether the common level of covariance declined depended on how amplitude was normalized, but the positive frontoparietal-relative direction did not. Prior cross-sectional work established that age differences in covariance-based connectivity are closely tied to BOLD amplitude (Strain et al., 2022). The present study extends that work longitudinally, across four brain states, and to the organization of networks relative to one another. The four-state design separates what recurred from what depended on state. The FP-relative slope was positive in every state, but its magnitude and the full ten-network profile differed across states, so recurrence applies to the direction of the frontoparietal shift rather than to an invariant network pattern. The equal-state point estimates were positive for FP and Language and negative for Auditory and Visual1, and FP’s own signal-normalized level increased before centering, so its relative rise was not produced only by declines elsewhere. Previous work has shown greater frontal recruitment in older adults during task performance (Davis et al., 2008) and flexible engagement of frontoparietal regions across many tasks (Cole et al., 2013). The present results add a within-person trajectory in frontoparietal covariance, measured relative to other networks, that recurs across both rest and task. FP cortical thickness also declined less than that of the other networks, and the functional trajectory persisted after adjustment for cortical thickness. These results should be read alongside reports of declining segregation in associative systems, including frontoparietal control networks (Chan et al., 2014; Malagurski et al., 2020). The measures differ: segregation contrasts within- and between-network connectivity, whereas the present profile com-pares within-network covariance across networks, so a relative increase in FP within-network covariance can coexist with declining FP segregation. The fitted profile also contracted modestly in three states, as networks starting low tended to rise and those starting high tended to fall. This partial convergence of network-relative levels is conceptually related to, but distinct from, neural dedifferentiation (Koen and Rugg, 2019). 17 The normalization results bear on how longitudinal connectivity find-ings are interpreted. Native covariance declined alongside released signal pair amplitude. The estimated common trajectory was near zero under sig-nal normalization but negative under candidate total normalization, and the two trajectories differed directly. That difference corresponded to a de-cline in signal pair amplitude relative to candidate total pair amplitude. A correlation-type measure, which retains noise variance in its denominator, can therefore show a common decline that a signal-normalized measure does not. The present longitudinal decline under total normalization differs from the comparative preservation of correlation reported cross-sectionally (Strain et al., 2022). The studies also differ in design, network aggregation, and denominator construction. BOLD amplitude reflects neurovascular as well as neuronal factors, both of which change with age (Liu, 2013; Tsvetanov et al., 2021). The present decomposition identifies which conclusions depend on amplitude normalization, not the physiological source of the amplitude decline. Network-level conclusions were more stable: both profile omnibus tests remained significant and FP-relative slopes remained positive in every state under both normalizations, while individual network–state estimates changed. Increased frontal recruitment or connectivity in older adults is often interpreted as compensation or scaffolding (Park and Reuter-Lorenz, 2009; Cabeza et al., 2018), an interpretation that requires a link to maintained or improved performance. Within-person coupling between the FP-relative measure and fluid cognition was not detected, although between-person associations were. The present data therefore establish the trajectory but not its function. Testing compensation directly will require repeated, task-relevant cognitive measurements that allow within-person associations to be estimated precisely. Several limitations define the scope of these conclusions. The analysis used released imaging-derived measures, so residual motion and session-level data quality could not be assessed directly, and total amplitude was reconstructed from released products. The signal-normalized measure is a network-level ratio, so changes in relative parcel amplitudes within a network can contribute to it. FP was characterized after the profile results in the same cohort, and its prominence should be confirmed in an independent sample. The detailed profile varied across sites, although the equal-state ordering and the positive FP-relative slope were consistent at every site. Finally, moderation by baseline age was not detected, so the reported effects are averages across the observed 18 age range. Adult aging, on this evidence, is not well described as uniform weakening of functional networks. Native covariance declines alongside signal pair amplitude, and whether a common decline remains depends on how amplitude is normalized. Beneath that overall change, the relative organization of networks shifts in a state-dependent way, with a positive frontoparietal-relative shift that recurs across rest and task.

S8. Cognition 

The repeated-cognition analysis included 746 participants (87.6% of the imaging cohort) with at least two visits containing both four-state FP-relative 16 fMRI and FluidIQ. The four within-person coefficients were not jointly dif-ferent from zero ( χ24 = 5 .63 , p = .228 ) and did not differ detectably across states ( χ23 = 4 .08 , p = .253 ). Standardized within-person coefficients ranged from −0.057 to +0 .040 , and all simultaneous intervals included zero (Ta-ble S13A; Fig. S9A). Between-person coefficients were jointly different from zero ( χ24 = 36 .58 , p = 2 .20 × 10 −7), with intervals excluding zero in REST and visual–motor. Participants in the repeated-cognition subset differed from the others mainly in longitudinal follow-up and age (Table S13B; Fig. S9B): they had more eligible fMRI visits (mean 2.61 versus 1.69), longer imaging follow-up (4.97 versus 2.23 years) and younger baseline age (60.1 versus 67.0 years), whereas the first equal-state FP-relative value differed little (standardized difference −0.09 ). FluidIQ variance among the 746 participants was 0.632 times that among the 87 participants with a single paired visit (bootstrap 95% interval 0.481–0.883).
