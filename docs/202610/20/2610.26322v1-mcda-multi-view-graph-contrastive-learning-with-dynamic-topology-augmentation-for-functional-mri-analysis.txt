MCDA: Multi-View Graph Contrastive Learning with Dynamic
Topology Augmentation for Functional MRI Analysis
Hao Zhang, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Xiaoyun Liu, Shuo Huang, Yue Ma,
Liang CaoB, Mingxia LiuB, Senior Member, IEEE, and Li ZhangB
Abstract—Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) is a vital non-invasive
tool for diagnosing brain disorders and uncovering patholog-
ical mechanisms. However, existing graph contrastive learning
(GCL) methods often underexploit augmentation histories across
training epochs and the relationships among different augmented
views. In this paper, we introduce a topology-guided Multi-
view graph Contrastive learning framework with Dynamic graph
topology Augmentation (MCDA) for fMRI analysis. Unlike tra-
ditional GCL that uses fixed noise or edge-dropping, MCDA
employs a Dynamic Augmentation Update (DAU) module that
iteratively refines the augmented topology. Specifically, we incor-
porate self-aware and cross-sample augmentation strategies to
simultaneously capture individual-specific functional signatures
and inter-subject heterogeneity. We then design a topology-guided
multi-view graph contrastive learning scheme that explores inter-
relationships not only between the original network and its aug-
mented views, but also across different augmented views to max-
imize structural information preservation. We characterize the
score-level optimization directions and establish a lower bound
for the contrastive objective. The framework combines classifi-
cation and contrastive losses for brain disorder diagnosis and
examines discriminative functional connectivity patterns as po-
tential biomarkers. Experiments on two public rs-fMRI datasets
comprising 1,236 subjects demonstrate that MCDA outperforms
the state-of-the-art methods. The code has been made publicly
available at: https://github.com/goodcodingagoodboy/MCDA.

Various graph-based learning methods have been developed

fMRI representation learning and brain disorder analysis [6],
[7]. By treating each brain network as a graph structure,
GCL typically generates different augmented views of a brain
network and then optimizes feature space via a contrastive loss,
thereby boosting robustness and accuracy in downstream tasks.
Beyond generating augmented views at the current training
step, we investigate how their evolution across epochs can
support history-guided augmentation refinement. Furthermore,
different augmented views are expected to express distinct
functional connectivities from brain networks, enabling a
comprehensive representation of topological structures. Nev-
ertheless, most existing GCL methods [6], [8], [9] fail to
model the interrelationships among augmented views and tend
to overlook feature expression between augmented networks.
Those limitations often affect discriminative power of graph
representation, ultimately affecting the accuracy and robust-

topology augmentation to continuously generate two com-
plementary brain networks, integrating self-aware and cross-
sample augmentation to model both subject-specific traits
and functional variability across individuals. Then, we pro-
pose a topology-guided multi-view graph contrastive learning
scheme, which can capture not only the interrelationships
between each augmented view and the original network but
also the interrelationships between different augmented views.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Graph Augmentation of FMRI-Derived Brain Networks
Graph augmentation generates diverse brain network views
to support robust representation learning from rs-fMRI data.
Existing techniques can largely be categorized as static epoch-
based augmentation strategies. Early representative meth-
ods [6], [10] perform graph augmentation directly on the
original BOLD time series using window slicing to generate
diverse training samples while preserving temporal continuity.
More recently, deep generative models have been employed to
produce diverse views that conform to the data distribution.
For instance, Wang et al. [11] integrated diffusion models with
graph augmentation, simultaneously modifying both the topo-
logical structure and node features to enhance brain network
representations. Li et al. [12] perform graph augmentation
via multi-view contrastive self-supervised learning, where a
learner-instructor framework enforces consistency and enables
self-updating to mine hidden topological connections from raw
fMRI data.
Augmentation strategies driven solely by current representa-
tions do not explicitly exploit the evolution of augmented views
across training epochs. In contrast, MCDA summarizes recent
augmentation histories using a shared BiGRU and adaptively
fuses current views with historical references through sigmoid
gating, enabling history-guided refinement beyond current-step
view generation.
B. Graph Contrastive Learning for fMRI analysis
Graph contrastive learning (GCL) analyzes relationships be-
tween augmented views to learn brain network representations


3
for rs-fMRI-based brain disorder diagnosis. By maximizing the
agreement between differently augmented views of the same
brain network, GCL encourages the model to capture invariant
and robust functional patterns [13]. Early works in this domain
primarily utilized single-view settings, focusing on temporal
or functional perturbations to project brain networks into
a discriminative feature space [14]. More recently, research
has shifted toward multi-view contrastive learning to leverage
diverse perspectives of brain connectivity. For instance, Li et
al. [12] developed a learner–instructor framework for unsuper-
vised brain topology inference, using multi-view contrastive
self-supervision to adaptively refine ROI interconnections.
Zong et al. [7] proposed an alternative multi-view approach
that computes similarity metrics at both node and graph level
perspectives between the original brain networks and their
graph diffusion variants, enabling simultaneous capture of
node and edge level features.
These studies illustrate the value of multi-view inputs and
multi-level representations in GCL. Nevertheless, constructing
multiple views does not by itself specify how consistency
and diversity should be balanced across augmented representa-
tions. Building on our preliminary multi-view formulation [9],
MCDA jointly regulates original to augmented and augmented
to augmented relationships. Rather than uniformly maximizing
agreement, it combines self-aware to original consistency with
relative cross-sample to original and two augmented views
diversity, encouraging complementary representations from
the two augmentation branches.
III. METHODOLOGY
In this work, we propose a topology-guided multi-view
graph contrastive learning framework with dynamic graph
topology augmentation (MCDA) for rs-fMRI-based brain dis-
order analysis. The framework is composed of three key
components: 1) brain network construction, 2) dynamic graph
topology augmentation, and 3) topology-guided multi-view
graph contrastive learning. An overview of the proposed
MCDA framework is presented in Fig. 1(a). More details are
elaborated below.
A. Brain Network Construction
To characterize brain dynamics and functional interactions,
we use rs-fMRI to construct an undirected spatio-temporal
brain network G and a functional connectivity matrix A [15].
For each subject, blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) sig-
nals from rs-fMRI represent an undirected spatio-temporal
network G = (V, E) ∈RT ×N. The vertex set V = {vti | t =
1, . . . T; i = 1, . . . , N} includes each ROI (region of interest)
at every time point t, where T denotes the number of time
points during scanning, and N is the number of brain regions.
The edge set E contains two subsets from both temporal
and spatial connectivity. For each subject, the functional
connectivity matrix A ∈RN×N is constructed by computing
Pearson correlations between the mean BOLD time series of
all ROI pairs.

C. Topology-guided Multi-view Graph Contrastive Learning
We introduce a topology-guided multi-view graph con-
trastive learning scheme to jointly constrain the relation-
ships among the original network and its two augmented
views. Specifically, the objective LTMC combines a consistency
constraint between the original network and the self-aware
augmented view with diversity constraints involving the cross-
sample augmented view. This asymmetric design assigns con-
sistency and diversity roles to the two augmentation branches.

IV. EXPERIMENT
A. Materials and Data Pre-processing
We evaluate the proposed MCDA framework on two
rs-fMRI datasets: 1) REST-meta-MDD Consortium (REST-
MDD1) [21] and 2) Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange
(ABIDE2) [22]. For each dataset, we use the top three largest
sites in the experiments. The demographic information of these
subjects from REST-MDD and ABIDE is provided in Table I.
1) REST-MDD. For the REST-MDD dataset, the three
largest sites are selected for analysis: Site 20, Site 21, and
Site 25, which respectively include 282 patients with major
depressive disorder (MDD) and 251 healthy controls (HCs);
86 MDD patients and 70 HCs; and 89 MDD patients and
63 HCs, with acquisition details provided in the original
dataset publication [21]. All rs-fMRI data from REST-MDD
were preprocessed using the DPARSF pipeline [23]. The first
10 volumes were discarded, followed by standard prepro-
cessing procedures including head motion correction, spatial
smoothing, normalization, band-pass filtering (0.01–0.10,Hz),
nuisance signal regression, and spatial standardization.
2) ABIDE. In ABIDE, we use three sites, including Site
NYU with 79 ASD and 105 HC subjects, Site UM with 55
ASD and 55 HC subjects, and Site USM with 58 ASD and 43
HC subjects, with acquisition details provided in the original
dataset publication [22]. The rs-fMRI data were preprocessed
based on the DPARSF pipeline [23].
For both datasets, the brain was parcellated into 116 ROIs
using the original Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL)
atlas [24], and the mean BOLD time series was extracted from
each ROI. The repetition time (TR) was 2000 ms at all six
sites. For each subject, the final model input consisted of 200
time points from 116 ROIs, yielding G ∈R200×116.
B. Experiment Setting
In our experiments, we perform five-fold cross-validation
independently at each site using random subject-level splits.
In each fold, one subset is held out for testing, while the
remaining data are split into training and validation sets at
an 80:20 ratio for model fitting and selection, respectively.
All competing methods are retrained using identical data
partitions. Model selection is based solely on validation per-
formance. Results are reported as the mean and standard
deviation across the five test folds. The evaluation metrics
include accuracy (ACC), area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUC), precision (PRE), and recall (REC).
We compare MCDA with the following six SOTA methods
for fMRI analysis. Specifically, two methods (i.e., BrainGNN3
and LCAF4) are advanced rs-fMRI analysis approaches, fol-
lowed by augmentation-based approaches (i.e., TSDA5 and
GCDA) that enrich brain network representations. The remain-
ing two methods (i.e., UCGL6 and MCS-GL) adopt graph
contrastive learning from different views for classification.
1) BrainGNN [4]: This is a graph neural network designed
for analyzing fMRI and detecting neurological biomarkers.
With the brain network/graph as input, BrainGNN uses two
node-level graph convolutional layers to learn the node repre-
sentations, capturing topological and functional patterns from
fMRI data.
2) LCAF [25]: Kang et al. propose a unified FC-based
framework integrating feature selection, feature extraction, and
interpretation to reduce error propagation across stages. An
adaptive attention network selects subject-specific, disease-
related connections, while a functional network relational
encoder models inter-network relationships to capture global
topology without predefined edges.
3) TSDA [10]: In our implementation, TSDA generates
two augmented sequences by extracting the first and last
90% of each ROI’s mean BOLD time series. Subsequent
processing follows the same pipeline as MCDA for brain
disorder classification.
4) GCDA [11]: GCDA combines graph diffusion augmen-
tation with two shared-parameter graph isomorphism networks
for contrastive pretraining, followed by supervised fine-tuning.
We adapt this two-stage procedure to our five-fold protocol:
pretraining uses the training subjects without labels, while fine-
tuning uses the same subjects with their diagnostic labels.
5) UCGL [6]: UCGL employs dual-stage fMRI augmen-
tation and two parallel ST-GCNs for contrastive pretraining,
followed by supervised fine-tuning. We replace the fixed
60%/20%/20% allocation with our fold-wise partitions, using
the training subjects for both stages and their labels only
during fine-tuning. For GCDA and UCGL methods, validation
subjects are used only for model selection, and test subjects
are reserved for final evaluation.

C. Classification Results
The quantitative results of MCDA and the six competing
methods for MDD vs. HC classification on the REST-MDD
dataset and ASD vs. HC classification on the ABIDE dataset
are reported in Table II and Table III, respectively, under
within-site five-fold cross-validation. We perform two-sided
paired t-tests on the ACC values of MCDA and each com-
peting method obtained from the same five outer test folds
(n = 5 pairs; degrees of freedom = 4). “*” denotes a nominal
p < 0.05 for the difference in ACC between MCDA and
the corresponding method. From these tables, we have the
following observations.
First, compared to BrainGNN and LCAF, MCDA shows
improved discriminative power. For instance, MCDA achieves
ACC improvements of 6.7 and 6.6 percentage points over
BrainGNN on Site 20 and Site 21 of REST-MDD, respec-
tively. The possible reason is that topology-guided multi-
view contrastive learning effectively aligns multiple views of
brain connectivity by preserving topology semantics, enabling
invariant and discriminative representation learning across
views.
Second, compared with the two augmentation-based meth-
ods, TSDA and GCDA, MCDA achieves higher mean ACC
and AUC at each evaluated site. On Site NYU of ABIDE,
MCDA exceeds TSDA by 12.8 percentage points in ACC and
13.6 percentage points in AUC. The performance gap suggests
that simple window-slicing augmentation may be insuﬀicient
to capture the complex functional patterns needed for ASD
diagnosis. In contrast, MCDA’s dynamic topology augmen-
tation maintains the brain’s complete structures, allowing its
contrastive learning to capture both localized dysfunctions and
global network characteristics.
Additionally, MCDA achieves higher mean ACC and AUC
than the graph contrastive learning methods UCGL and MCS-
GL at each evaluated site. Specifically, for MDD diagnosis on
Site 25, MCDA exceeds UCGL by 10.1 percentage points in
ACC and 15.8 percentage points in AUC. These results support
the effectiveness of dynamic augmentation and topology-
guided multi-view contrastive learning for within-site brain
disorder classification.

V. DISCUSSION
A. Exploratory Analysis of Brain Region-Level Associations
We analyze brain region-level associations in the self-
aware and cross-sample augmentation features of held-out
test subjects from Site 20 of REST-MDD and Site NYU of
ABIDE separately. Features are extracted using frozen models
trained and selected without the corresponding test subjects.
For each subject and view, ROI temporal-by-channel features
are centered over time, and linear centered kernel alignment
(CKA) [26] is used to construct a 116×116 association matrix.
For each ROI pair, the two view-specific scores form a two-
dimensional response in a multivariate linear model adjusted
for prespecified demographic and head-motion covariates.
Diagnostic-group effects are evaluated using Pillai’s trace and
Freedman–Lane residual permutations [27]. Covariate-only
model residuals are permuted jointly across subjects for both
views and added back to the fitted values before refitting, using
identical permutations for all ROI pairs. Joint p-values are
corrected across the 6,670 unique ROI pairs within each site
using the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure [28]. Among ROI
pairs with BY-adjusted p-values below 0.05, up to ten pairs
with the smallest joint p-values are visualized using BrainNet
Viewer [29], with line thickness representing Pillai’s trace. The
MDD–HC and ASD–HC patterns are shown in Fig. 4(a) and
(b), respectively.
As shown in Fig. 4(a), the visualized connections for the
MDD–HC comparison involve the bilateral insula (INS.L
and INS.R), right middle frontal gyrus (MFG.R), and right
supplementary motor area (SMA.R). Previous studies have
associated insula network connectivity with depressive symp-
toms in MDD [30] and reported altered structural–functional
coupling of the right SMA [31]. For the ASD–HC compar-

propagation in DAU module effectively constrains and adjusts

the cross-sample augmented networks demonstrate more sub-

ROIs located in the upper-right and lower-left regions.

E. Parameter Analysis
Site 20 of REST-MDD was used as the development site.
Hyperparameters were selected using validation AUC from the
five-fold partitions of this site. The selected configuration was
subsequently fixed for all site-wise experiments. The reported
results reveal performance on the held-out test folds for the
evaluated parameter settings.
1) Influence of Trade-off TMC Loss Parameters: To ex-
amine the effects of trade-off parameters in topology-guided
multi-view contrastive loss LTMC of Eq. (13), we adjust the
values of α and β. The parameter α, which regulates the
contribution of self-aware and cross-sample augmentation, is
varied from 0 to 1. Meanwhile, β, reflecting the impact of
differences between augmented brain networks, is tested at
0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10. Fig. 7 presents a subset of the ac-
curacy and AUC results, which indicates that MCDA achieves
optimal performance when α = 0.3, β = 1. Furthermore, we
investigate how the TMC loss LTMC, scaled by the trade-
off parameter λ, affects the classification performance. The
corresponding results on Site 20 are represented in Fig. 8, and
MCDA ultimately achieves its optimum at λ = 0.5. Using large
values for λ, our framework cannot achieve good results. This
implies that excessive weighting of the TMC loss causes the
framework to overly focus on the relative distances between
multiple augmented views, leading to a less compact feature
embedding space and consequently impairing classification
performance.

F. Limitation and Future Work
Several limitation of current work need to be considered. On
one hand, the brain network construction relies solely on rs-
fMRI. In the future, we will integrate multi-modal data (e.g.,
diffusion tensor imaging [36], demographic factors [37] and
clinical scores [38]) to explore complementary relationships
between imaging and none-imaging patterns. On the other
hand, our application scenario is limited to data collected
from a single site, overlooking the potential heterogeneity
caused by differences in acquisition machine parameters across
multiple sites in real-world settings. A possible solution is to
employ domain adaption to reduce cross-domain distribution
difference [39], which will be one of our future work.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper introduces a topology-guided multi-view graph
contrastive learning framework (MCDA) with dynamic topol-


12
ogy augmentation for brain disorder diagnosis and analysis.
In our design, two complementary brain networks are gener-
ated through a dynamic augmentation update (DAU) module,
incorporating both self-aware and cross-sample strategies to
capture subject-specific traits and functional heterogeneity
across individuals. In addition, we develop a topology-aware
multi-view contrastive learning paradigm that simultaneously
models the interrelationships between the original graph and
its augmented counterparts, as well as between the augmented
views themselves. We further characterize the optimization
directions of the contrastive constraints through similarity-
score gradient analysis and establish a lower bound for the con-
trastive objective. Experiments on two public rs-fMRI datasets
demonstrate improved classification performance compared
with several state-of-the-art methods.
