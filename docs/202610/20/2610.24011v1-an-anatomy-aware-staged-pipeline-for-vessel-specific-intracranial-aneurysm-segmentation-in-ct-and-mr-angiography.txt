Abstract. The TopAneu 2026 challenge requires intracranial aneurysms to be segmented at the voxel level and assigned to one of more than fifty vessel-specific classes, on CT and MR angiography alike. We ad-dress it with a six-stage anatomy-aware pipeline rather than a single segmentation network. A logistic-regression classifier routes each scan to a modality-specific branch; networks segment the vasculature, first as a binary mask and then over the full 36-class arterial taxonomy; a can-didate localiser turns raw angiography and vessel priors into suspicious regions; a candidate-centred network segments the lesion in each crop; connected components become instances; and a per-instance classifier assigns each its anatomical location. Two principles organise the design. Whenever an upstream model output becomes a downstream training input it is generated out-of-fold, so no stage trains on unrealistically clean predictions of itself. And the anatomical decision is made once per lesion rather than once per voxel, which fits the data: of 406 connected aneurysm components in the full release, 405 carry exactly one location label. Task 1 is read off the Task 2 mask, making the two consistent by construction. We report stage-wise performance and a five-part ablation isolating what vessel priors and class-aware sampling contribute at each stage. 

Keywords: Intracranial aneurysm · Vessel-specific segmentation · CT angiography · MR angiography · Cascaded segmentation · Anatomical priors. 

## 1 Introduction 

Unruptured intracranial aneurysms occur in roughly 3% of adults, and the de-cision to treat or observe rests on a few anatomical descriptors. Deep-learning aneurysm detection on TOF-MRA and CTA is well established [3] and the ADAM challenge [7] provided a reproducible benchmark, but all treat the target as one undifferentiated lesion class. Anatomical labelling has been pursued separately: TopCoW introduced topology-aware segmentation of the circle of Willis [9] and TopBrain extended it to the whole arterial tree [10]. TopAneu 2026 [6] is the first benchmark to require both jointly, over more than fifty vessel-location classes with laterality. Posing this as one fifty-way segmentation problem is unattractive. Aneurysm voxels are already a vanishing fraction of an angiographic volume, and divid-ing that foreground across fifty long-tailed classes leaves most locations with a handful of instances; the evidence separating them is also of the wrong kind for a segmentation network, since what distinguishes a left from a right M1 aneurysm is global anatomical position. We therefore decompose the task into the staged pipeline of Fig. 1, each stage operating at its own granularity and consuming only what is available at test time. Cascading a coarse anatomical stage into a fine lesion stage is established practice [1]; what is specific here is that the inter-mediate anatomy is itself the label space of the final decision. Our contributions are the pipeline, a strict out-of-fold discipline for every learned prior, and an ablation isolating what vessel anatomy and class-aware sampling contribute. 

## 2 Methods 

2.1 Data and the out-of-fold discipline 

The TopAneu release provides multi-centre CTA and MRA with aneurysm masks carrying a vessel-location class, type masks, silver-standard vessel masks over a 36-class arterial taxonomy, and per-case location listings [6]. We used the pilot release of 98 cases (58 CTA, 40 MRA, 50 classes) and the full release of 417 — An Anatomy-Aware Staged Pipeline for Aneurysm Segmentation 3

109 CTA, all positive, and 308 MRA of which 197 are positive — which refines the taxonomy to 52 classes. Vessel masks are released for training only, so every stage that consumes ves-sel anatomy must consume a prediction rather than the annotation. Cases are split into five outer folds — stratified by aneurysm positivity for the aneurysm datasets, count-balanced for the vessel datasets — and each outer fold’s remain-der into 80/20 inner training and validation. We apply one rule throughout: whenever an upstream model output becomes a downstream training input, it is generated out-of-fold. Any prior consumed by a later stage therefore comes from the model whose outer fold excluded that case, and no stage is trained on predictions of itself. 

2.2 Stage 1: modality routing 

Modality is not guaranteed to be known at inference, so it is inferred from the image. A logistic-regression classifier over global image and header statistics — volume shape, voxel spacing, physical size, intensity moments and robust per-centiles, with no filename information — routes each scan, and every subsequent stage is duplicated per modality. 

2.3 Stage 2: vessel segmentation 

Each branch segments the vasculature twice: a binary model and a multiclass model over the 36-class taxonomy. The binary output is a cheap, robust prior; the multiclass output carries the anatomical identity attribution needs. Whether the first helps the second is ablation A: A0 predicts the multiclass mask from raw angiography alone, A1 from raw plus the out-of-fold binary prediction as a second channel. All segmentation stages are 3D full-resolution U-Nets configured per dataset by a self-configuring framework [4], trained with deep supervision and a com-pound Dice and cross-entropy objective; branches resample to their own median spacings, 0.25 × 0.43 × 0.43 mm for CTA and 0.55 × 0.30 × 0.30 mm for MRA. The vessel taxonomy is itself long-tailed: in the MRA split 3rd-A2 occurs in 33 of 197 training cases and 3rd-A3 in 55, and several classes common by case presence occupy very small voxel volumes and are learned poorly. Ablation R evaluates class-aware patch sampling. Standard nnU-Net centres 33% of patches on foreground; the class-aware variant raises this to 75% and, with probability 0.80, centres on one of seven small or weakly learned labels drawn uniformly, so roughly 60% of patches target them. Since this changes both the foreground rate and the class selection, R separates them: default sampling (R0), the raised fraction alone (R1), and the raised fraction with targeted selection (R2). 

2.4 Stages 3–4: candidate localisation and crop segmentation 

Rather than segmenting across the whole volume, we first localise candidates. A detector predicts a candidate map from raw angiography and vessel priors, 4 G. Drwiega et al. 

and connected components of the thresholded map become bounding boxes. This raises recall and cuts the foreground/background imbalance that loss reweighting alone does not fully remove. Ablation B varies its inputs: raw only (B0), plus binary vessel (B1), plus multiclass (B2), plus both (B3). A second network segments the lesion inside each crop, where the aneurysm occupies a far larger fraction of the field of view. Ablation S varies the crop channels from raw alone (S0) through binary vessel (S1), multiclass (S2), both (S3), and both plus the candidate map (S4); predictions are merged back into full image space. Targets are class-collapsed to one foreground label at both stages, so every lesion contributes to a single detection objective. 

2.5 Stages 5–6: instances, location and task outputs 

Connected components of the merged mask become aneurysm instances, which matters because a case may carry several. The data support this granularity: the full release contains 406 components across 306 positive cases, of which 405 carry exactly one location label, so attribution is well posed as one decision per instance. Each instance is described by the raw crop, its instance mask, the binary and multiclass vessel crops, and geometric features: centre of mass, volume, bounding-box size, overlap with vessel labels, a histogram of neighbouring labels, and modality. Ablation L removes these in turn, from no vessel information (L0) through binary features (L1) and the multiclass label histogram (L2) to the full crop-plus-parcellation representation (L3). This matters because the label spaces diverge at the junctions, where M1 trunk, M1 bifurcation and M1–M2 junction share a parent vessel and are separated only by position along it. The Task 2 mask is produced by filling each instance with its predicted location identifier, and Task 1 is the set of unique non-zero labels in that mask.

## 4 Discussion and Conclusion 

The pipeline is organised around one principle: each sub-problem is solved at the granularity at which it is well posed. Modality is an image-level property; vessels and lesions are voxel-level structures; anatomical location belongs to a lesion rather than a voxel, and the data agree — 405 of 406 components carry a single label, so attribution is a per-instance decision over a few hundred examples. The second principle is the out-of-fold discipline. In a pipeline this deep, four model outputs become training inputs downstream, and reusing folds across that boundary inflates estimates in the way documented for non-nested model selection [8,5]. One rule everywhere costs an extra prediction pass per fold and buys estimates that do not collapse when the stages are assembled end to end. The ablations answer a question the pilot results raise but cannot settle: whether anatomical information helps because it localises the search or because it identifies the vessel. B tests the first at the detector, L the second at attribu-tion, A whether the cheap binary prior earns its place as input to the expensive multiclass one, and R the same of sampling. The pilot evidence already con-strains the answer — lesions are missed even when lying almost entirely inside an accurate vessel mask — which is why stages 3–4 change the field of view rather than adding another prior. In summary, we presented a six-stage anatomy-aware pipeline for the TopAneu 2026 tasks, with every learned prior generated out-of-fold and attribution posed per lesion instance. 6 G. Drwiega et al.
