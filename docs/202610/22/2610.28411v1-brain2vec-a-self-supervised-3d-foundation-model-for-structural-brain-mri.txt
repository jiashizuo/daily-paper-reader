Abstract
Deep learning models have become essential tools in medical imaging, yet training
them end-to-end for diverse tasks remains challenging due to limited labeled data
and computational constraints. To address this, we introduce Brain2Vec, a foun-
dation model pre-trained on 74,425 T1-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging
(MRI) scans pooled from multiple studies. Brain2Vec can serve both as a weight
initialization backbone for fine-tuning downstream tasks and as a standalone fea-
ture extractor. We evaluated Brain2Vec in three tasks: downstream predictive
performance, convergence speed, and embedding quality. Using Brain2Vec’s pre-
trained weights as initialization and fine-tuning on labeled data for a brain age
prediction task, results demonstrate improved final accuracy and faster conver-
gence compared to random initialization. Additionally, its frozen embeddings
outperform anatomically defined volumetric features on four of five downstream
classification tasks and perform competitively with a state-of-the-art foundation
model pre-trained on substantially more data. Lastly, we show that Brain2Vec
encodes information primarily from a spatially localized and neuroanatomically
meaningful set of brain regions, and that manipulating a subset of latent fea-
tures produces monotonic, region-specific effects on the reconstructed anatomy.
Brain2Vec code and trained models are available as an open-source package
on https://github.com/CBICA/Brain2Vec. Users can apply Brain2Vec lo-
cally by installing the package, or directly through the NiChart cloud platform
https://cloud.neuroimagingchart.com, where Brain2Vec features can be
derived without requiring local installation or specialized infrastructure.

Introduction
Traditional machine learning approaches for deriving imaging biomarkers from structural Magnetic
Resonance Imaging (MRI) typically rely on a predefined feature extraction step, such as segmentation
or parcellation of regional anatomy, followed by supervised modeling on the resulting measurements
[12, 3]. These pipelines have supported a wide range of neuroimaging applications, including brain
age estimation and disease prediction [34], but they depend on predefined anatomical segmentations
and task-specific model development.
Deep learning (DL) has made it possible to learn predictive representations directly from images,
potentially bypassing hand-engineered feature pipelines. In practice, however, training end-to-end
models for specific neuroimaging tasks poses significant challenges. MRI volumes are substantially

more computationally demanding than 2D natural images, with 3D volume-based training incurring
considerable memory and computational costs [7, 39]. At the same time, neuroimaging datasets are
typically small, labeled datasets are limited, and class distributions are often imbalanced, contrasting
sharply with the large-scale labeled datasets available in computer vision, such as ImageNet [10]
with over 14 million images for 2D classification. These constraints hinder the development of robust
task-specific deep models and motivate the need for transferable representations learned from large,
heterogeneous MRI collections.
Self-supervised pre-training offers a practical way to mitigate these limitations by learning transferable
image representations from large collections of unlabeled scans [23, 21]. Rather than optimizing
directly for a single downstream target, self-supervised models are trained using a generic pretext
objective and can then be adapted to classification or regression tasks using substantially smaller
labeled datasets. This paradigm is especially appealing in neuroimaging, where large-scale imaging
repositories are increasingly available, but task-specific annotations remain expensive, heterogeneous,
and often scarce.
In this work, we present Brain2Vec, a self-supervised foundation model pre-trained on 74,425
T1-weighted MRI scans pooled from multiple studies within the iSTAGING harmonized dataset
[20]. The training data span a broad range of scanner hardware, acquisition protocols, and clinical
populations, allowing the model to learn a high-dimensional representation of neuroanatomy from
diverse real-world data. Brain2Vec produces a 768-dimensional embedding for each scan, which
can be used in two complementary ways: as initialization for downstream fine-tuning, or as a frozen
feature representation for supervised prediction.
Validation experiments demonstrate that Brain2Vec improves both predictive performance and
convergence speed when used for encoder fine-tuning. When evaluated as a feature extractor,
Brain2Vec outperformed SPARE-BA, a brain age model trained on regional volumes derived from
DLMUSE [3] anatomical segmentation, across all tasks except a single AD prediction task. It also
outperformed NeuroJEPA [22], a foundation model pre-trained on 1.55M scans (683, 860 T1w), on
multiple classification and brain age prediction tasks. This result is particularly notable given that
Brain2Vec was pre-trained on ≈9.2× less data. This suggests that learning a strong representation
of brain structure may not require pretraining at the scale of millions of scans. Unlike natural-image
datasets, where visual content varies enormously across scenes, objects, and viewpoints, preprocessed
structural brain MRI is a comparatively low-variance domain: scans are skull-stripped and registered
to a common atlas space, so the variability a model must capture is largely confined to inter-individual
anatomical differences, including aging and disease related changes, and acquisition effects. Together,
these results establish Brain2Vec as an effective self-supervised backbone for structural neuroimaging
analysis.
Contributions.
This work makes the following contributions:
1. First, we introduce Brain2Vec, a self-supervised 3D masked autoencoder trained on 74,425
T1-weighted MRI scans drawn from multiple studies, with the goal of learning transferable
representations of brain structure.
2. Second, we demonstrate that pretrained Brain2Vec weights serve as a stronger initialization
for downstream task-specific applications than random initialization. We illustrate this here
through brain age prediction, where we observe improvements in both convergence speed
and final accuracy.
3. Third, we show that frozen Brain2Vec embeddings support competitive lightweight down-
stream modeling. They outperform segmentation-based volumetric features on four of five
disease and risk-factor classification tasks, performing competitively with NeuroJEPA [22],
while achieving strong brain age prediction performance.
4. Last, we perform an extensive analysis of Brain2Vec latent embeddings, showing that the
model encodes information primarily from a spatially localized and neuroanatomically
meaningful set of brain regions. We prove that manipulating a subset of latent features pro-
duces monotonic, region-specific effects on the reconstructed anatomy, which is important
for the interpretability of disease or aging related effects.

Related Works
Structural neuroimaging has a long tradition of characterizing brain morphology through
segmentation-based pipelines, in which anatomically defined regions of interest (ROIs) are de-
lineated and summarized as volumetric, morphometric, or thickness-based features, encompassing
measures such as regional volumes, cortical thickness, surface area, and lesion or abnormality loads
[15, 14, 12, 17, 4]. Imaging features are used in downstream pipelines as inputs to conventional
machine learning models for tasks such as disease classification, brain age prediction, and biomarker
discovery [19, 8, 34, 25]. This paradigm has driven substantial progress in neuroimaging research,
enabling the discovery of imaging biomarkers and the development of predictive models across a
wide range of conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease, brain aging, and psychiatric disorders. How-
ever, its reliance on a-priori anatomical definitions and task-specific model development inherently
constrains its generalizability across diverse clinical applications.
Self-supervised learning has proven particularly well-suited to settings where labeled data are limited
but unlabeled scans are abundant. In computer vision, Vision Transformers (ViTs) showed that
images can be modeled effectively as sequences of patches, while masked autoencoders (MAEs)
demonstrated that reconstructing heavily masked inputs can yield scalable and transferable visual
representations [13, 21].
In medical imaging, recent work has highlighted the promise of self-supervised pre-training for
improving downstream predictive performance and sample efficiency across classification and seg-
mentation tasks [23]. Several methods originally developed for computer vision have since been
adapted to medical and neuroimaging datasets. Among these, masked autoencoders have demon-
strated improvements in medical image classification and segmentation, including in 3D settings
where labeled data are particularly costly to obtain [39, 7]. Specifically, in brain MRI analysis, domain-
tailored self-supervised pre-training has been shown to enhance downstream diagnostic models, and
recent work has begun exploring large-scale brain-specific foundation models for neuroimaging tasks
[24, 9, 36, 22].

Materials and method
3.1
Datasets
The pre-training dataset comprised 74,425 T1-weighted MRI scans pooled from multiple studies
within the iSTAGING consortium [20]. While the majority of scans were from cognitively unimpaired
subjects, the dataset also included individuals with MCI and dementia, enabling the model to capture
disease-related neuroanatomical changes. We expect that including diseased groups in pre-training
plays a beneficial role in downstream tasks presented in Section 4 for disease classification. A detailed
breakdown of pre-training datasets and sample characteristics is provided in Table 1. The dataset
was stratified by diagnosis and partitioned into 80% training, 10% validation and 10% test set at the
participant level, so that all longitudinal scans from a given participant were assigned to the same set.
For brain age prediction, we used 19, 671 cognitively unimpaired baseline scans from the iSTAGING
consortium, spanning 13 studies (Supplementary Table 7). To ensure a balanced age distribution
across splits, scans were stratified into 10-year age bins and partitioned into 60% for training, 30%
for validation, and 10% for testing. For out-of-distribution validation, we used the MIRIAD [29]
dataset, comprising baseline T1 MRI scans from 46 mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s subjects and 23
cognitively normal controls.
For disease classification, we used 21,176 scans from the iSTAGING consortium, spanning 15 studies
(Supplementary Tables 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12), covering six diagnostic and risk-factor categories:
cognitively unimpaired (CU), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), diabetes, elevated BMI, smoking status,
and hypertension. To mitigate inter-site variability, data from multiple studies were balanced across

3.2
Architecture
Brain2Vec is a 3D Masked Autoencoder [21] with an assymetric encoder-decoder design (Fig. 1).
Feature extraction.
A preprocessed volume x ∈R128×128×128 is split into N = 163 = 4096
non-overlapping 8 × 8 × 8 patches {xp}N
p=1, each flattened to a 512-dimensional vector and linearly
projected to the encoder width d = 768. A learnable [CLS] token is prepended to the sequence.
Tokenization.
The encoder is a 12-layer ViT [13] with a hidden size of 768, 12 attention heads, and
an MLP ratio of 4. A random subset (75%) of patches are masked and the encoder only processes a
small subset of visible patches (25%), without incorporating mask tokens [11].
Decoder.
The decoder is an 8-layer ViT with an input dimensionality of d = 512 and 16 attention
heads. The encoder’s outputs are first projected to the required dimensionality and a shared learnable
mask token is inserted at every masked position, followed by positional embeddings. The decoder
processes the full sequence of tokens and a linear head predicts the 8 × 8 × 8 = 512 voxel values of
each patch. In all previous work, the decoder is not used for feature extraction but only during pre-
training and discarded afterwards. We proposed an approach on using the decoder for interpretability
(Sec. 4.3).
Objective.
Let ˆxp denote the predicted voxels of patch p. The model is trained to minimize the
mean squared error over masked patches only:

Figure 1: Overview of the Brain2Vec pre-training architecture and downstream applications.
(Pre-training) Brain2Vec is a 3D Masked Autoencoder (MAE) pre-trained on 74,425 T1-weighted
MRI scans from the iSTAGING consortium. Input volumes are divided into 8 × 8 × 8 patches, with
75% randomly masked. A 12-layer Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder processes only the visible
patches to generate a 768-dimensional latent representation. Mask tokens are then introduced, and an
8-layer ViT decoder reconstructs the original scan. (Evaluation) The pre-trained encoder serves two
complementary roles. First, it provides a robust weight initialization for end-to-end fine-tuning on a
downstream task (brain age regression), accelerating convergence and improving accuracy relative
to random initialization. Second, its frozen 768-dimensional embeddings serve as fixed features for
lightweight downstream models, including a Support Vector Regressor (SVR) for brain age regression
and SVMs for five binary classification tasks: CU vs. AD, elevated BMI, diabetes, smoking status,
and hypertension. We additionally assess the quality and interpretability of these frozen embeddings
via self-attention visualization, correlation with anatomically-defined Regions of Interest (ROIs), and
latent-space manipulation, showing that Brain2Vec encodes spatially localized, neuroanatomically
meaningful representations.
Embedding extraction.
After pre-training, the decoder is discarded. We freeze the encoder
weights and pass all the patches without any masking. The final embedding is obtained from the
[CLS] token, though obtaining the final embedding using mean pooling is also a valid option and is
used in other recent foundation models like NeuroJEPA.

the foundation model nature of this work, the training set was intentionally maximized to expose
the model to as much diverse data as possible during pre-training, as broader data coverage is
critical for learning generalizable representations. Brain2Vec was trained for 500 epochs with an

(β ∼υ(−0.3, 0.3)), RandomFlip (lateral axis only), RandomNoise (mean=0, std=0), and affine

3.4
Evaluation
The pre-trained Brain2Vec model was evaluated on six downstream tasks including: (i) five classifica-
tion tasks (CU vs. AD, elevated BMI, diabetes, smoking status and hypertension) and (ii) a brain age
prediction task, under two complementary configurations:
• Encoder fine-tuning: The pre-trained encoder weights were used as initialization for a
Vision Transformer (ViT) applied to a brain age prediction task. This approach preserves
the original ViT architecture, allowing the downstream model to leverage the rich structural
representations acquired during pre-training.
• Feature extraction: MRI scans were passed through the frozen pre-trained encoder to
extract 768-dimensional embeddings (Brain2Vec features), which subsequently served
as fixed input representations for lightweight downstream models using Support Vector
Machines (SVMs).
We compared fine-tuning from Brain2Vec pretrained weights against training from random ini-
tialization, measuring both final accuracy and convergence speed. In the feature extraction set-
ting, we compared downstream predictive performance against SPARE-BA, a model trained using
segmentation-based regional volumetric features and using features extracted from NeuroJEPA,
a state-of-the-art foundation model, providing a direct assessment of Brain2Vec relative to both
traditional and more recent neuroimaging feature pipelines.

4.2
Feature extraction
4.2.1
Brain age prediction
We trained a Support Vector Regressor (SVR) on frozen Brain2Vec encoder embeddings to predict
brain age. The rationale is that an effectively pretrained encoder that captures neuroanatomically
relevant structure should enable a simple non-parametric model to generalize effectively to age
prediction from fixed embeddings alone, without any backbone fine-tuning. We used 5-fold cross-
validation for data splitting, with hyperparameters tuned via Optuna [1] using nested 5-fold cross-
validation on the training folds, optimizing for R2 score. Splits were randomly sampled.
Our results (Table 3, Fig. 5) demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves performance comparable
to fully supervised ViT regressors, despite its simplicity, suggesting that Brain2Vec embeddings
capture rich structural information relevant to brain aging. Notably, the SVR model trained on
Brain2Vec features outperformed SPARE-BA, a validated and established brain age estimation
method trained on volumetric features from anatomical segmentation using a pre-defined atlas [19],
and performed comparable to SVR models trained on NeuroJEPA embeddings. This suggests that
Brain2Vec features encode structural information that is at least as informative as anatomically defined,
handcrafted regional volume features. We also evaluated the utility of brain age gap (predicted age
−actual age) to capture disease related or risk-factor effects on multiple disease-specific datasets
described in Sec. 3.1. The brain age gap from Brain2Vec-SVR model showed significant group
8


differences in AD (Cohen’s d = 0.94, p < 0.001) and elevated BMI (Cohen’s d = 0.43, p < 0.001),
outperforming ViT-BA model on this measure. Results are reported in supplementary Fig. 8.
Validations on the external IXI dataset were performed using the same SVR models trained in the
cross-validation folds, without any additional training or fine-tuning. This approach was chosen
to directly assess the generalization performance of the learned Brain2Vec feature space without
any additional model adaptation, consistent with the frozen feature extraction paradigm. The same
inference procedure was applied to SPARE-BA models trained on DLMUSE-derived features and
NeuroJEPA-SVR trained on NeuroJEPA embeddings, ensuring a fair comparison between approaches
under identical conditions. For the IXI inference, we combined predictions from all 5-fold SVR
models into an ensemble, as this approach typically yields more robust and stable predictions by
averaging out individual model variances. The results of the IXI inference are included in Table 3
and Fig. 5. The SVR model fitted on Brain2Vec embeddings yielded the best results, confirming that
Brain2Vec has learned a generalizable representation of brain morphology that serves as a compact
yet informative feature space for efficient downstream tasks.

4.2.2
Disease and risk-factor classification
Following the same protocol as the brain age prediction experiments, we trained SVM classifiers
on our disease classification dataset explained in Sec. 3.1. We used 5-fold cross-validation, with
hyperparameters tuned via Optuna [1] through nested 5-fold cross-validation on each training set,
optimizing for F1 score. Splits were stratified by disease or risk-factor.
Despite the encoder being trained without any disease supervision, the learned representations yielded
strong classification performance across all disease groups, indicating that Brain2Vec captures
clinically meaningful morphological structure beyond its explicit training objective. Compared to
SPARE models trained on DLMUSE-derived features, our approach outperformed across all tasks in
terms of accuracy, AUC, F1 score, recall and precision, except for AD classification, where DLMUSE
retained a clear advantage. Compared to NeuroJEPA, Brain2Vec performed better on diabetes and
smoking classification, while NeuroJEPA performed better on AD and elevated BMI classification,
with comparable results on hypertension (Table 4).

4.3
Interpretability of Brain2Vec’s Learned Representations
In medical imaging, there is a growing need for interpretable models, as they enable researchers and
clinicians to understand how a model makes predictions and which features are important for each task
[37]. We assessed Brain2Vec’s interpretability through three complementary strategies: (i) visualizing
self-attention maps to examine which spatial regions the model attends to, (ii) correlating latent
embeddings with anatomically-defined regions of interest to identify which brain structures drive
specific features, and (iii) manipulating latent embeddings and inspecting their effect on reconstructed
anatomy to test whether individual features encode monotonic, region-specific changes.
Attention maps.
We visualized what the model considers important by plotting its self-attention
maps (Fig. 6). The resulting maps show that different attention heads learn distinct spatial patterns
rather than attending uniformly across the entire brain, consistent with findings in other models [6].
As shown in the attention visualizations, different heads consistently emphasize different anatomical
regions across the axial, coronal, and sagittal views. Some heads exhibit relatively localized attention
around specific brain structures, whereas others capture more distributed patterns across larger
portions of the brain. This diversity suggests that the multi-head self-attention mechanism enables
Brain2Vec to encode complementary spatial information, with individual heads potentially capturing
different anatomical or contextual characteristics of the input MRI. An important exception is the
behavior of heads 0 and 9: across views and samples, these heads frequently assign substantial
attention to regions outside the brain, particularly along the image boundaries and background (see
Section A.6). This indicates that they are not primarily encoding meaningful brain anatomy and may
instead capture image-level or acquisition-related characteristics.

DLMUSE regional volume, together with the mean difference in each latent dimension between

4.4
Inference Speed and Scalability
Inference efficiency is a critical but often underreported aspect of deep learning evaluation, particularly
in medical imaging, where 3D volumetric data introduces substantial computational overhead. For
Brain2Vec, which processes full 3D MRI scans to produce subject-level embeddings, efficient
inference is especially important given the large and diverse cohorts it targets.
We compared Brain2Vec’s inference time against DLMUSE, which uses an nnU-Net [26] to segment
the brain and compute ROI volumes from the full 3D input, and against NeuroJEPA, which uses
a similar ViT encoder augmented with a Mixture of Experts (MoE) layer [35] at inference time.
Benchmarks were conducted on the IXI dataset (581 scans) using an A40 GPU, as well as single-
subject inference on CPU. On GPU, Brain2Vec processed the full IXI cohort in 30 seconds (≈0.05
seconds per scan), an ≈87× speedup over DLMUSE and an ≈7× speedup over NeuroJEPA.
On CPU, Brain2Vec remained ≈8× faster than DLMUSE, whereas NeuroJEPA was faster than
Brain2Vec (6.98 vs. 45.73 seconds). This is likely because NeuroJEPA’s sparse MoE layer activates
only a subset of its parameters for each input, which reduces sequential computation on CPU.
Brain2Vec’s dense ViT encoder, by contrast, benefits more from GPU parallelism. Overall, Brain2Vec
offers substantially lower computational cost in GPU-based, cohort-scale settings while maintaining
strong predictive performance (Table 6).

Discussion
In this work, we introduced Brain2Vec, a self-supervised foundation model trained on 74, 425 T1-
weighted brain MRI scans. We evaluated Brain2Vec across multiple settings: fine-tuning for brain
age prediction, feature extraction for disease classification, external validation on an independent
cohort, interpretability analysis, and inference efficiency. We demonstrated that Brain2Vec serves as
a versatile dual-purpose framework: (1) accelerating convergence and improving predictive accuracy
when fine-tuned as a 3D ViT backbone, and (2) offering a rich, 768-dimensional frozen feature
space that is competitive with or superior to both anatomically-derived volumetric features and larger
foundation models across a range of disease classification tasks. Beyond predictive performance,
we showed that Brain2Vec’s learned representations are anatomically meaningful and interpretable,
with generative manipulation of its latent dimensions revealing biologically interpretable, continuous
neurodegenerative phenotypes in reconstructed brain space.
Our results reveal a consistent trade-off between anatomical segmentations and self-supervised
continuous embeddings. While traditional pipelines like DLMUSE excel at detecting localized
structural changes such as volumetric atrophy in Alzheimer’s Disease, they are constrained by
fixed anatomical boundaries. Conversely, Brain2Vec representations outperformed DLMUSE on
harder classification tasks (e.g., diabetes, smoking, elevated BMI). This suggests that Brain2Vec
may be learning representations beyond those captured by standard volumetric features derived from
anatomical segmentation.
One notable finding of this study is that Brain2Vec-SVR models achieve competitive predictive
performance relative to NeuroJEPA, a state-of-the-art foundation model, across several downstream
tasks, despite Brain2Vec being pre-trained on ≈9.2× fewer scans (74, 425 vs 683, 860). We attribute
this sample efficiency to the inductive bias of our training objective within the structural MRI domain.
12


At their core, Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) like NeuroJEPA, use a context input x
to estimate the embedded state of a target input y. This process relies on a predictor conditioned on an
extra variable z to account for variability during feature-space prediction. However, in standardized
neuroimaging datasets where scans are skull-stripped, atlas-registered, and anatomically aligned,
latent representation matching presents a relatively low-variance objective. It is well documented
that representation collapse is a concern with JEPA architectures [2]. In contrast, representation
collapse is not a concern with reconstruction-based methods. Generative architectures mask a high
percentage of the input and use an encoder to produce latent embeddings z of the masked input y
and using a decoder, learn to reconstruct the original (unmasked) input x. This forces the encoder
to learn high-fidelity structural priors and inter-regional anatomical dependencies across the entire
brain volume. This dense supervisory signal explains both the model’s high sample efficiency and the
strong, explicit alignment observed between Brain2Vec latent dimensions and regional brain volumes
(Section 4.3).
A common critique of deep feature extractors in neuroimaging is their limited interpretability relative
to anatomically defined ROI volumes. Through latent manipulation experiments (Section 4.3), we
provided evidence that Brain2Vec’s latent dimensions can be linked to anatomically meaningful,
region-specific effects, offering a step toward addressing this limitation. Progressively shifting latent
dimensions along disease-associated vectors produced monotonic, anatomically plausible changes in
reconstructed scans, specifically capturing ventricular expansion, and gray and white matter volume
decrease. This generative steering provides evidence that the learned latent space encodes coherent,
continuous radiological phenotypes rather than uninterpretable noise.
Despite being trained on a large and diverse dataset, Brain2Vec has two notable limitations. First, UK
Biobank dataset dominates the training data (≈55%), which may bias learned representations toward
its specific acquisition protocol and reduce embedding quality for scans acquired under substantially
different conditions. Second, the training cohort is skewed toward older adults (mean age 65.0
years; fewer than 5% of scans from studies with mean age below 45), which may limit the model’s
representational accuracy for younger populations. Users applying Brain2Vec outside its training
distribution should take these factors into account.
Brain2Vec code and trained models are publicly available at https://github.com/CBICA/
Brain2Vec. Embeddings can be extracted locally via the installable package or through the NiChart
cloud platform (https://cloud.neuroimagingchart.com), which requires no local installation
or specialized infrastructure.

A.5
Selection of Brain2Vec features for latent manipulation
To construct the subset of manipulated features S used in Section 4.3, we identified Brain2Vec
latent dimensions that are strongly associated with specific DLMUSE ROIs and have a consistent,
monotonic effect on those regions under manipulation. For each Brain2Vec dimension, we computed
(i) the mean difference between CU and AD subjects and (ii) its Pearson correlation with the best-
matching DLMUSE ROI. Based on this ranking, we selected the 22 dimensions with the largest and
most consistent effects (supplementary Table 14).
We restricted this latent manipulation analysis to the CU vs. AD task because it shows the largest
anatomical differences between groups among the classification tasks we studied. We repeated the
same selection procedure for other tasks in our cohort, including elevated BMI, smoking, diabetes,
and hypertension, but did not observe features with comparably strong effects. This suggests that the
latent directions identified for CU vs. AD reflect a stronger, more anatomically coherent signal than
those for tasks with subtler or more heterogeneous structural correlates.
