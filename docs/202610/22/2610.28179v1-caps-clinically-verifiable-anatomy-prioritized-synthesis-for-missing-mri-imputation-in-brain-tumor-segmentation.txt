Abstract 

Multi-parametric MRI is the gold standard for neuro-oncology, yet acquiring complete 4-modality protocols may be unfeasible in real-world clinical practice. When handling missing modalities, existing automated systems force a critical compromise: robust segmenters operate as uninterpretable "black boxes" that bypass image generation, while standard synthesis models often produce blurry reconstructions lacking diagnostic reliability. To address this issue, we propose CAPS, a Clinically-Verifiable Anatomy-Prioritized Synthesis framework that reconciles computational precision with clinical transparency. Our multi-modal generative architecture is driven by a hybrid objective function (L1 + LAFP + LDualDice ) that explicitly enforces the preservation of deep anatomical features and strict topological boundaries, rather than relying solely on pixel-wise optimization. Evaluated comprehensively on the BraTS Adult Glioma dataset, the framework achieves superior synthesis fidelity across intensity-based metrics, and demonstrates robust downstream segmentation performance (82 .38% mean Dice), successfully restoring verifiable visual context. Furthermore, the framework exhibits exceptional zero-shot cross-domain generalization, maintaining high structural fidelity across completely unseen Meningioma, Pediatric, and Sub-Saharan African cohorts without domain-specific adaptation. Finally, a blinded radiological assessment confirms that our anatomy-aware approach significantly en-hances both perceived image quality and clinical utility, providing clinicians with the reliable visual evidence required to trust downstream automated workflows. 

Keywords: Image Synthesis, Missing Modalities, MRI, Brain Tumors, Generalization 

1. Introduction 

Gliomas are among the most prevalent primary Cen-tral Nervous System (CNS) tumors Weller et al. (2015), with high-grade gliomas being particularly aggressive due to their highly infiltrative growth and low sur-vival rates Guo et al. (2023). The gold standard for the diagnosis, treatment planning, and post-operative monitoring of these tumors is multi-parametric Mag-netic Resonance Imaging (mp-MRI). A standard neuro-oncological protocol typically comprises four scan modalities: Native T1-weighted (T1n), Post-contrast T1-weighted (T1c), T2-weighted (T2w), and T2 Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (T2f) Diana-Albelda et al. (2025a). Each modality provides unique and complementary tissue contrasts that are critical for ac-curate tumor characterization. For instance, the ac-tive, proliferating tumor core and neoangiogenesis are primarily visible in the gadolinium-enhanced T1c se-quence, while the surrounding peritumoral edema and non-enhancing infiltrative regions are best delineated using T2f. However, acquiring the complete 4-modality protocol in real-world clinical settings is sometimes un-feasible, as scans may be incomplete due to time con-straints, patient motion artifacts, financial limitations, or contraindications to gadolinium-based contrast agents Li et al. (2024). The absence of even a single modality introduces a twofold clinical and algorithmic challenge. Clinically, it deprives neuroradiologists of vital anatom-arXiv:2610.28179v1 [eess.IV] 20 Oct 2026 ical information, potentially leading to suboptimal sur-gical planning and, ultimately, negatively impacting pa-tient survival and recurrence rates. Algorithmically, state-of-the-art automated segmentation systems, which are increasingly integrated to assist clinical workflows, are typically designed to process the full set of four in-put channels, thereby experiencing severe performance degradation or failure when a modality is missing or zero-padded Sharma and Hamarneh (2019). To mitigate this problem, two main strategies have emerged in the literature. The first one focuses on de-signing robust segmentation frameworks that can pre-dict tumor boundaries despite incomplete inputs Zhang et al. (2022); Pipoli et al. (2025). While these systems achieve remarkable accuracy, they present a significant challenge in clinical practice: they output a tumor mask without restoring the missing anatomical image, leav-ing the clinician without the visual evidence needed to corroborate the automated delineation. The second strategy relies on image synthesis to impute the miss-ing modality Diana-Albelda et al. (2025b). However, traditional synthesis methods primarily optimize stan-dard pixel-wise reconstruction losses, such as L1, which frequently results in blurry outputs that miss important structural details Longuefosse et al. (2025), thereby re-stricting their practical utility for the clinicians. Addressing these limitations, we propose CAPS, a novel Clinically-Verifiable Anatomy-Prioritized Syn-thesis framework that bridges the gap between computa-tional robustness and clinical reliability. Instead of opti-mizing solely for pixel-level similarity, our approach ex-plicitly maximizes the preservation of anatomical struc-tures and tumor topology. By restoring the missing modality with high structural fidelity, our method pro-vides the clinician with the necessary visual data to plan surgeries and verify AI-generated segmentations, while simultaneously supporting robust downstream au-tomated segmentation, as illustrated in Figure 1. • Empirical Analysis of the Accuracy-Interpretability Trade-o ff . We compare robust segmentation frameworks against synthesis-based pipelines and conclude that, while robust seg-menters may achieve marginally higher numerical accuracy on missing modalities, high-fidelity image synthesis is an indispensable step to provide the visual context that neuroradiologists require for diagnostic verification.

2. Related Work 

Handling missing mp-MRI modalities in neuro-oncology generally falls into two distinct paradigms: ro-bust segmentation architectures that adapt to incomplete inputs Zhang et al. (2024), and medical image synthesis models that generate the absent data Dar et al. (2019). 

2.1. Robust Segmentation with Missing Modalities 

Recent literature has extensively explored adaptive segmentation networks designed to be resilient against incomplete input configurations. Several studies have focused on learning modality-agnostic networks and shared latent representations that remain stable even when specific sequences are omitted Dorent et al. (2019). For instance, early foundational approaches like HeMIS Havaei et al. (2016) achieved this by comput-ing statistical moments across available feature maps. This concept has evolved into sophisticated feature-level fusion strategies employing attention mechanisms, transformer-based architectures like mmFormer Zhang et al. (2022), and state-space models such as IM-Fuse Pipoli et al. (2025). Additionally, knowledge distillation techniques have been widely proposed to transfer struc-tural information from a full-modality “teacher” net-work to a missing-modality “student” network Hu et al. (2020). Despite achieving remarkably high computational ac-curacy, typically evaluated through standard metrics like the Dice Similarity Coe ffi cient (DSC) and Haus-dor ff Distance (HD95), these robust segmenters face a critical usability limitation. Because they do not gener-ate the missing scan and directly predict tissue bound-aries, they fail to provide the structural reference that radiologists rely on. This lack of diagnostic trans-parency hinders the adoption of pure segmentation ap-proaches in clinical workflows where visual verification is mandatory. 

2.2. Medical Image Synthesis 

A parallel line of research focuses on cross-modality medical image synthesis to impute the missing se-quences. Early generative methods heavily relied on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) Goodfellow et al. (2014); Tavse et al. (2022); Al Khalil et al. (2024), such as Pix2Pix Isola et al. (2017) and CycleGAN Zhu et al. (2017), which primarily performed one-to-one image translations. More recently, the field has ad-vanced towards highly adaptable architectures capable of handling multi-modal inputs, shifting from basic 1-to-1 mappings to complex many-to-one synthesis mod-els Meng et al. (2024). This evolution is highlighted by advanced Di ff usion Models Kebaili et al. (2025b) like AMM-Di ff Kebaili et al. (2025a), which adaptively pro-cess variable sets of inputs, and State Space Models like I2I-Mamba Atli et al. (2024), which capture long-range contextual features across multi-contrast MRI. However, a fundamental limitation persists: most of these models are trained using generic pixel intensity-based objectives like L1 or Mean Squared Error Nie et al. (2018). Because these losses penalize errors on a purely pixel basis, they lack structural awareness and tend to over-smooth the generated images, washing out critical high-frequency details such as intricate tumor borders Isola et al. (2017). Furthermore, their perfor-mance is often solely measured via Image Quality Met-rics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM), leaving their actual utility for downstream automated clinical tasks unverified. 

2.3. Structural Constraints and Joint Architectures 

To mitigate the smoothing e ff ects of intensity-based losses, recent studies have introduced structural con-straints and joint synthesis-segmentation frameworks. For example, methods like EaGAN Yu et al. (2019) explicitly integrate edge-aware perceptual constraints to preserve high-frequency structures. Similarly, 3D Wavelet Di ff usion Models (WDM) Ferreira et al. (2024) strive to maintain spatial resolution while generating missing modalities. Moreover, unified frameworks have been proposed to jointly optimize synthesis and seg-mentation, acknowledging that image generation must ultimately be useful for tumor delineation Dorent et al. (2025). However, while feature-based objectives like perceptual loss e ff ectively balance pixel accuracy and realism in natural scenes, they often lack the anatomical 3rigor required for 3D medical volumes, where structural integrity must be prioritized over stylistic consistency Longuefosse et al. (2025). Despite these significant advances, existing joint frameworks often struggle to preserve fine tissue inter-faces when critical modalities are missing. Our work addresses this exact issue. By integrating a targeted Anatomical Feature-Prioritized (AFP) loss and a Dual Dice loss into a multi-modal 3-to-1 generative architec-ture, our framework successfully reconciles algorithmic precision with clinical interpretability. This approach successfully delivers highly verifiable restorations for radiologists while maintaining competitive performance in subsequent segmentation tasks. 

3. Method 

This section details the proposed Clinically-Verifiable Anatomy-Prioritized Synthesis framework (CAPS), designed to impute missing MRI modalities while preserving high-fidelity structural details. First, the mathematical problem formulation and the overall two-stage pipeline are established. Subsequently, the network architecture and the hybrid objective function are introduced. Finally, the specific implementation and training configurations required to ensure ro-bust convergence and downstream clinical utility are outlined. 

3.1. Problem Formulation and Pipeline 

The core objective of our framework is to synthesize a missing MRI scan from a subset of available comple-mentary modalities, ensuring that the generated image retains both structural fidelity and diagnostic utility. Let M = {T 1n, T 1c, T 2w, T 2 f } denote the complete set of four standard neuro-oncological MRI scan modal-ities acquired for a given patient. In real-world clin-ical scenarios, it is common for a specific modality to be missing, corrupted, or otherwise unavailable Li et al. (2024). We denote the target missing modality as t ∈ M and its corresponding ground-truth 3D vol-ume as Y, and formalize the problem as a multi-modal synthesis task (specifically, a 3-to-1 mapping). The set of available input modalities is therefore defined as 

X = M \ { t}. Our goal is to train a generative map-ping function G, parametrized by weights θ, that takes the available modalities X as a multi-channel input and synthesizes a high-fidelity target estimation of the miss-ing sequence, denoted as ˆ Y:ˆY = Gθ(X), (1) where the synthesized volume ˆ Y is explicitly optimized to approximate the underlying anatomical information on Y.To achieve this, we propose a Clinically-Verifiable Anatomy-Prioritized Synthesis framework (CAPS) de-signed to operate in two interconnected phases (see Fig-ure 1): 1. Image Synthesis Phase: The core generative net-work takes the available 3D volumes X and syn-thesizes ˆ Y according to Figure 2 scheme. Unlike traditional synthesis pipelines that optimize purely for intensity matching, our generator G is driven by a hybrid objective function. This function in-tegrates a pre-trained feature extractor to explic-itly align the latent anatomical representations of ˆ Y

with those of Y through S aux , ensuring the preser-vation of intricate tumor morphologies. 2. Downstream Segmentation Phase: To validate the clinical utility of the synthesized modality, ˆ Y is subsequently combined with the available modal-ities X to assemble the full imputed protocol, de-fined as Msynth = X∪{ ˆY}. This complete set is then fed into an independent segmentation network S to predict the final tumor boundaries. This two-stage pipeline guarantees that the synthesis process is not evaluated in isolation, but rather through its ability to provide actionable and interpretable visual data that supports robust downstream clinical tasks. It is important to note that, since each MRI sequence encodes fundamentally di ff erent tissue contrasts, both the generator G and the auxiliary extractor S aux are in-dependently trained for each specific missing modality scenario t ∈ M, yielding four specialized pairs ( Gt,

S aux ,t). In contrast, the final clinical segmenter S is trained as a single unified model capable of processing any imputed 4-modality protocol, regardless of which sequence was synthesized. This design ensures that each synthesis pathway is maximally specialized for its target modality, while the downstream segmenter re-mains a general-purpose clinical tool.

3.2.1. Generator (G) 

The generator consists of a 3D Residual U-Net (nnResU-Net) Isensee et al. (2024) architecture config-ured with six encoding and decoding stages. By incorporating residual connections within each convolutional block, the network facilitates an e ffi cient gradient flow that prevents the vanishing gradient prob-lem typical of deep 3D models Isensee et al. (2021), thereby accelerating convergence and enhancing the sta-bility of the generative process. The encoder is designed to process a multi-channel stacked input X, composed of the three available MRI modalities, while the decoder is responsible for synthesizing the single-channel vol-ume ˆ Y corresponding to the missing sequence. To pre-vent the emergence of chequerboard artifacts, the de-coder utilizes a trilinear interpolation strategy followed by standard convolutions Longuefosse et al. (2026), en-suring smooth spatial transitions in the reconstructed anatomy. 

3.2.2. Auxiliary Feature Extractor ( Saux )

To drive the anatomy-prioritized optimization, we utilize an auxiliary network, Saux , which acts as a frozen expert during the training of G. This network is pre-trained using only one single real modality as input to ensure that its latent embeddings are highly spe-cialized in the specific anatomical features of each se-quence. During the generative phase, Saux remains strictly frozen and serves as a multi-scale feature extrac-tor. Specifically, to compute the Anatomical Feature-Prioritized ( LAFP ) loss Longuefosse et al. (2025), fea-ture maps are extracted from every level of both the en-coder and the decoder, forcing the generator to preserve structural invariants across multiple levels of abstrac-tion. Furthermore, this network provides the topologi-cal guidance required for the LDualDice loss, aligning the synthesized lesion area with the expected clinical mor-phology. Architecturally, Saux shares the same nnU-Net Res-Enc M configuration and training objective as the final clinical segmenter S , with the critical distinction that its input layer is configured to accept a single modality channel rather than the full four-channel protocol. 

3.2.3. Final Clinical Segmenter (S ) 

The final component of our framework is the clin-ical segmenter S , which utilizes the nnU-Net ResEnc M architecture Isensee et al. (2024) to achieve an op-timal balance between segmentation performance and computational e ffi ciency. This network is optimized us-ing the standard nnU-Net objective function, combining Dice loss and Cross-Entropy loss in equal proportion. Unlike the auxiliary extractor, this network is trained to process the full complement of four input modali-ties. To enhance its resilience to synthetic imputation, 

S is trained under a specialized augmentation scheme 5where there is a 50% probability that any given modal-ity is replaced by its synthetic counterpart generated by 

G. During the inference phase, this network acts as the definitive evaluator, processing the imputed 4-modality protocol to produce the final tumor masks. This config-uration ensures that the segmenter is not only capable of interpreting synthetic data but also of integrating it seamlessly with real modalities to maintain high diag-nostic accuracy.

3.4. Implementation and Training Details 

CAPS is implemented in PyTorch and integrated into the adapted nnU-Netv2 architecture Isensee et al. (2021); Longuefosse et al. (2024). 6Preprocessing and Data Augmentation. All MRI vol-umes were independently normalized using standard Z-score normalization (zero mean, unit variance) per modality. During training, we employed a robust on-line data augmentation pipeline to prevent overfitting, which included spatial transformations (random rota-tions and scaling) and intensity transformations (Gaus-sian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness /contrast adjust-ments, and gamma correction) Isensee et al. (2021). No-tably, spatial mirroring (flips) was explicitly disabled to preserve the inherent left-right anatomical asymmetries of the brain. 

Training Configuration. The generative model was trained using 3D patches of size 96 × 160 × 160 voxels and a batch size of 2. The network parameters were op-timized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with Nesterov momentum Shi (2024) (0 .99) and weight de-cay (3 × 10 −5). The initial learning rate was set to 10 −2

and progressively decayed according to a polynomial schedule function over the training process. To ensure training stability, especially given the multi-objective nature of our hybrid loss function, we applied gradient clipping. The model was trained for 1000 epochs, with each epoch consisting of 250 itera-tions. All models were trained on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU (48 GB VRAM). 

Inference Phase. During the evaluation phase, the in-ference was performed using a sliding-window ap-proach with a 50% patch overlap Isensee et al. (2021). To reconstruct the final synthetic modality ˆ Y, we em-ploy a mean aggregation strategy across overlapping patches. 

4. Experimental Setup 

This section outlines the experimental configuration utilized to validate CAPS. Initially, the selected neuro-oncological datasets and their respective data partition-ing strategies are detailed for both the primary evalua-tion and the cross-domain inference. Finally, the quan-titative evaluation metrics employed to assess both im-age reconstruction quality and downstream segmenta-tion accuracy are defined. 

4.1. Datasets and Data Partitioning 

To comprehensively train and evaluate our proposal, we utilized the globally recognized Brain Tumor Seg-mentation (BraTS) datasets Menze et al. (2014). All multi-parametric MRI sequences (T1n, T1c, T2w, and T2f) across these datasets share a standardized prepro-cessing pipeline: they are co-registered to the same anatomical template, skull-stripped, and resampled to an isotropic resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. 

Primary Training and Evaluation (Adult Glioma). The core development of our method was conducted using the BraTS 2023 Adult Glioma datasetBaid et al. (2021) (conceptually identical to the BraTS 2021 and 2022 co-horts), whose o ffi cial training set comprises 1251 pa-tient cases. We performed an 80% − 20% split on this subset: the 80% partition was exclusively used to train the generative synthesis network G, while the remaining 20% was strictly reserved for training both the auxiliary feature extractor S aux and the final clinical segmenter S ,ensuring complete data separation from the generator. In order to ensure a completely unbiased evaluation, all our quantitative results, ablation studies, and State-of-the-Art comparisons were extracted directly from the offi cial, unseen BraTS Adult Glioma Validation set, which comprises unseen 219 patient cases. To guar-antee absolute fairness and reproducibility, the down-stream segmentation metrics on this validation set were computed and retrieved through the o ffi cial Synapse au-tomated evaluation platform. 

Cross-Domain Generalization Datasets. To evaluate the zero-shot robustness of our framework under sig-nificant domain shifts, we conducted additional infer-ence on three distinct, unseen neuro-oncological cohorts from the BraTS challenge: 

• BraTS Meningioma LaBella et al. (2023): 1000 cases featuring structurally distinct, typically be-nign extra-axial tumors. 

• BraTS Pediatric Glioma Kazerooni et al. (2024): 99 cases, presenting unique developmental brain anatomies and di ff ering tumor morphologies. 

• BraTS Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) Glioma Ade-wole et al. (2023): 60 cases acquired from di-verse, low-resource clinical settings, introducing significant variations in scanner hardware and im-age quality.

5. Results 

Evaluation of the proposed framework encompasses a rigorous analysis of both synthesis fidelity and clini-cal utility. Initial experiments establish the performance degradation caused by missing modalities, providing a baseline for the subsequent ablation studies on archi-tecture and loss formulation. Additionally, the results include a comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art models, alongside a zero-shot generalization analy-sis and a blinded radiological validation to confirm the diagnostic reliability of the synthesized modality. 

5.1. Impact of Missing Modalities 

Before evaluating the results of CAPS, it is crucial to quantify the clinical and computational impact that the absence of MRI modalities has on automated segmenta-tion systems, detailed in Table 1. The reference model, trained and evaluated on the full protocol of four real modalities, establishes the up-per performance bound with a mean Dice coe ffi cient of 86 .65% and a Hausdor ff distance (HD95) of 12 .07mm. On the other hand, the zero-imputation strategy reveals critical network dependencies on specific sequences, identifying the absence of the T1c sequence as the most critical scenario, where the Dice coe ffi cient for the en-hancing tumor (ENH) drastically collapses to 14 .02%. Similarly, the missing T2f case disproportionately af-fects the whole tumor (WT), with its Dice score drop-ping to 47 .66% due to the loss of contrast in the peritu-moral edema regions. Although robust approaches, such as modality dropout training, partially mitigate these declines, these systems operate under a “black-box” paradigm that de-livers an isolated segmentation mask without restoring the underlying anatomical volume. This lack of vi-sual evidence prevents neuroradiologists from corrob-orating the AI-generated segmentation, thus justifying the pressing need for our synthesis framework, which is aimed at preserving structural fidelity alongside diag-nostic interpretability. 

5.2. Ablation Study: Architecture and Loss 

To empirically justify the design choices of our syn-thesis and segmentation framework, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study where all configurations were evaluated under the missing T1c scenario, as it rep-resents the most critical challenge from an anatomical perspective, according to Table 1. The architectural and loss decisions derived from this scenario were subse-quently applied identically to the remaining three miss-ing modality cases, with final results reported across all scenarios in Table 5. 8Architectural Optimization of G. As detailed in Ta-ble 2, we established a baseline using the standard nnU-Net architecture, which yielded a mean Dice coe ffi -cient of 70 .86% and a HD95 of 22 .55 mm. The inte-gration of 3D residual blocks (nnResU-Net) facilitated a more robust training optimization, raising the Dice score to 71 .99%. This performance gain is character-istic of residual architectures, which help mitigate gra-dient degradation in deep 3D networks while preserving high-frequency spatial features. Finally, replacing stan-dard transposed convolutions with a trilinear interpola-tion decoder e ff ectively mitigated spatial chequerboard artifacts, stabilizing synthesis quality and achieving an optimal NMSE, PSNR and mean dice. 

Hybrid Loss Formulation through S aux . The quantita-tive results presented in Table 3 and the visual com-parisons in Figure 3 underscore the critical importance of our hybrid loss formulation. As observed in Ta-ble 3, optimization relying exclusively on the voxel-level absolute error ( L1) yields the poorest segmenta-tion performance, which suggests that purely intensity-based metrics do not prioritize the preservation of essen-tial anatomical structures, treating all voxels with equal weight regardless of their diagnostic relevance. The introduction of Dice-based components into the objective function leads to a measurable improvement in the mean Dice score of the synthesized images. How-ever, the most robust validation results are achieved only when combining all three terms: L1, the Anatom-ical Feature-Prioritized loss ( LAFP ), and the LDualDice ,which ensures a synergy between global intensity con-sistency, deep latent feature alignment, and topological boundary integrity. Limitations & Future Work. Despite these promising results, the current method presents certain limitations, particularly its decoupled two-stage structure and its re-liance on a specific pre-trained segmentation network. To address these constraints, future research could focus on the development of a unified, end-to-end architecture that leverages anatomy-prioritized constraints within a single joint framework, potentially further closing the accuracy-interpretability gap. Additionally, other promising avenues for investigation include adapting the framework to accommodate dynamic input config-urations (e.g., 1-to-1 or 2-to-1 synthesis) and extend-ing this anatomy-prioritized paradigm to other complex oncological domains, such as multi-parametric prostate Armato III et al. (2018) or liver MRI. Finally, although the clinical validation confirms the diagnostic utility of the synthesized volumes, the assessment was performed by a single radiologist, so future studies may incorpo-rate multiple experts to robustly assess inter-rater relia-bility. 

6. Conclusion 

In this work, we presented a Clinically-Verifiable Anatomy-Prioritized Synthesis framework (CAPS) to address the critical challenge of missing MRI modal-ities in neuro-oncological imaging Chrysochoou et al. (2026). Traditional approaches to this problem typi-cally force a compromise between computational ac-curacy and diagnostic transparency: robust segmenters predict tumor boundaries while leaving clinicians blind to the underlying anatomy Zhang et al. (2022); Pipoli et al. (2025), whereas standard synthesis models recon-struct images that lack the structural fidelity required for clinical trust Longuefosse et al. (2025). By driving our 3D generative architecture with a hybrid objective func-tion ( L1 + LAFP + LDualDice ), we successfully bridged this gap, enforcing the preservation of deep anatomical features and strict topological boundaries. Our comprehensive evaluation on the BraTS Adult Glioma dataset Baid et al. (2021) demonstrated that our framework significantly outperforms existing State-of-the-Art synthesis methods with a highly compet-itive downstream segmentation mean Dice score of 16 82 .38%. Beyond standard computational metrics, our approach exhibited exceptional zero-shot generalization capabilities across completely unseen and morphologi-cally distinct domains, including Meningioma LaBella et al. (2023), Pediatric Kazerooni et al. (2024), and Sub-Saharan African Adewole et al. (2023) cohorts. Most importantly, a blinded radiological evaluation con-firmed that our anatomy-aware synthesis substantially improves both perceived image quality and clinical util-ity, providing human experts with the verifiable visual context needed to trust downstream automated segmen-tation.
