## Abstract 

Automated radiology report generation for multi-sequence brain MRI holds strong promise for alleviating radiolo-gists’ workload, yet it remains a demanding problem: brain MRI studies are inherently volumetric, each pulse sequence carries complementary diagnostic information, and anno-tated data are critically scarce for low-resource clinical languages. Existing brain MRI-report resources are still dominated by English reports, translated clinical text, or datasets adapted from task-specific imaging benchmarks, leaving routine low-resource clinical reporting and hetero-geneous multi-sequence MRI protocols insufficiently repre-sented. To bridge this gap, we introduce ViBMR , to the best of our knowledge, the first large-scale multi-sequence brain MRI dataset paired with routine Vietnamese clini-cal reports, comprising 3,649 patients and 11,875 MRI se-ries across T1, T2, FLAIR, and contrast-enhanced T1 se-quences. Building on ViBMR, we establish a comprehensive evaluation setup for brain MRI report generation, cover-ing representative medical vision-language models, multi-sequence inputs, and feature aggregation strategies. The results provide key insights into dataset characteristics and model behavior, while demonstrating the necessity of na-tive low-resource clinical datasets for reliable Vietnamese-origin brain MRI report generation. Our dataset and base-line models will be released for non-commercial research.

Among imaging modalities, brain Magnetic Res-onance Imaging (MRI) plays a pivotal role in detecting arXiv:2610.29905v1 [cs.CV] 21 Oct 2026 and characterizing intracranial abnormalities. Unlike many other modalities, routine brain MRI is acquired as a multi-sequence study, in which T1, T2, FLAIR, and contrast-enhanced T1 (T1c) provide mutually complementary infor-mation: T2 and FLAIR are sensitive to edema and white matter lesions, whereas T1c better characterizes enhance-ment patterns [4, 9, 22, 34, 37]. Automating the joint inter-pretation of these sequences and synthesizing them into a textual report could substantially reduce radiologists’ work-load and shorten diagnostic turnaround. Despite this progress, most existing brain MRI-report datasets are constructed and released in English, leav-ing low-resource clinical languages largely unaddressed [1, 10, 18, 21, 24, 28, 32]. This is a critical limitation, because radiology reports are strongly language, institu-tion, and population-dependent: writing style, terminology, abbreviation usage, and the granularity of anatomical de-scription can differ markedly across hospitals and regions [6, 12, 29, 31]. Furthermore, the epidemiological and mor-phological characteristics of Asian populations often differ from those of European and North American cohorts in both disease prevalence and body habitus [11, 19, 27]. Together with differences in language, reporting conventions, acqui-sition protocols, and dataset composition, these factors mo-tivate our cross-dataset analysis in RQ1. Beyond the lan-guage barrier, some existing “report generation” datasets do not originate from routine clinical reports. Instead, they are repurposed from datasets built for other tasks such as lesion segmentation, with findings and impressions subse-quently authored by hired radiologists for pre-annotated re-gions [3, 24, 26]. This construction process yields report structures that deviate from the actual hospital workflow, in which reports are formed directly from a radiologist’s read-ing of the scan. Addressing these challenges requires two ingredients in tandem: (i) a dataset for a low-resource language that faith-fully reflects routine clinical reporting, and (ii) a compre-hensive empirical study that clarifies which visual com-ponents and fusion strategies are genuinely beneficial for multi-sequence brain MRI report generation. Existing re-sources, however, satisfy neither requirement, especially for Vietnamese. In this paper, we bridge this gap through two main contributions: First, we introduce ViBMR, the first and, to the best of our knowledge, largest multi-sequence brain MRI dataset paired with routine Vietnamese clinical reports. The dataset is collected from a tertiary hospital in Vietnam and com-prises 3,649 patients with 11,875 series. It covers the four MRI sequences commonly used in neuroimaging practice, including T1, T2, FLAIR, and T1c, as well as diverse sequence combinations that reflect the variability of real-world acquisition protocols. The reports are extracted di-rectly from hospital records and exhibit the long-tailed dis-tribution characteristic of clinical practice, ranging from frequent findings such as small vessel disease to rare but clinically significant abnormalities (see Figure 2). ViBMR therefore provides a large-scale Vietnamese resource for studying medical report generation in a low-resource lan-guage setting. Second, building on ViBMR, we conduct a set of re-search questions around brain MRI report generation in this low-resource setting. We systematically evaluate the transferability of segmentation-pretrained multi-sequence encoders to report generation, analyze the contribution of individual MRI sequences and their combinations, com-pare multiple feature aggregation strategies ranging from mean fusion and learnable weighted fusion to spatial gat-ing, channel gating, and cross-attention, and quantify the cross-dataset generalization gap between existing datasets and Vietnamese-origin reports. These experiments establish reliable baselines for ViBMR and provide practical empiri-cal insights for future model design in multi-sequence brain MRI report generation. 

## 2. Related Work 

Datasets for Brain MRI Report Generation. Public brain MRI datasets have substantially advanced medical image analysis, especially for lesion segmentation, dis-ease classification, and algorithmic benchmarking. Rep-resentative benchmarks such as BraTS [2], ISLES [15], the WMH Segmentation Challenge [20], and ATLAS [25] provide high-quality annotations for specific clinical sce-narios, including glioma, stroke lesions, white matter hy-perintensities, and post-stroke lesion segmentation. How-ever, these resources are primarily designed for anomaly segmentation tasks, rather than for learning the correspon-dence between multi-sequence brain MRI studies and free-text diagnostic reports. Recent MRI-text resources have be-gun to address this gap. RadGenome-Brain MRI [24] pro-vides brain MRI scans, anomaly segmentation masks, and radiologist-written descriptions for abnormal regions, mak-ing them suitable for grounded regional report generation. However, RadGenome-Brain MRI is curated from multiple public anomaly segmentation datasets, followed by man-ually authored findings and impressions for annotated re-gions [24]. Therefore, its report structure differs from rou-tine radiology reports directly collected from hospital work-flows. MR-RATE [10] further expands MRI-text learning by pairing brain and spine MRI studies with radiology re-ports and metadata. Another important gap is language cov-erage. Existing MRI image-report resources remain limited for native low-resource clinical languages. Current brain MRI resources are commonly released or standardized in English, including Chinese-to-English processed reports in AutoRG-Brain [24] and Turkish-to-English reports in MR-RATE [10]. Beyond brain MRI, Cervical-RG focuses on 3D multi-sequence cervical cancer MRI report generation [41], while Auto-Rad and SPINE focus on lumbar or spinal MRI report generation using English or LLM-standardized nar-rative reports [13, 40]. This limitation is important because radiology reports are language, institution, and population-dependent: reporting style, terminology, abbreviation us-age, anatomical description granularity, and patient charac-teristics may differ across hospitals and regions. Therefore, a Vietnamese brain MRI image-report dataset is necessary for studying report generation in a low-resource clinical lan-guage setting. In this context, our dataset complements ex-isting brain MRI resources by focusing on routine multi-sequence brain MRI studies paired with Vietnamese clinical reports.

Models for 3D MRI report generation. Recent medi-cal vision-language models have extended report generation and visual question answering from 2D radiology images to volumetric medical imaging. General 3D medical MLLMs such as M3D-LaMed [1] and Med3DVLM [38] demon-strate the feasibility of aligning 3D medical images with clinical text for tasks including image-text retrieval, VQA, and report generation. For brain MRI, AutoRG-Brain intro-duces grounded report generation with lesion and anatomi-cal masks [24], PIRTA improves the factuality of acute is-chemic stroke report generation from 3D DWI MRI using paired image-domain retrieval and text-domain augmenta-tion [23], and Brain3D adapts a 2D medical vision encoder into a native 3D encoder for volumetric brain tumor MRI report generation [5]. These works demonstrate the po-tential of 3D visual representations for neuroradiological reporting, but their visual input is commonly formulated as a single volumetric scan 3D image rather than routine multi-sequence brain MRI studies that must be interpreted jointly. Beyond brain MRI, Cervical-RG [41] addresses multi-sequence cervical cancer MRI report generation by concatenating aligned 3D MRI sequences at the input level before ViT-based visual encoding. 

## 3. Dataset Construction Process 

3.1. Data Preprocessing Pipeline 

We constructed a large-scale real-world brain MRI dataset collected from a major tertiary hospital in Vietnam, con-sisting of MRI studies from 3,649 patients with diverse in-tracranial abnormalities and neurological conditions. The radiology reports were extracted from raw hospital PDF documents through a dedicated preprocessing pipeline. To ensure patient privacy and regulatory compliance, all per-sonally identifiable information and sensitive metadata were removed during the anonymization process. We then ex-tracted the findings section of the reports as the ground-truth target for the report generation task. In parallel, we collected the corresponding MRI volumes for each re-port and aligned them with the extracted report descrip-tions. Although the number of MRI sequences varies across patients, four modalities are consistently and com-monly used in routine neuroimaging practice: T1-weighted (T1), T2-weighted (T2), Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recov-ery (FLAIR), and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (T1c). These modalities provide complementary diagnostic information regarding anatomical structures, lesion charac-terization, edema, enhancement patterns, and pathological tissue properties. Therefore, we selected these four volu-metric MRI sequences as the primary inputs for our evalua-tion methods. As shown in Fig. 3, for each patient, we first organized the DICOM files by study and sequence, and then assembled the ordered slices into volumetric MRI scans us-ing the spatial information stored in the metadata, including slice position, image orientation, and voxel spacing. Since the raw scans were acquired under heterogeneous clinical protocols, the reconstructed volumes may differ in orienta-tion, spatial resolution, field of view, and matrix size. To reduce this variability, all 3D volumes were resampled to an isotropic spatial resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. We then applied automated brain extraction using HD-BET [17] to obtain brain masks, which were used to suppress non-brain tissues and crop the intracranial region. This step removes irrelevant background structures while preserving the clini-cally meaningful anatomical context, allowing the model to process each sequence as a standardized 3D volume while maintaining anatomical continuity across slices.

As shown in Fig. 2a, this modality imbalance highlights the need for models that can handle incomplete or inconsistent multi-sequence inputs. To characterize the clinical content of the dataset, we extracted structured clinical labels from the radi-ology reports. We first identified recurrent expressions de-scribing anatomical regions, laterality, abnormal findings, Fazekas grades, and absence of abnormality. A large lan-guage model was used to suggest mappings between seman-tically equivalent expressions, which were subsequently re-viewed and verified by a radiologist. As shown in Fig. 2c, the anatomical distribution of the most commonly mentioned regions include the paranasal sinuses, ventricles, periven-tricular region, deep white matter, subcortical white mat-ter, frontal lobe, and basal ganglia. In addition, a single report may contain multiple co-existing findings across dif-ferent anatomical regions, such as concurrent chronic small vessel disease, sinus abnormalities, infarction, hemorrhage, or mass-related findings. Therefore, the report generation task is not limited to predicting a single dominant abnormal-ity, but requires the model to describe multiple abnormali-ties and their corresponding anatomical locations within the same study. These statistics suggest that the dataset covers both intracranial abnormalities and extracranial incidental findings, while also exhibiting substantial imbalance across disease categories and anatomical locations. Such charac-teristics make report generation challenging, as the model must learn from heterogeneous multi-sequence inputs, fre-quent routine findings, rare but clinically significant abnor-malities, and complex anatomical descriptions.

Language model. The fused visual tokens are mapped into the language-model embedding space using a two-layer visual projector. The projector consists of layer normaliza-tion, a linear projection from the visual feature dimension to the LLM hidden size, a GELU activation, and a sec-ond linear layer. The projected visual embeddings are con-catenated with the textual prompt embeddings and fed into Qwen3-4B-Instruct. The model is trained to generate the findings section of the Vietnamese brain MRI report. 

Implementation details and hyperparameters. We split ViBMR into train/validation/test sets with an 8/1/1 ratio at the patient level. Each patient contributes exactly one MRI study, with no patient overlap across splits. During report-generation training, we optimize the fusion module, the visual projector, and the LoRA parameters of the lan-guage model. We use LoRA with rank r = 16 , scaling factor α = 32 , and dropout rate 0.05 . All fusion variants are trained under the same optimization setting for a fair comparison. We train for 20 epochs with a batch size of 1 and use gradient accumulation over 8 steps. The maximum number of visual tokens is set to 64, and the maximum tar-get report length is set to 1024 tokens. We use AdamW as the optimizer. The learning rate is set to 1 × 10 −4 for both the fusion module and the visual projector, and 1×10 −5 for LoRA parameters. 

## 5. Evaluation 

5.1. Evaluation Metrics 

We evaluate report generation using BLEU-1, BLEU-4, ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, and BERTScore. While BLEU and ROUGE measure lexical overlap, BERTScore evaluates semantic similarity based on contextualized rep-resentations. To further assess clinical correctness, we apply the pre-viously described clinical label extraction method to both the reference and generated reports. The extracted labels are then compared at the sample level to measure agree-ment in clinically relevant findings, including anatomical region, laterality, abnormal finding, Fazekas grade, and ab-sence of abnormality. For sample i, let Ti and Gi denote the clinical label sets extracted from the reference and gen-erated reports, respectively. We define T P i = |Ti ∩ Gi|,

5.2. Result and Analysis 

RQ1. Cross-dataset Generalization. 

Models trained on MR-RATE show limited generalization to Vietnamese-origin reports. To examine this, we use an M2FTrans and Qwen3-4B-Instruct [39] baseline, where the segmentation-pretrained M2FTrans bottleneck is fine-tuned jointly with LoRA adapters in the LLM. We randomly sam-ple 899 brain MRI cases from MR-RATE and split them into training, validation, and test sets with an 8:1:1 ra-tio. The model is trained on MR-RATE, then evaluated on both the held-out MR-RATE test split and the ViBMR test set. For ViBMR, reports are translated into English us-ing the GPT-5.4 API model [30], while preserving medical keywords and domain-specific terminology. As shown in Figure 4, performance of the model drops from 29.50 to 18.85 BLEU-1, 13.68 to 1.03 BLEU-4, and 33.48 to 16.68 ROUGE-L when moving from MR-RATE to ViBMR test set. The largest gaps occur in ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2, both exceeding 18 points, indicating a substantial cross-dataset performance gap. However, this experiment does not isolate the specific source of this degradation. Pos-sible contributing factors include translation artifacts, dif-ferences in reporting style and clinical expression, popu-lation and disease distributions, scanner and imaging pro-tocol differences, and overall dataset composition. There-fore, the observed gap should be interpreted as evidence of cross-dataset domain shift rather than being attributed to any single factor. These results suggest that ViBMR pro-vides a complementary setting for studying the robustness and cross-dataset generalization of medical vision-language models in a low-resource clinical and linguistic context. 

RQ2. Effect of Segmentation-Pretrained MRI Encoders. 

Segmentation-pretrained multi-sequence MRI encoders provide stronger visual representations for brain MRI re-port generation than the evaluated single-modality base-lines. For this modality ablation study, we select the two best-performing fusion strategies and two original baselines, evaluating selected modality combinations on the same sub-set of 939 studies containing all four sequences. However, improvements vary across fusion strategies. In particular, M2FTrans-MMFT achieves relatively consis-tent performance across modality combinations, potentially benefiting from its pretrained fusion tokens that capture modality relationships. Overall, these results highlight the benefits of complementary MRI sequences, the effective-ness of T1+T2+FLAIR, and the importance of fusion design for exploiting multi-modality information.
