Abstract 

Familial amyloid-β (A β) variants can alter aggregation kinetics, but how these molecular differences interact with structural brain connectivity remains incompletely understood. We developed a mutation-aware mechanistic framework coupling a coarse-grained monomer–oligomer–fibril aggregation–fragmentation system to graph diffusion on the 540-node largest connected component of the Budapest Reference Connectome. Experimental A β42 nucleation scores were mapped exponentially to relative primary nucleation rates for seven variants, with wild type as the reference. Robustness was evaluated through mutation-score uncertainty, alternative kinetic mappings, global parameter sensitivity, seed and edge-weight perturbations, 20 degree-preserving randomized connectomes, spatial propagation analysis, synthetic ABC-SMC parameter recovery, posterior prediction, and reaction-level Chemical Langevin simulations. E22G exhibited the earliest threshold crossing and great-est cumulative oligomer burden, whereas A2V was delayed under the selected mapping. Mutation rankings persisted across the tested network perturbations, although regional burden patterns changed with topology. Connectome distance from the seed regions was strongly associated with later oligomer arrival (Spearman 

ρ ≈ 0.92). Under inferred parameter uncertainty, the mean timing-rank correlation was 0.990 and cumulative-burden ordering was preserved in every posterior draw; exact peak-amplitude ordering was less stable. Stochastic ensemble medians retained the deterministic mutation ordering despite overlap among individual trajectories. Within this proof-of-concept framework, mutation-dependent kinetics primarily influence relative aggregation timing and cumulative burden, while structural con-nectivity shapes spatial propagation. Nominal background rates, model-time units, and synthetic parameter recovery limit interpretation to mechanistic comparisons rather than clinically calibrated prediction. 

Keywords: amyloid-β; familial Alzheimer’s disease; structural connectome; aggregation– fragmentation; parameter uncertainty; stochastic modelling.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder that occurs predominantly in sporadic form, although a smaller proportion of cases arise through autosomal-dominant inheritance associated with pathogenic variants in genes such as amyloid precursor protein (APP ) and the presenilins [1–4]. Mutations within or near the APP region encoding amyloid-β (A β) can alter peptide charge, hydrophobicity, conformation, and aggregation kinetics, thereby influencing the formation of soluble oligomers and fibrillar deposits that are central features of AD pathology [5, 6]. A key early step in A β aggregation is nucleation, in which soluble peptides form a stable nucleus that permits subsequent aggregate growth. Under physiological conditions, this initial step is often kinetically slow and energetically unfavorable [7]. Once nucleation occurs, additional processes including elongation, secondary nucleation, fragmentation, and conversion can accelerate aggregate formation and amplify oligomer and fibril burden [8, 9]. Mutations that alter nucleation behavior may therefore produce substantial differences in downstream aggregation dynamics [10]. Familial AD mutations provide a useful setting in which to study how single-amino-acid substitutions affect A β aggregation [11]. The present study considers the variants A2V, A21G, D23N, E22G, E22Q, E22K, and H6R, with wild type (WT) used as the reference condition [12]. These mutations have been associated with distinct aggregation and neuropathological phenotypes. E22G (Arctic) has been linked to enhanced protofibril for-mation and accelerated aggregation [13]; E22Q (Dutch), E22K (Italian), and D23N (Iowa) have been associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy and altered amyloid deposition [14–16]; A21G (Flemish) has been linked to altered APP processing and A β generation [17]; H6R (English) has been associated with altered early oligomerization dynamics [18]; and A2V has been reported to modify A β aggregation propensity [19]. These mutation-specific differences motivate a modeling framework in which aggregation kinetics are not assumed to be identical across variants. Recent high-throughput mutational measurements provide quantitative mutation scores that can be used to represent relative effects on A β nucleation [20]. In the present study, these scores are used to scale the primary nucleation rate in the main model, while alternative mappings involving secondary nucleation are examined as mechanism-uncertainty analyses. This provides a direct link between experimentally informed mutation effects and a coarse-grained aggregation model. To connect molecular aggregation kinetics with large-scale brain organization, we de-veloped a mutation-aware mechanistic model on the Budapest Reference Connectome [21]. Local A β dynamics are represented by a coarse-grained Smoluchowski-type aggregation– fragmentation system containing three effective species: monomers, oligomers, and fibrils. The reaction system includes primary nucleation, secondary nucleation, elongation, con-version, fragmentation, and clearance. These local dynamics are coupled across brain regions through diffusion on the structural connectome Laplacian, producing a network aggregation–fragmentation–diffusion model. The central aim of the study is to determine whether experimentally informed mutation-specific aggregation kinetics produce robust differences in global amyloid dynamics and how those differences interact with structural network topology. The analysis therefore distinguishes between two related questions: 2whether mutation-specific kinetics determine the timing and cumulative burden of aggre-gation, and whether the structural connectome determines the regional pattern through which that burden is expressed. To assess robustness, the model is examined under several complementary analyses. Mutation-score uncertainty is propagated through the aggregation model, and global sensitivity analysis is used to identify parameters that most strongly influence aggregation timing, burden, and spatial spread. Robustness is further evaluated across alternative seed configurations, connectome weighting schemes, and degree-preserving randomized connectomes. Spatial propagation is assessed by relating regional activation times and burden to network distance from the seed set. A likelihood-free ABC-SMC analysis is used to examine internal parameter recoverability, followed by posterior-predictive propagation of parameter uncertainty through the full connectome. Finally, a Chemical Langevin formulation is used to quantify reaction-level stochastic variability around the deterministic dynamics, while interregional network transport remains deterministic. The purpose of this study is to develop and test a mechanistic proof-of-concept framework linking mutation-specific A β aggregation kinetics to connectome-constrained brain-wide propagation. The resulting model is intended to clarify which aspects of the dynamics are primarily controlled by molecular kinetics and which are shaped by large-scale structural connectivity. 

# 2 Modeling and Computational Methods 

The modelling framework linked experimentally derived mutation-specific A β42 nucleation information with a coarse-grained aggregation–fragmentation model and a structural brain connectome. The main analysis consisted of a deterministic mutation-aware network model, followed by uncertainty, sensitivity, network-robustness, synthetic parameter-recovery, posterior-predictive, and stochastic analyses. It includes monomer production, primary nucleation, secondary nucleation, elongation, oligomer-to-fibril conversion, fibril fragmentation, and species-specific clearance [9, 24, 26]. # 2.3 Network robustness and spatial propagation analyses 

A series of robustness analyses was used to determine whether the mutation-dependent conclusions were sensitive to the choice of seed regions, edge-weight representation, or exact connectome topology. Seed robustness was assessed using alternative 16-node con-figurations: the 16 highest-strength candidate nodes, all 16 posterior cingulate candidates, the 16 highest-strength precuneus candidates, and a balanced configuration containing eight posterior cingulate and eight precuneus nodes. Twenty additional random 16-node 9seed sets were drawn from the complete pool of 36 candidate nodes. For each seed configuration, all eight mutation conditions were simulated, and mutation rankings for threshold-crossing time, peak oligomer burden, and oligomer AUC were compared using Spearman rank correlation. Regional oligomer patterns were also compared with those from the primary 18-node seed configuration. The effect of structural-weight representation was examined using the original weighted connectome, a binary version of the same topology, and a square-root transformation of the original edge weights. To test the role of the empirical network topology itself, 20 degree-preserving randomized connectomes were generated using connected double-edge rewiring [32]. Each null network preserved the number of nodes and edges, the exact degree sequence, graph connectedness, and the complete global set of empirical edge weights, while the positions of individual edges and weights were randomized. All null connectomes were normalized using the same empirical normalization constant, κ = 782, so that the diffusion scale remained comparable with that of the original connectome. Simulating all eight mutations on all 20 null networks produced 160 randomized-network simulations. Global mutation rankings were compared with those from the empirical connectome, while regional patterns were compared using Spearman correlation, cosine similarity, total-variation distance, and overlap between the 10% highest-burden nodes. Spatial propagation was further examined by relating node-level oligomer dynamics to weighted distance from the seed set. Posterior-predictive robustness was evaluated for aggregation timing, peak oligomer burden, cumulative oligomer burden, mutation rankings, and regional spatial patterns. Because the posterior was obtained from synthetic summaries generated by the same mechanistic simulator, these analyses measure internal robustness and do not constitute external biological or clinical validation.

# 3.1 Mutation-specific aggregation dynamics on the empirical connectome

The strongest separation was observed for oligomer threshold-crossing time. WT reached the fixed threshold at 110.67 model-time units, whereas A2V crossed later at 117.27. H6R and A21G remained close to WT, with crossing times of 110.10 and 110.00, respectively. # 3.2 Mutation-score uncertainty and alternative kinetic mecha-nisms 

Mutation-dependent aggregation timing remained evident when uncertainty in the ex-perimentally measured nucleation scores was propagated through the local aggregation model. Under the primary-nucleation formulation used in the principal network analy-sis, WT reached the local reference threshold at 7.4 model-time units. A2V showed a markedly delayed response: only 64% of its 500 realizations crossed the threshold, and the median crossing time among those realizations was 61.2 model-time units. H6R and A21G remained comparatively close to WT, with median crossing times of 6.3 and 6.1, respectively. In contrast, E22K, D23N, E22Q, and E22G showed progressively earlier crossing, with median times of 3.7, 2.2, 1.1, and 0.6 model-time units, respectively. The magnitude of these effects depended on where mutation sensitivity was introduced into the reaction system. produced a different quantitative response. When both primary and secondary nucleation were scaled, A2V did not attain a finite median crossing time within the simulation window, while the rapidly nucleating variants remained clearly separated from WT (Figure 5). Previous kinetic experiments similarly indicate that familial A β42 substitutions can alter nucleation pathways rather than producing a uniform change in all aggregation processes [36]. The present results do not imply that all variants act exclusively through primary nucleation; instead, they show that the mutation-dependent timing signal is preserved while its magnitude depends on the mechanistic mapping used. The distinct behavior of A2V is also consistent with experimental evidence that this variant follows an unusual and context-dependent assembly pathway [38].

# 3.5 Oligomer propagation follows structural distance from the seed regions 

A strong spatial propagation gradient was observed between the posterior-cingulate/ precuneus seed set and the remainder of the connectome. Using the primary 5% mutation-specific oligomer threshold, all 522 non-seed nodes crossed the threshold for every mutation. The Spearman correlation between weighted connectome distance from the seed set and oligomer arrival time was approximately 0.92 across all eight mutation conditions, 18 indicating that regions located farther from the seed set were consistently reached later. The same pattern was apparent when regions were grouped into five connectome-distance quintiles: median oligomer arrival time increased progressively from the nearest to the farthest regions (Figure 8). A complementary spatial relationship was observed when oligomer burden was evalu-ated at the common WT-derived reference time. The correlation between connectome distance and regional oligomer burden was negative for every mutation, ranging from 

−0.926 for A21G to −0.824 for E22G. Thus, regions located farther from the seed set generally showed lower oligomer burden at the same reference time. The consistency of these relationships across mutations suggests that the spatial gradient was primarily a property of connectome-constrained propagation rather than a feature of any single familial variant. This result is consistent with the connectome-based epidemic-spreading analysis of Iturria-Medina et al., in which anatomical connectivity constrained the regional timing and distribution of misfolded-protein propagation [39]. The use of posterior cingulate and precuneus regions as seeds is also biologically compatible with longitudinal amyloid-PET evidence identifying these default-mode regions among the early cortical sites of measurable A β accumulation [25].

# 3.7 Timing and cumulative oligomer burden remain stable under parameter uncertainty 

Propagation of the ABC-SMC parameter uncertainty through the complete 540-node connectome showed that the principal conclusions for mutation-specific timing and cumu-lative oligomer burden were highly stable. Across posterior draws, the mean Spearman correlation between the posterior-predictive and nominal mutation rankings for threshold-crossing time was 0.990, with a median of 1.0. Exact reproduction of the nominal timing ranking occurred in 98% of posterior draws (Figure 9). The posterior probability of crossing the oligomer threshold earlier than WT was 1% for A2V, 99% for H6R, A21G, E22K, and D23N, and 100% for E22Q and E22G (Table 6). E22G was the fastest mutation in every posterior draw, whereas A2V was the slowest in 99% of draws. 20 Cumulative oligomer burden was even more stable than timing. The mutation ranking for oligomer AUC was identical to the nominal ranking in every posterior draw. The posterior probability that cumulative oligomer burden exceeded WT was 0% for A2V and 100% for H6R, A21G, E22K, D23N, E22Q, and E22G (Table 6). Thus, the mutation-specific ordering of cumulative burden was completely preserved under the parameter uncertainty represented by the ABC-SMC posterior. Peak oligomer amplitude was substantially less stable. Its posterior mean mutation-rank correlation was −0.177, the median correlation was −1.0, and the nominal peak ordering was recovered exactly in only 36% of posterior draws. Therefore, exact peak-amplitude ranking was more sensitive to parameter uncertainty than either threshold-crossing time or cumulative oligomer burden. Nevertheless, all 24 nominal scalar predictions evaluated across timing, peak burden, and AUC fell within their corresponding 95% posterior-predictive intervals. Regional spatial patterns were also generally preserved under the selected parameter uncertainty. For WT, the posterior mean spatial Spearman correlation with the nominal regional pattern was 0.981, with a median of 0.989. Taken together, these results indicate that uncertainty in the selected kinetic parameters had relatively little effect on the principal conclusions concerning aggregation timing, cumulative oligomer burden, and broad spatial organization, while exact peak-amplitude ordering remained less certain.

21 3.8 Reaction-level stochasticity preserves ensemble mutation ordering 

The Chemical Langevin implementation reproduced the deterministic reaction drift to numerical precision, with a maximum absolute difference of 8 .67 × 10 −19 . Three hundred independent stochastic trajectories were generated for each of the eight mutation conditions, giving 2400 trajectories in the primary stochastic analysis. At the ensemble level, the mutation-specific median rankings agreed exactly with the deterministic ordering for threshold-crossing time, peak oligomer burden, and oligomer AUC (Spearman ρ = 1 for all three outcomes). Individual stochastic realizations showed greater variability than the ensemble sum-maries. The median replicate-level rank correlation with the deterministic ordering was 0.881 for threshold-crossing time and 0.929 for both peak oligomer burden and oligomer AUC. Thus, individual stochastic trajectories could overlap even though the ordering of the mutation-specific ensemble medians remained unchanged. The probability of crossing the oligomer threshold earlier than WT was 6.3% for A2V, 51.3% for H6R, 54.0% for A21G, 73.7% for E22K, 88.0% for D23N, and 99.3% for both E22Q and E22G (Table 6). The probabilities for H6R and A21G were close to 50%, indicating substantial stochastic overlap with WT, whereas the probabilities for E22Q and E22G showed a much clearer tendency toward earlier crossing. Across complete stochastic realizations, E22G was strictly the fastest mutation in 76.7% of simulations, whereas A2V was strictly the slowest in 90.0%. Reaction-level stochasticity therefore introduced variation among individual trajectories without changing the deterministic ordering when the stochastic responses were summarized at the ensemble level. Increasing the CLE system-size parameter reduced the discrepancy between the stochastic ensemble mean and the corresponding deterministic trajectory. For WT, normalized RMSE decreased from 0.205 at Ω = 100 to approximately 0.035 at Ω = 500 and 0.008 at Ω = 2000. A similar convergence pattern was observed for E22G. Because Ω was not calibrated to measured molecular copy numbers, this analysis was interpreted as a robustness test of reaction-level fluctuations rather than as a quantitatively calibrated estimate of biological molecular noise [34, 35].

24 4 Discussion 

This study developed a mutation-aware aggregation–diffusion framework to examine how experimentally informed differences in A β nucleation can influence simulated aggregation at the scale of the structural brain network. The principal finding is that mutation-specific molecular kinetics and connectome topology played distinguishable roles in the model. Differences in the experimentally derived nucleation scores produced reproducible differences in aggregation timing and cumulative oligomer burden even though all mutation conditions were simulated on the same structural connectome and with the same primary seed configuration. E22G showed the earliest threshold crossing and the largest cumulative oligomer burden, E22Q and D23N showed intermediate-fast behavior, E22K produced a smaller acceleration relative to WT, H6R and A21G remained comparatively close to WT, and A2V showed the slowest deterministic trajectory under the selected mutation-to-rate mapping. Importantly, the largest mutation-dependent differences were observed in timing and cumulative burden rather than in absolute peak oligomer concentration, suggesting that the effect of the mutation-specific parameterization was expressed more strongly through when aggregation developed and how much burden accumulated over time than through a large separation in maximum network-mean oligomer level. This interpretation is consistent with the experimental basis of the model. Seuma et al. used deep mutational scanning to show that sequence variation within A β42 can strongly alter fibril nucleation and that dominant familial Alzheimer disease mutations within the peptide tend to increase nucleation [22]. Experimental kinetic work has also demonstrated that familial substitutions near residues 22 and 23, including E22K, E22G, E22Q, and D23N, alter A β42 

aggregation through changes in nucleation-dependent processes [36]. The particularly strong effect of E22G in the present simulations is biologically compatible with the original characterization of the Arctic APP mutation by Nilsberth et al., who reported substantially enhanced protofibril formation relative to WT A β [37]. The present simulations should not, however, be interpreted as a ranking of clinical severity among familial mutations. They instead show the consequences of applying experimentally informed differences in nucleation phenotype to a common mechanistic and anatomical system. The behavior of A2V illustrates why this distinction between a model-derived kinetic effect and a clinical interpretation is important. The APP A673V mutation corresponding to A2V in the A β sequence has a complex biological phenotype: homozygous carriers have been associated with early-onset Alzheimer-type disease, whereas mixtures of mutant and WT peptide can alter aggregation differently from the mutant peptide alone [40]. Messa et al. subsequently demonstrated that A2V follows a distinctive molecular-assembly pathway and that its aggregation behavior depends on peptide composition and intermolecular interactions [38]. The delayed trajectory in the present model is therefore best interpreted as the consequence of the particular experimental nucleation phenotype and mapping used here rather than as a general statement about the clinical effect of A2V. The mechanism-uncertainty analysis reinforces this point more broadly. Mutation-dependent differences persisted when the experimental score was mapped to alternative reaction pathways, but the magnitude of the separation changed depending on whether mutation sensitivity was assigned to primary nucleation, secondary nucleation, or both. This is biologically 25 reasonable because amyloid formation is governed by several coupled microscopic processes rather than by a single rate constant. Primary nucleation establishes new aggregates, whereas secondary nucleation, elongation, fragmentation, and conversion can subsequently amplify or redistribute aggregate burden [9, 26]. Experimental work by Cohen et al. demonstrated that fibril surfaces can catalyze the formation of new A β42 aggregates through secondary nucleation [26], while broader kinetic analyses have emphasized that several microscopic mechanisms can generate superficially similar macroscopic aggregation curves [9]. The mechanism analysis in the present study therefore does not establish that familial mutations act exclusively through primary nucleation; rather, it shows that experimentally observed mutation differences can remain influential at the network scale while the quantitative magnitude of that influence depends on the biochemical pathway through which they are represented. The global sensitivity analysis further supports a distinction between the local kinetic processes controlling network-wide aggregation and the structural factors controlling its spatial expression. Monomer production, oligomer-to-fibril conversion, primary nucleation, monomer clearance, fragmentation, secondary nucleation, and elongation all influenced threshold-crossing time to different degrees, whereas the global diffusion parameter ρ

had little association with threshold timing, peak network-mean oligomer burden, or oligomer AUC. Primary nucleation nevertheless remained an important determinant of timing, with increasing knuc associated with earlier threshold crossing, which is consistent with the role of nucleation as an early rate-limiting step in amyloid assembly [9, 22]. At the same time, the comparatively strong effects of monomer production and conversion demonstrate that mutation-specific nucleation cannot be considered in isolation from the rest of the reaction system. Once aggregates are present, the balance among production, conversion, secondary nucleation, fragmentation, elongation, and clearance determines how rapidly oligomer burden accumulates and whether material remains in the oligomeric pool or progresses toward fibrillar states [9, 26]. A notable result was that diffusion became more influential when the outcome explicitly measured spatial spread, despite its weak association with the global timing and burden metrics. This pattern provides a mechanistic interpretation of the overall findings: reaction kinetics largely determine the global pace and cumulative amount of aggregation in this model, whereas interregional transport becomes increasingly important when the question concerns how that burden is distributed through the brain. This separation is also consistent with previous multiscale approaches in which local production or clearance processes interact with structural pathways to determine large-scale protein distribution [39]. The network-robustness experiments provide additional evidence for this kinetic– topological separation. Mutation rankings for timing, peak burden, and cumulative burden were preserved under alternative posterior-cingulate and precuneus seed configu-rations and under weighted, binary, and square-root-transformed representations of the connectome. More importantly, the same global mutation ordering was retained across all 20 degree-preserving randomized networks even though the detailed regional pat-terns were substantially altered. Median correlations between empirical-connectome and randomized-connectome spatial patterns were only approximately 0.19–0.35, and overlap among the highest-burden regions was low. These results indicate that the mutation-26 dependent kinetic signal was sufficiently strong to survive extensive changes in network organization, whereas the identity of the regions carrying the greatest burden depended much more strongly on the empirical topology. This behavior is conceptually consistent with connectome-based models of neurodegeneration. Raj et al. demonstrated that diffusion over anatomical connectivity can generate characteristic spatial patterns of neu-rodegenerative disease [23], while Iturria-Medina et al. developed an epidemic-spreading model in which structural connectivity, pathological-agent propagation, and clearance jointly shaped regional misfolded-protein distributions [39]. The present study adds a different layer to this class of models by allowing the local molecular kinetics themselves to differ between familial A β variants while holding the anatomical scaffold constant. The strong relationship between structural distance and simulated arrival time provides further support for this interpretation. Across all mutations, the distance–arrival Spearman correlation was approximately 0.92, and median arrival time increased progressively from the nearest to the farthest connectome-distance quintile. This result closely parallels the general prediction of network-spreading models that anatomically more distant regions are reached later when transport is constrained by structural pathways [23, 39]. The use of posterior cingulate and precuneus regions as principal seeds is also biologically plausible because longitudinal amyloid-PET studies have identified the precuneus and posterior cingulate among regions showing particularly early A β accumulation in preclinical disease [25]. Nevertheless, the present analysis does not establish that these regions are the unique biological origin of familial amyloid pathology; the seed-robustness results instead show that the major mutation ordering was not dependent on a single exact seed configuration. The synthetic ABC-SMC and posterior-predictive analyses provide a complementary assessment of uncertainty that was not available from the deterministic simulations alone. In the synthetic recovery experiment, the generating values of γ, ksec , kfrag , and kconv all fell within their corresponding 95% posterior intervals, showing that the selected summary statistics contained enough information to recover the known generating values under the assumed simulator and priors. The posterior was not equally concentrated for all param-eters, however, and substantial correlations between parameters, particularly between 

ksec and kconv , indicated compensatory relationships in which different combinations of reaction rates could generate similar summary behavior. This is an important feature of mechanistic models because agreement with an observed trajectory does not necessarily imply that every microscopic parameter is uniquely identified. The ABC-SMC analysis is an internal parameter-recovery and practical-identifiability experiment rather than biological calibration; the pseudo-observed summaries were generated from the same model used for inference, and no patient biomarker data were used to construct the posterior [33]. When the resulting parameter uncertainty was propagated through the complete connectome, the major conclusions for threshold timing and cumulative oligomer burden were remarkably stable. The mean rank correlation for threshold timing was 0.990, exact timing order was recovered in 98% of posterior draws, and the oligomer-AUC ranking was preserved in every draw. In contrast, exact peak-amplitude ranking was unstable under parameter uncertainty, demonstrating that not every model output was equally robust. This difference is scientifically useful rather than problematic: it identifies timing and cumulative burden as more defensible mutation-level outputs of the present framework, 27 while suggesting that fine distinctions in peak oligomer amplitude should not be empha-sized without stronger parameter constraints. The posterior-predictive analysis therefore narrows the principal interpretation of the model to those quantities that remained stable when uncertainty in the kinetic parameters was explicitly propagated. Reaction-level stochasticity addressed a different source of uncertainty. The Chemical Langevin formulation introduces fluctuations associated with individual reaction channels rather than changing the underlying kinetic parameter values, and therefore asks whether the deterministic mutation differences persist when the aggregation reactions themselves are noisy. At the ensemble level, the deterministic ordering was preserved for threshold-crossing time, peak oligomer burden, and oligomer AUC, but individual stochastic trajectories showed considerably greater overlap. Near-WT mutations such as H6R and A21G crossed earlier than WT in only about half of individual stochastic realizations, whereas E22Q and E22G crossed earlier in almost all realizations. E22G was the strictly fastest condition in 76.7% rather than 100% of individual stochastic simulations, demonstrating that the stochastic analysis does not imply an invariant ordering in every realization. This distinction between ensemble behavior and individual trajectories is important when interpreting stochastic mechanistic models. The Chemical Langevin equation is an approximation to reaction-level stochastic dynamics that becomes increasingly consistent with deterministic behavior as the effective system size increases [34, 35]. The observed convergence toward the deterministic trajectories as Ω increased is therefore expected and provides an internal numerical check on the stochastic formulation. However, Ω was not calibrated to molecular copy numbers in brain tissue, so the widths of the stochastic distributions should not be interpreted as estimates of patient-to-patient biological variability. Instead, the CLE analysis demonstrates that the principal mutation-dependent tendencies are not artifacts of a purely deterministic solver while simultaneously showing that stochastic fluctuations can blur distinctions between mutations whose deterministic trajectories are close to WT. Several limitations define the scope of these findings. The mutation-specific nucleation scores were converted into kinetic-rate multipliers through an exponential mapping, which is a modelling assumption rather than a direct estimate of microscopic rate constants. The three-state monomer–oligomer–fibril system is intentionally coarse grained and does not capture the full complexity of A β aggregation [9]. In addition, the Budapest Reference Connectome represents a healthy consensus network rather than disease- or mutation-specific anatomy [21], and important processes such as vascular amyloid deposition, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and region-specific clearance are not included. Simulation time is expressed only in model-time units and should not be interpreted as clinical years or age at onset. Finally, the ABC-SMC analysis used synthetic pseudo-observations and therefore demonstrates internal parameter recoverability rather than external validation. Future work should focus on calibration against mutation-specific in vitro kinetics and validation using longitudinal amyloid-PET or fluid-biomarker data. Within these limits, the model supports the conclusion that mutation-dependent kinetics primarily influence the relative timing and cumulative burden of aggregation, whereas connectome structure mainly shapes its spatial distribution. 28 5 Conclusion 

This study developed a mutation-aware mechanistic framework for examining how familial Aβ variants may influence simulated aggregation and propagation across a structural brain network. Experimentally derived mutation-specific nucleation phenotypes were incorporated into a coarse-grained Smoluchowski-type aggregation–fragmentation model and coupled across the Budapest Reference Connectome through graph diffusion. Within this framework, mutation-dependent differences in local aggregation kinetics were sufficient to generate distinct network-level trajectories even when the underlying connectome and principal seed configuration were held constant. The most consistent differences were observed in aggregation timing and cumulative oligomer burden. E22G showed the earliest propagation and greatest cumulative oligomer burden, E22Q and D23N showed intermediate-fast dynamics, H6R and A21G remained comparatively close to WT, and A2V showed delayed dynamics under the selected mutation-to-rate mapping. These results should be interpreted as consequences of the experimentally informed kinetic parameterization rather than as a ranking of clinical mutation severity. A major finding of the study was that molecular kinetics and structural network topology played related but distinguishable roles. Global sensitivity analysis showed that aggregation timing and cumulative burden were influenced mainly by kinetic parameters, including monomer production, nucleation, conversion, fragmentation, elongation, and clearance, whereas the overall diffusion scale contributed more strongly when spatial spread was considered. Consistent with this distinction, mutation rankings were preserved across alternative seed configurations, alternative connectome-weight representations, and all 20 degree-preserving randomized connectomes, while the detailed regional oligomer patterns changed substantially after network randomization. The strong positive relationship between weighted connectome distance and oligomer arrival time further showed that structural organization constrained the spatial progression of the simulated burden: regions located farther from the posterior-cingulate/precuneus seed set were systematically reached later. The uncertainty and stochastic analyses further clarified which model conclusions were most robust. The synthetic ABC-SMC experiment recovered the known generat-ing values of the selected parameters within their 95% posterior intervals, while also revealing differences in practical identifiability and correlations among kinetic parameters. Propagation of this parameter uncertainty through the complete connectome showed that mutation-specific timing and cumulative oligomer burden were highly stable: the mean posterior-predictive rank correlation for timing was 0.990 and the oligomer-AUC ranking was preserved in every posterior draw. In contrast, exact peak-amplitude ordering was substantially more sensitive to parameter uncertainty, indicating that peak burden should be interpreted more cautiously than timing or cumulative burden. Reaction-level stochastic simulations using the Chemical Langevin equation produced a complementary result. Individual trajectories showed considerable variability and overlap, particularly for mutations close to WT, but the mutation ordering of the ensemble medians remained identical to the deterministic ordering for threshold-crossing time, peak oligomer burden, and oligomer AUC. 29 Taken together, the analyses support a specific interpretation of the model: familial mutation-dependent aggregation kinetics primarily determine the relative timing and cu-mulative magnitude of simulated A β burden, whereas the structural connectome primarily shapes the spatial route through which that burden is expressed. This conclusion was supported across deterministic simulations, mutation-score uncertainty, global sensitivity analysis, seed and edge-weight perturbations, randomized-connectome experiments, spatial propagation analysis, synthetic ABC-SMC recovery, posterior-predictive simulations, and reaction-level stochastic modelling. At the same time, the framework remains a mechanis-tic proof of concept rather than a clinically calibrated predictive model. Simulation time is expressed in model-time units, the mutation scores are relative nucleation phenotypes rather than measured microscopic rate constants, the connectome represents a healthy consensus network, and the model does not incorporate tau pathology, neuroinflammation, vascular amyloid deposition, region-specific clearance, neuronal loss, or patient-specific biomarker trajectories. Future extensions should therefore focus on mutation-specific kinetic calibration, external validation using longitudinal amyloid-PET or fluid-biomarker data, incorporation of regionally varying clearance and additional pathological processes, and evaluation on individual or disease-specific structural networks. Such developments would allow the present molecular-to-network framework to move beyond internal mech-anistic robustness toward stronger biological calibration and eventual subject-specific modelling.
