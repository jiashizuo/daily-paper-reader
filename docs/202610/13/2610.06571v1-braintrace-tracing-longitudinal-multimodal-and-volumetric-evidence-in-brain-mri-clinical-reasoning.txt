Abstract 

Brain MRI interpretation is a longitudinal clinical reasoning problem: radiologists compare serial studies, integrate information across MRI sequences, localize find-ings within volumetric anatomy, and translate this evidence into report-grounded assessments. Existing medical VQA and 3D imaging benchmarks capture impor-tant parts of this workflow, but often evaluate brain MRI through isolated images, static volumes, or ungrounded report-style answers, thereby obscuring failures in the evidence chain that support clinical validity. We introduce BrainTRACE , a report-grounded benchmark for evaluating whether vision-language models can trace the evidence structure required for longitudinal brain MRI interpretation. BrainTRACE contains 7,273 scored VQA instances derived from 1,778 longitu-dinal patients, 7,299 MRI studies, and approximately 29k co-registered 3D MRI sequence volumes. The benchmark is organized by five levels of clinical reasoning, from acquisition recognition to case-level synthesis, and by evidence demands covering longitudinal comparison, report-grounded references, multi-sequence integration, and volumetric spatial evidence. BrainTRACE supports rendered in-puts compatible with standard VLM interfaces, a 3D-evidence condition, and a decomposed case-reasoning track that audits six steps in a longitudinal evidence chain. Evaluation of 20 VLM configurations shows that current systems can identify isolated visual cues but rarely compose them into grounded longitudinal interpretations. We release the benchmark specification, evaluation lists, scoring implementation, scoring rubrics, and audit-record format to support reproducible progress in brain MRI VLM evaluation. 1

BrainTRACE then localizes failures through two complementary annotations. The clinical reasoning depth axis follows the workflow of longitudinal brain MRI interpretation, from acquisition recog-nition and single study characterization to interval comparison, trajectory tracking, and case-level synthesis. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We formalize brain MRI VLM evaluation as longitudinal evidence tracing, where clinical validity depends on comparing serial studies and grounding conclusions in the relevant imaging and clinical references. • We instantiate this definition as BrainTRACE, a 7,273 instance benchmark with hidden report and measurement derived references, diagnostic two-axis annotation, three evaluation tracks, exact closed-form scoring, and factual audit scoring for open-ended and case-level answers. • We evaluate 20 model configurations covering general, medical, frontier, and native volume VLMs, and identify a longitudinal evidence composition gap: the best broad compatible closed-form accuracy is 41.9%, but the strongest open-ended clinical pass is 19.8%, and the best case success rate is 1.4%. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Medical VQA Benchmarks 

Medical VQA benchmarks have expanded from selected 2D radiology images to broader, volumetric, temporal, and reasoning-oriented evaluation. VQA-RAD [27] and SLAKE [32] established radiology VQA on selected 2D medical images, while GMAI-MMBench [8] broadened evaluation across departments, modalities, and task types. Medical-Diff-VQA [19] tests paired chest-X-ray difference reasoning, and CXReasonBench [28] evaluates structured diagnostic reasoning in chest X-rays. M3D-Bench [2] evaluates 3D medical image understanding, CT-RATE [16] pairs CT volumes with radiology text, SGMRI-VQA [36] introduces frame indexed spatial grounding for volumetric MRI, and 3D-RAD [13] adds longitudinal temporal diagnosis on CT scans. These benchmarks cover important axes of medical VLM evaluation, but they do not define brain MRI evaluation as report-grounded longitudinal evidence tracing. Brain MRI follow-up is sequence, volume, and time dependent: a clinically valid answer may require comparing serial studies, integrating sequence-specific findings, localizing volumetric burden, and preserving that evidence through clinical synthesis. 32.2 Brain Imaging Benchmarks and Resources 

Brain imaging benchmarks address brain data more directly, but they still do not make longitudi-nal brain MRI follow-up the central unit of report-grounded VLM evaluation. OmniBrainBench [40] evaluates multimodal brain imaging comprehension clinical tasks, largely through 2D visual evidence. NOVA [6] stress tests out-of-distribution anomaly localization across rare brain MRI pathologies. BrainMD [48] bridges 2D VLMs and 3D brain MRI through slice selection, while AutoRG-Brain/RadGenome-Brain [29] grounds cross-sectional brain MRI report generation. Com-plementary resources provide important but different signals: BraTS [34] established a multi-contrast tumor segmentation, OASIS-3 [24] provides longitudinal neuroimaging cohorts, and longitudinal MRI methods such as Reuter et al. [44] motivates registration-aware serial comparison. BrainTRACE differs by making longitudinal brain MRI follow-up the central unit of VLM evaluation. It jointly tests report-grounded comparison across longitudinal studies, multi-sequence MRI integra-tion, volumetric localization, burden assessment, and clinical reasoning beyond surface description. Table 1 summarizes benchmark-level coverage of these evidence attributes. Broader datasets and non-benchmark resources are included in Appendix A. 

3 BrainTRACE Design and Construction 

3.1 From clinical records to evaluation instances 

BrainTRACE treats each VQA item as a report-grounded clinical evidence test rather than a generic image question pair. Each item is derived from a longitudinal brain MRI record containing serial studies, available MRI sequences, radiology reports, and segmentation-derived measurements. A key design is to decouple what the model is allowed to observe from what defines clinical correctness .Concretely, each released instance is organized into three components: • Model-facing input: the permitted visual evidence, the question, the required answer format, and allowed non-leaking metadata. • Hidden reference: the report or measurement derived answer target, required facts, accepted variants, and clinical material contradictions used for scoring. • Diagnostic labels: the clinical reasoning depth, evidence demand tag, and evaluation track used to localize failures. For closed-form questions, the hidden reference is a normalized target answer. For open-ended and case-level questions, it further specifies the facts that must be present and the contradictions that would make an answer clinically unacceptable. Reports and measurements determine what is clinically supported, while models receive only the permitted visual evidence. 

3.2 Tasks, evidence demands, and evaluation tracks 

Each BrainTRACE item is annotated along three diagnostic dimensions: what clinical operation is being tested, what evidence is required, and under which input condition the item is evaluated. This separation is central to the benchmark: a failure may reflect the wrong clinical operation, missing evidence use, or an unsupported clinical synthesis. 

Clinical reasoning depth. We first instantiate the clinical reasoning depth axis as a five-level task taxonomy. L1 covers acquisition and basic detection. L2 covers single study characterization, including burden, location, and appearance. L3 tests interval comparison and disease-specific effects. L4 tests trajectory, timing, and treatment response. L5 covers case-level synthesis. This taxonomy makes failures clinically localizable: an answer can fail because the model missed the abnormality, compared the wrong timepoints, misread burden evolution, or produced a synthesis unsupported by the available evidence. Appendix B.4 defines the clinical reasoning depth and example question families. Table 6 in Appendix gives the per-track denominators and task family inventory.

Evaluation tracks. BrainTRACE instantiates these annotations through three evaluation tracks. The broad compatible track contains 5,653 instances and renders evidence into standard image inputs, allowing both open-weight and closed-source VLMs to be evaluated under the same interface. The 3D-evidence track contains 1,200 items and evaluates volumetric clinical targets using rendered multi-slice evidence or supported native-volume inputs. The case reasoning track contains 70 longitudinal cases and 420 decomposed VQA steps. It makes the longitudinal evidence chain explicit (as shown in Figure 2) by decomposing each case into six report-grounded VQA steps: anchor localization, early interval change, interval characterization, burden trajectory, latest status, and final synthesis. This decomposition turns an opaque case-level failure into a localized failure along the evidence path. 

3.3 Report-grounded benchmark construction 

BrainTRACE is derived from the publicly released MR-RATE corpus [12]. We retain patients with sufficient longitudinal context for evidence chain questions: each retained patient has at least three MRI studies with paired imaging, radiology reports, and segmentation-derived anatomical measurements. Each retained study is centered on a T1-weighted acquisition and may include co-registered T2-weighted, FLAIR, SWI, or other clinically relevant sequences. The benchmark targets longitudinal brain MRI follow-up studies from MR-RATE, rather than non-longitudinal referrals or the full neuroradiology spectrum. Appendix B.2 reports the upstream corpus scale and retained cohort composition. The clinical references come from authentic MR-RATE radiology reports rather than synthetic report text. In the MR-RATE release, reports are anonymized and structured into clinical information, technique, findings, and impression fields through an iterative LLM-assisted construction procedure. 5Figure 3: BrainTRACE constructs report-grounded VQA instances through a human-in-the-loop pipeline that separates hidden clinical references from model-facing visual evidence. Reports and segmentation-derived measurements define answer targets, required facts, contradictions, and scoring rubrics. Models receive only the permitted visual inputs. BrainTRACE uses these structured reports as source clinical documents, then links report-supported findings to available imaging studies and segmentation-derived measurements. Figure 3 summarizes the human-in-the-loop construction pipeline. The process first identifies report-supported evidence statements, including findings, anatomical locations, interval relations across studies, disease burden statements, disease status language, and quantitative measurements. Template eligibility rules then decide which statements can support a task: for example, an interval change question requires a finding described at comparable timepoints, whereas a measurement question requires a segmentation-derived value aligned to the relevant study. Eligible evidence is converted into a task card containing the question, answer format, hidden ground truth target or rubric, diagnostic labels, and the rendered visual input under the specified evidence condition. Human reviewers inspect the task cards and intermediate outputs throughout this process, checking report support, timepoint alignment, visual evidence availability, answer format validity, redaction, and scoring consistency. Appendix Section B.1 describes the model-facing and non-model-facing records used to reconstruct and audit these steps. 

3.4 Quality Control Ground truth control. BrainTRACE stores a non-model-facing hidden reference for each item, including the target answer, required supporting facts, clinically material contradictions, and scoring criteria. Closed-form items use a lightly normalized target answer. Open-ended items use a rubric of required facts, optional facts, and record contradicting statements. For case reasoning items, each decomposed VQA step has its own reference, so the evaluation can identify where the evidence path breaks. The case reasoning track receives item level review by an American Board of Radiology (ABR) certified radiation oncologist, including the question, ground truth, and audit rubric for each decomposed step. 

Leakage control. BrainTRACE controls leakage by separating inputs from hidden clinical records. During evaluation, models receive only the rendered visual evidence, the question, the answer format, and allowed non-leaking metadata. They do not receive radiology reports, parsed report fields, segmentation masks, measurement tables, or hidden reference fields. Metadata is redacted when it could reveal the answer. Each instance passes quality control checks for redaction, report support, visual evidence availability, and scoring consistency. 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate 20 model configurations spanning open-weight general VLMs, open-weight medical VLMs, closed-source frontier VLMs, and available native-volume medical baselines (Table 2). All models are evaluated zero-shot using only the permitted model-facing visual evidence, question, answer format, and allowed non-leaking metadata. Reports, segmentation masks, parsed findings, measurements, and hidden reference fields are never shown. Each model receives one attempt per item. Malformed outputs, refusals, and abstentions remain in the denominator. For 3D evidence items, general VLMs use released rendered inputs, while native-volume medical baselines use their supported volume input interfaces. Thus, 3D evidence results compare supported interfaces on shared clinical targets rather than isolating native-volume representation as a controlled variable. 

4.2 Evaluation Metrics Closed-form accuracy. Closed-form questions are scored by exact match after light answer nor-malization and uniform removal of visible inline reasoning blocks. We report subclass accuracy by clinical reasoning depth and overall accuracy separately for the broad compatible and 3D evidence tracks. 

Open-ended and case-level clinical pass. Open-ended answers and decomposed case-level steps are scored by a fixed factual clinical audit. 95 Conclusion 

We introduced BrainTRACE , a report-grounded benchmark for longitudinal brain MRI VLM evalu-ation. BrainTRACE asks whether models can trace evidence across serial studies, MRI sequences, volumetric anatomy, and report-derived clinical references, rather than answer isolated image ques-tions. The main finding is not simply that current VLMs score low, but that their successes do not compose: local recognition rarely survives through longitudinal, report-grounded clinical synthesis. This gap persists under current 3D evidence and native-volume interfaces, suggesting that volumet-ric access alone is insufficient without stronger multi-sequence, temporal, and evidence-grounded reasoning. B Dataset construction and task coverage 

This appendix chapter documents how the retained MR-RATE follow-up cohort is converted into auditable evaluation instances. The emphasis is on provenance, release boundaries, leakage control, 15 and task coverage: which records are model-facing, which records define hidden report-grounded references, and which clinical reasoning depth and evidence attributes each task family is intended to test. 

B.1 Construction and audit records 

The construction record has two purposes: it defines what an evaluated model may see, and it preserves the evidence needed to audit the reference after scoring. For item i, we write the model-facing record as 

Xi = {qi, a i, m i, v i},

where qi is the rendered question, ai is the answer format and any visible options, mi is the permitted metadata, and vi is the rendered visual evidence. The non-model-facing reference record is 

Ri = {yi, r i, e i, c i},

where yi is the target answer or reference response, ri is the rubric, ei is the report- or measurement-supported evidence, and ci records exclusion, redaction, and scoring checks. Only Xi is shown during model evaluation. Ri is retained for reconstruction, leakage review, factual audit, and reproducibility. 

1. Clinical eligibility and task specification. Each task family first fixes the clinical reasoning depth to be tested, the required evidence attributes, the answer format, and the allowed metadata. Eligibility rules then determine whether a retained patient and study sequence can support that question. For example, an interval-change item requires comparable findings across timepoints, whereas a volumetric item requires a segmentation-derived measurement linked to the relevant study. 

2. Reference formation. Eligible report statements and segmentation-derived measurements are converted into a hidden reference. Closed-form items store a normalized target answer. Open-ended items store required facts, critical rubric slots, accepted paraphrases, and clinically material contradictions. Case-reasoning items store the same information separately for each of the six decomposed steps, so a failure can be assigned to a specific part of the longitudinal reasoning sequence rather than only to the final case answer. 

3. Model-facing instance construction. The released question record contains the text seen by the model, the answer format, any answer options, the permitted demographic or timepoint metadata, and the visual-evidence specification. It does not include the source report, evidence quotations, segmentation masks, measurement tables, or the hidden reference. Broad-compatible models receive rendered views; 3D-evidence items additionally preserve the volume pathway needed by native-volume baselines. 

4. Leakage and evidence-availability review. Before release, each item is checked for redaction, report support, visual-evidence availability, and consistency between the visible question and the hidden reference. Closed-form items also receive a text-only leakage probe using a stripped version of the question and metadata. Items that fail rendering or reference checks are listed in the exclusion records rather than removed without trace. 

5. Scoring and reproducibility. During evaluation, model outputs are joined back to the hidden reference only at scoring time. Closed-form answers are scored by deterministic normalization and exact match. Open-ended and case-level answers are graded against the factual-audit rubric described in Appendix C.1. The companion repository contains the corresponding scoring specification, rendering instructions, evaluation lists, exclusion records, and scoring implementation: https: //github.com/lanqz7766/BrainTRACE . These materials define the released denominator and allow an external user to reconstruct the benchmark from the same MR-RATE access conditions. 

B.2 Upstream MR-RATE source and retained follow-up cohort 

BrainTRACE is constructed from MR-RATE [12], a public MRI corpus with paired clinical radiology reports. The upstream corpus contains 83,425 patients, 98,334 MRI studies, and 705,254 MRI series or metadata rows. BrainTRACE retains the longitudinal subset needed for follow-up reasoning: 1,778 patients, 7,299 MRI studies, and 65,974 series-level metadata rows, with at least three MRI studies per 16 retained patient. This source design makes provenance auditable and keeps the references grounded in authentic, anonymized radiology reports rather than generated report text. It does not imply population representativeness for all neuroradiology indications. B.4 Clinical-level taxonomy 

BrainTRACE organizes questions by the clinical reasoning depth required to answer them, rather than treating all items as interchangeable visual recognition tasks. The levels therefore define what kind of evidence operation is being tested; they are not merely ordered difficulty bins. • L1: Image and acquisition recognition. These questions test whether a model can identify acquisition properties and basic visual findings, including sequence, plane, laterality, focal abnormality, and concise image description. • L2: Anatomical localization and single-study finding recognition. These questions require lesion counting, anatomical localization, size or volume categorization, signal and enhancement characterization, morphology assessment, and asymmetry recognition within a single study. • L3: Disease-specific interpretation and interval change. These questions compare find-ings across studies, including size change, new lesion appearance, resolution, enhancement evolution, mass-effect change, interval summaries, and volume change. 18 • L4: Longitudinal trajectory or quantitative change assessment. These questions re-quire reasoning over trajectories, including progression pattern, first onset, peak timepoint, narrative trajectory, response category, and serial volume change. • L5: Case-level and integrated clinical judgment. These questions require synthesis across visual evidence and clinical context, including impression generation, disease-status assessment, comparative summaries, and differential diagnosis with supporting evidence. C.1 Factual-audit scoring for open-ended and case-level answers 

Open-ended and case-level answers are scored by factual clinical audit rather than lexical similarity. A response passes only if it covers the required clinical facts, satisfies the critical rubric slots, avoids clinically material contradictions, and does not introduce unsupported claims that would alter the interpretation. This distinction is necessary for radiology-style answers: fluent prose can still fail if it changes laterality, reverses interval direction, assigns a finding to the wrong anatomy, cites the wrong timepoint, or asserts an unsupported disease-status conclusion. For each open-ended item or decomposed case step i, the evaluator receives the question qi, the model answer yi, the reference answer ri, accepted paraphrases when available, required clinical facts, critical rubric slots, and pre-specified failure criteria. The evaluator does not receive the source report, segmentation tables, evidence quotations, or construction record. All evaluated outputs use the same judge model version, instruction text, response schema, and deterministic decoding setting, and the complete judge record is archived for each item. 20 C.1.1 Scoring fields and pass rule 

For each item or decomposed case step, the judge record contains the following fields. These fields define the scoring variables used in the reported metrics: • required_fact_results : checks whether named findings, locations, laterality, time-points, trajectory direction, and current status required by the reference are explicitly present or clinically equivalent. • slot_results : evaluates each rubric slot against the model answer. Critical slots enter the pass rule; noncritical slots are retained for coverage and quality analyses. • critical_errors : records hard contradictions such as wrong anatomy, wrong lateral-ity, wrong interval direction, wrong timepoint, wrong modality-specific reading, wrong diagnosis, or clinically material severity errors. • unsupported_claims : records claims not supported by the reference or rubric, such as a new lesion, disease progression, treatment response, or diagnosis absent from the clinical reference. • clinically_acceptable : gives the final rubric-based factual verdict used for Pass %. • quality_score : records graded answer quality on the same 1–5 clinical scale used in the human validation subset. 

Pass rule. Let Fi = 1 if all required facts for item i are present or clinically equivalent, Si = 1 if all critical rubric slots are satisfied, Ei = 1 if no critical error is present, and Ui = 1 if no clinically material unsupported claim is present. The item-level pass indicator is 

Pi = Fi · Si · Ei · Ui.

Equivalently, an answer fails if it omits a required fact, misses a critical rubric slot, contradicts the reference, or introduces a clinically material unsupported claim.

C.4 Per-step attrition in case-reasoning 

Figure 4 gives the per-step diagnostic: Panel A tracks cumulative case survival, while Panel B shows which decomposed reasoning steps pass before a case collapses. Claude Opus 4.6 has the highest final-synthesis pass rate among the displayed rows (7.1%), but still has 0/70 full-case survival; GPT-5-mini has the highest aggregate Step Pass (24.5%) but also reaches 0/70 full-case survival. The GPT-5-family rows remain closely spaced in aggregate Step Pass, as shown by the consolidated bootstrap intervals in Figure 11. Increasing GPT-5.4 reasoning effort redistributes successes across step types but does not improve aggregate Step Pass, reinforcing that the bottleneck is evidence propagation and final synthesis rather than a single isolated recognition step.

E Evidence-channel ablation details 

This appendix clarifies the matched evidence-channel comparisons used in Section 4.3. These comparisons are diagnostic: they test whether accuracy on selected closed-form items depends on longitudinal context, multi-sequence information, and visual evidence when the question text, answer options, permitted non-image fields, and scoring rule remain fixed. They are not intended as population-wide performance estimates over all BrainTRACE items. 

E.1 Evidence retained in each comparison 

The latest-timepoint condition retains the current study but removes prior longitudinal studies. Its paired accuracy drop measures dependence on serial context, including interval change and trajectory evidence. The T2-only condition retains the longitudinal T2 series but removes non-T2 sequences. Its drop measures dependence on multi-sequence contrast and sequence-specific findings, including enhancement or susceptibility cues when those are needed by the reference. The no-image condition withholds visual inputs while keeping the same question and answer space. Its drop measures residual dependence on question text, answer priors, or permitted non-image cues when rendered MRI evidence is absent. 28 E.2 Finding and interpretation 

Figure 11 gives the paired bootstrap intervals for these evidence-channel drops. The intervals are computed within each matched comparison, so the appendix uses them to support the direction and stability of the drop rather than to introduce another leaderboard. Across the retained comparisons, the paired drops remain negative for both audited models. This supports the paper’s main interpretation that the selected BrainTRACE items require the retained evidence channels, not only answer priors or generic question text. The matched item sets were constructed to share a common full-evidence reference accuracy of 50.0%, making the paired drops directly comparable within this diagnostic design. Because the ablation set is calibrated to this 50.0% baseline, the results should be interpreted as paired diagnostic drops rather than population-level estimates. Abstentions in the no-image condition are counted as incorrect under the same closed-form exact-match rule used throughout the benchmark. The companion release provides the evaluated items for each comparison, ensuring that the denominators underlying the paired intervals remain auditable.

BrainTRACE evaluates longitudinal reasoning over brain MRI follow-up studies in an MR-RATE-derived cohort. Its task families assume multi-sequence brain imaging, English language radiology reports, and cases with sufficient follow-up, report, and segmentation-derived evidence. It is not intended to represent other anatomies, other languages, one-off clinical referrals with sparse lon-gitudinal context, or the full spectrum of neuroradiology indications. Its scores are designed for reproducible model comparison, not for estimating absolute clinical performance. Users should report per-level, per-attribute, and acquisition subgroup results rather than treating a single aggregate score as a clinical capability measure. The benchmark is report-grounded rather than independently re-read from scratch. The references come from authentic MR-RATE clinical radiology reports and segmentation-derived measurements, and released questions, ground truth, and audit fields pass construction and consistency checks. The case reasoning track further receives item-level review by an ABR-certified radiation oncologist. Even with these controls, reports are clinical documents rather than pixel-level annotations. A report may compress timepoints, omit a visually apparent but clinically secondary finding, or express uncertainty, and segmentation-derived measurements can carry registration, acquisition, and algorithmic error. Scores should be read as agreement with the reviewed, report-grounded clinical references under the released benchmark specification, not as a new adjudicated imaging diagnosis or deployment certification. The primary open-ended scores use a GPT family factual audit judge, and a small cross-family audit in Appendix C.6 shows judge family sensitivity for GPT-5.4 outputs. The 3D evidence track tests a clinically important evidence condition, but its rendered 16-slice and native-volume pathways introduce an input interface confound. These results should be read as comparisons among available model interfaces on shared clinical targets, not as a controlled ablation of 3D representation alone. BrainTRACE releases evaluation records for audit, but it does not redistribute raw imaging. Users must obtain access to the upstream MR-RATE corpus [12] and follow the corresponding data-use terms. 35
