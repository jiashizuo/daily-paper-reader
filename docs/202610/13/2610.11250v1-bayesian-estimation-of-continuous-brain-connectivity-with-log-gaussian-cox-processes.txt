# Abstract 

Continuous structural connectivity describes the connection between any two points of the cortical surface by an intensity function. Based on the endpoints of tractography streamlines, existing methods estimate this function mostly by kernel smoothing, which only gives a point estimate. We propose a Bayesian framework that estimates continuous connectivity with uncertainty quantification. The endpoints of tractography streamlines, mapped to two spheres, are modeled by a log-Gaussian Cox process on the product of the spheres with a Mat´ ern Gaussian process prior. With the finite element representation of the prior, the Laplace approximation of the posterior enables the computation on a fine mesh with over 400 ,000 latent variables per hemisphere pair. Numerical experiments show the accuracy of the point estimate of this approach and the coverage of its credible intervals. In the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, the proposed method predicts held-out streamlines better than kernel smoothing. 1arXiv:2610.11250v1 [stat.ME] 8 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

The structural connectome is the network of white matter fibers that connect brain regions [Park and Friston, 2013, Fornito et al., 2013]. Its organization is linked to cognitive abilities, and its alteration is associated with many neurological and psychiatric disorders [Craddock et al., 2013, Caznok Silveira et al., 2024]. Diffusion MRI and tractography reconstruct these fibers non-invasively as a large set of streamlines [Zhang et al., 2022], from which the structural connectivity is estimated. The standard approach parcellates the cortex into regions of interest by a brain atlas [Desikan et al., 2006, Glasser et al., 2016] and counts the streamlines between each pair of regions. The resulting connectivity matrix and the subsequent conclusions depend on the choice of the atlas [Zalesky et al., 2010, de Reus and Van den Heuvel, 2013, Arslan et al., 2018], and averaging over large regions loses the spatial detail within each region [Moyer et al., 2017]. Continuous structural connectivity avoids the parcellation [Moyer et al., 2017, Cole et al., 2021, Mansour L et al., 2022]. Here, the streamline endpoints are modeled as a point process on the cortical surface, and the target is the intensity function L(x, y ) of the connections between any two points x and y of the cortex. This representation is more reproducible and predicts behavioral traits better than the region-based one [Cole et al., 2021, Consagra et al., 2024]. It also allows the joint alignment of the connectivity and the cortical surfaces across subjects [Cole et al., 2025]. Existing methods estimate L by kernel density estimation (KDE) [Moyer et al., 2017, Cole et al., 2021, Mansour L et al., 2022], with spherical kernels after the cortical surface is mapped to a sphere [Moyer et al., 2017] or with geodesic distances on the cortical mesh [Mansour L et al., 2022], or with the Riemannian diffusion kernel of the cortical surface [Wang et al., 2025]. Consagra et al. [2026] represent the density by a neural network on the product of two spheres and fit it by penalized maximum likelihood. These estimates depend on the choice of the kernel and the bandwidth or the penalty. Figure 21 illustrates both issues for one subject in which the atlas removes the detail within each region, and the KDE changes substantially with the bandwidth. Moreover, these methods only give a point estimate, so one cannot assess the reliability of the estimate or test, for example, whether the connectivity differs between groups of subjects. Gaussian process (GP) priors provide an alternative. The prior regularizes the estimate toward a smooth function, its smoothness is learned from the data through the hyperparam-eters, and the posterior quantifies the uncertainty of the estimate. However, the connectivity function is defined on a four-dimensional product of cortical surfaces [Consagra et al., 2024], and a high-resolution estimate needs a large number of discretization points. Standard GP computation scales cubically in this number [Rasmussen and Williams, 2006, Liu et al., 2020], which is infeasible at this scale. For density estimation, the logistic GP prior places a GP on the log-density [Leonard, 1978, Lenk, 1991, Tokdar, 2007], and the log-Gaussian Cox process models a point pattern by a Poisson process whose log-intensity is a GP [Møller et al., 1998]. Given the log-intensity and the number of points, the points of the Cox process are independent draws from the density proportional to the intensity, so the two models are closely related. Computation-wise, the latent function is discretized on a grid or a mesh, and the posterior is computed by Markov chain Monte Carlo [Tokdar, 2007, Kim et al., 2026], the Laplace approximation [Ri-ihim¨ aki and Vehtari, 2014], or the integrated nested Laplace approximation with the SPDE representation [Simpson et al., 2016]. On a grid, the Kronecker structure of a separable covariance also gives fast Laplace approximations for non-Gaussian likelihoods [Saat¸ ci, 2012, Flaxman et al., 2015]. However, the number of latent variables on a grid grows exponen-tially with the dimension, which makes these approaches impractical beyond three or four dimensions [Riihim¨ aki and Vehtari, 2014]. We propose a Bayesian framework for continuous structural connectivity based on a log-Gaussian Cox process on the product of two spheres. # 5 Real data application 

We apply the proposed method to the structural connectivity of children in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study [Casey et al., 2018]. The data contain 98 subjects, each scanned at two time points, the baseline and the 2-year follow-up. The subjects were 9 to 11 years old at baseline, and 49 of them were female. Streamlines were reconstructed by tractography, and their endpoints were mapped to the inflated spheres by the surface-based connectivity integration pipeline [Cole et al., 2021]. A scan contains a 15 median of 493 ,340 streamlines, and about 44%, 11%, and 44% belong to the blocks LL, LR, and RR, respectively. For each scan, we split the streamlines at random into two halves, estimate the connec-tivity from one half, and evaluate the estimate on the other half and on the scan at the other time point (Appendix C). All fits use the ico3 mesh. A common θ is selected by maximizing the sum of (18) over two scans on the grid κ ∈ { 8, 14 , 24 , 40 } and τ ∈ { 3, 5, 8}, and the selected ˆθ = (24 , 5) is used for all scans. For each scan, 20 posterior draws are taken as in Section 4, and the posterior mode is used as the point estimate. With ˆθ fixed, the fit of the three blocks of one scan, including the draws, takes about four to five hours on 8 CPU cores. We compare with the heat and Gaussian kernel estimators of Section 4, whose bandwidths are selected by five-fold likelihood cross-validation within the fitted half. Table 2 shows the predictive accuracy of the three methods. The proposed method predicts the held-out half better than both kernel estimators in every scan. Since a difference in the log-score estimates a difference in the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the true distribution of the ico3 region pairs (Appendix C), the proposed estimate is closer to the true connectivity of each scan at this resolution. Across time points, the estimate from one scan of a subject also predicts the other scan of the subject better in almost all comparisons (195 and 189 out of 196 with the Gaussian and heat kernel estimators). This score is lowered both by noise in an estimate, which does not recur in the other scan, and by oversmoothing, which places mass where the other scan has few streamlines. # 6 Conclusion 

This paper introduces a Bayesian framework that estimates continuous structural connec-tivity with uncertainty quantification. Existing methods estimate the connectivity function mostly by kernel smoothing, which gives only a point estimate and depends on the choice of the kernel and the bandwidth. To address this, we model the streamline endpoints by a log-Gaussian Cox process on the product of two spheres with a Mat´ ern Gaussian process prior defined through an SPDE. Computation-wise, the Laplace approximation selects the hyperparameters and gives posterior draws on a mesh with over 400 ,000 latent variables per hemisphere pair, using only products with the Fisher information. Several limitations remain. The posterior describes the uncertainty given the tractogra-phy output under the Poisson model, so it does not include the variation from tractography, such as the seeding and the dependence between streamlines. The Mat´ ern range selected for the real data is comparable to the edge length of the ico3 mesh, so a finer mesh may further improve the estimate at a higher computational cost. The Kronecker structure needs the same mesh on the two spheres, so the method does not directly apply to two different cortical surface meshes. Lastly, integrating over θ instead of fixing it at ˆθ would add the uncertainty of the hyperparameters to the posterior. The posterior draws of each subject can be carried to group-level analyses, such as testing the difference in connectivity between groups of subjects.

# B Implementation details 

The linear systems of Fisher scoring and of the posterior draws are solved by conjugate gradients with a diagonal preconditioner that approximates the diagonal of F . Each Fisher scoring step is followed by a backtracking line search [Nocedal and Wright, 2006, Chapter 3], which halves the step until hθ decreases by at least 10 −4 times the decrease predicted by its gradient. Fisher scoring stops when the decrease of hθ falls below 10 −6 relative or 10 −3

absolute. It is initialized at Z = 0 and βb = log {nb/ P

The stochastic Lanczos quadrature uses 4 Rademacher probes and 30 Lanczos steps, with the same probes for every θ. For the real data, which are fitted with large τ , we allow up to 60 Fisher scoring steps and 400 conjugate gradient iterations per step. In the simulation, (log κ, log τ ) is optimized by a pattern search with initial step log 1 .35, 26 which is halved when no coordinate move improves the marginal likelihood, and at most 8 evaluations. Results with a strict setting (10 −5 tolerances, 8 probes, 40 Lanczos steps, and Nelder–Mead with up to 40 evaluations) agree to four decimals.
