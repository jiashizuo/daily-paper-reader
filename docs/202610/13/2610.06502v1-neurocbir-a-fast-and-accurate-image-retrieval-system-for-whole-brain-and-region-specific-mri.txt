Abstract 

Content-based image retrieval (CBIR) in neuroimaging enables the identification of structurally similar brain scans, supporting diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning; however, existing methods are often limited to small datasets, single brain regions, or coarse class labels, thereby restricting their clinical utility and generalizability. Here, we present NeuroCBIR, a framework for fast and flexible retrieval of both whole-brain and region-specific 3D T1w MRI scans. A total of 103 cortical and subcortical regions are extracted to enable both whole-brain and region-level queries. NeuroCBIR leverages latent representations learned by a variational autoencoder (VAE) combined with contrastive learning, producing scan-specific embeddings that capture anatomical patterns. These embeddings were evaluated for subject re-identification, zero-shot age prediction, and zero-shot multi-class pathol-ogy stratification. Re-identification performance was high across both whole-brain and brain-region levels (mean average precision across the top-5 retrieved images (mAP@5) ≥ 98 .4%), with robust generalization across datasets and acquisition conditions. While NeuroCBIR is not trained for age prediction or pathology stratification, zero-shot evaluations for these two tasks demonstrate that the embeddings encode meaningful information for downstream tasks. Embedding extraction on a 4-core CPU required ∼18.7s per scan, whereas similarity search was e ff ectively instantaneous ( <0.01s). NeuroCBIR is publicly available for brain MRI with 

>26 000 precomputed T1w MRI embeddings. It supports reproducible research, region-specific flexibility, and clinically meaning-ful personalized diagnostic support. The software is available at https://github.com/minnelab/NeuroCBIR .

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in the study and diagnosis of neurological disorders, providing high-resolution, non-invasive insights into brain structure, function, and pathology. With the increasing availability of large-scale neuroimaging datasets, such as those developed for the study of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and normal aging (Jack et al., 2008; Ellis et al., 2009; LaMontagne et al., 2019), the volume of data available to clinicians and researchers has increased greatly. In the context of neu-roimaging, CBIR o ff ers the potential to assist clinicians and scientists by providing fast access to comparable cases, thus facilitating clinical decision making, supporting di ff erential di-agnosis, and enabling population-level analyses by e ffi ciently identifying subjects with similar structural or functional brain patterns across large datasets. In the past 5 years, deep learning has reshaped the field of CBIR. Many available methods use convolutional networks or autoencoders to learn low-dimensional embeddings directly from images. In the work of Deepak and Ameer (2020), T1-weighted tumor MRIs were passed through a pretrained convo-lutional neural network (CNN) and then a 2D siamese embed-ding trained with contrastive loss, achieving a tumor-retrieval performance of mAP@10 ≈ 97 .6% (mAP across the top-10 retrieved images). Likewise, the study by Onga et al. (2019) combined a 3D convolutional autoencoder (3D-CAE) with deep metric learning in patients with AD. It compressed full-resolution 3D MRIs by 4096:1 while preserving AD-related features. The learned embedding improved the separation be-tween AD and cognitively normal (CN) cases by 29.1% com-pared to a vanilla 3D-CAE. Similarly, Nishimaki et al. (2022) proposed Loc-VAE, a localized β-VAE that enforces each la-tent dimension to correspond to a specific brain region. Loc-VAE compressed at 4096:1, preserving disease signals while enhancing “locality” of features. Furthermore, the study by Muraki et al. (2024) described IE-CBIR, an isometric VAE em-bedding with 4096:1 compression. This method achieved an accuracy of 91.5% for discriminating AD vs. CN using the la-tent representation. In a di ff erent line of work, Dogan et al. (2025) combined deep and handcrafted features using a CNN (E ffi cientNet-B0) and Local Binary Patterns (LBP), followed by minimum redundancy–maximum relevance (mRMR) fea-ture selection. This hybrid CBIR-classifier achieved 98.9% accuracy over eight brain-MRI categories (including glioma, meningioma and AD). More recently, Tobari et al. (2025) in-troduced a Style Encoder Adversarial Domain Adaptation (SE-ADA) model to improve CBIR robustness across multi-cohort MRI datasets, achieving an F1-score of 74.7% in AD vs. CN retrieval. Alternatively, the study by Puglisi et al. (2024) addressed subject-level re-identification 3 by generating a fingerprint from 2D T1-weighted MRIs, achieving mAP@3 of 95.5% on the ADNI dataset. While demonstrating the feasibility of retriev-ing previous scans of the same patient, their study was limited to small 2D datasets. Parallel to these MRI-focused e ff orts, Denner et al. (2025) investigated the use of large-scale vision foundation models for medical CBIR in radiology. Using over 1.6 million images spanning multiple modalities and 161 pathologies, they bench-

Their results showed that foun-dation models can achieve competitive retrieval performance, Precision@10 = 58.3%, highlighting the potential of general-purpose embeddings for scaling CBIR to heterogeneous radiol-ogy datasets. The existing CBIR approaches described above are typically constrained by limited datasets, restricted applicability to spe-cific brain regions, and a lack of evaluation across heteroge-neous MRI acquisition protocols. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no publicly available system currently provides a CBIR framework for multiple brain-region MRI data. Our work leverages five large-scale datasets (ADNI, OA-SIS3, AIBL, MIRIAD, and SLIM), enabling evaluation across diverse populations, scanner manufacturers, and field strengths. A key advantage of our proposed CBIR system (NeuroCBIR) is that, although it employs deep learning for image retrieval, the retrieved results consist exclusively of expert-curated, real-world clinical data. We extend CBIR beyond the whole-brain level by supporting retrieval at the brain-region level, covering a total of 103 cortical and subcortical brain regions. This re-gional flexibility enhances medical usability by allowing clin-icians and researchers to query specific regions of interest ac-cording to their diagnostic needs, thereby enabling more precise and context-driven retrieval. From a technical perspective, we introduce a retrieval frame-work that couples VAE-derived latent embeddings with con-trastive learning using a multi-positive ranking loss. This de-sign exploits multiple valid matches per query, producing sta-ble and scalable embeddings that support e ffi cient and anatomi-cally meaningful MRI retrieval (Cheng et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023). A central goal of NeuroCBIR is accurate re-identification of same-subject scans at both whole-brain and region-specific lev-els. Simultaneously, the embedding space is explicitly struc-tured to rank anatomically similar inter-subject samples close to each query while separating dissimilar ones, ensuring that retrieved cases are truly comparable. This provides a reliable foundation for downstream zero-shot analyses and clinical in-terpretation, and enables e ffi cient re-identification within large-scale imaging repositories. Accordingly, the main contributions of this study are: 1. Design a fast and flexible framework for both whole-brain and region-specific retrieval, thereby enhancing clinical applicability. 2. Evaluate retrieval robustness across multiple datasets, MRI acquisition protocols, and imaging domains (scanner manufacturer and field strength). 3. Evaluate whether the learned retrieval embeddings cap-ture clinically meaningful information beyond accurate re-identification, by assessing their utility in zero-shot down-stream tasks such as age estimation and disease risk infer-ence. 4. Publicly release a CBIR system for brain MRI, capable of retrieving the most similar real-world imaging cases to support personalized diagnosis and tailored treatment decisions. We intend to share the source code and pre-computed image features, ensuring reproducibility while respecting data privacy and institutional data-sharing con-straints. 

2. Materials and Methods 

This section describes the MRI datasets and the methodolo-gies employed in this study. Two CBIR frameworks were de-veloped, one based on whole-brain scans and the other on seg-mented brain-regions. Since both frameworks share the major-ity of their processing pipeline, the common components are presented jointly, while the specific di ff erences are clearly indi-cated throughout the text. All source code, trained models, and precom-puted embeddings are publicly available at https: //github.com/minnelab/NeuroCBIR . A reproducibil-ity script ( dev/run_reproducibility_check.py ) enables full replication of model training, embedding extraction, and retrieval evaluation. This ensures that all results reported in the manuscript can be independently reproduced by other researchers. We first introduce the datasets and their corresponding pre-processing steps. Next, we describe the methodological frame-work, which includes: the architecture of the variational au-toencoder (VAE) and its training procedure, as well as the en-coder–projector model architecture and its training using con-trastive learning. Finally, we outline the evaluation metrics ap-plied in the di ff erent experimental scenarios, together with the statistical tests used for performance assessment.

2.2. Imaging prepossessing 

We followed the preprocessing protocol described in Fu et al. (2025). Specifically, we used skull-stripped and 4bias field–corrected T1w images processed according to the FreeSurfer process flow 6 (i.e., norm , 1 mm isotropic). We affi nely registered each image to the FreeSurfer Talairach space. We then rescaled image intensities to [0,1] and cropped them to [160, 176, 208] , starting from [48, 38, 10]. We determined this crop size to preserve most foreground information based on 200 randomly selected scans. We used FreeSurfer-processed brain segmentation masks (i.e., aseg+aparc ) for training and evaluation. For the ADNI and OASIS3 datasets, we obtained segmentations from FreeSurfer v5.3.0 in TheHiveDB platform (Muehlboeck et al., 2014). We segmented the AIBL, MIRIAD, and SLIM datasets using FastSurfer 7 v2.4.2 (Henschel et al., 2020). These masks delineate cortical and subcortical regions, enabling both global and region-specific analyses. In total, we included 103 cortical and subcortical regions in this study. As part of quality control, images with low mutual informa-tion with respect to the FreeSurfer Talairach template were vi-sually inspected, and scans showing evident preprocessing fail-ures were excluded (Iglesias, 2023). The data reported in Ta-ble 2 correspond to the dataset after this screening step. A dataset of brain regions was derived from all original brain datasets. We defined a common bounding box for each of the 103 brain regions using 500 randomly selected brains. We then segmented and cropped all regions based on these bounding boxes and resampled them to a universal size of [64, 64, 64] us-ing trilinear interpolation. As an example, we cropped the left hippocampus using coordinates [57, 31, 0] to [153, 175, 192], yielding an initial size of [96, 144, 192], which we subsequently resampled to [64, 64, 64]. 

2.3. Dataset setup 

We used the ADNI and OASIS3 datasets for training, valida-tion and testing, while reserving the AIBL, MIRIAD, and SLIM datasets exclusively for external testing. The ADNI and OA-SIS3 datasets were split at the subject level, allocating approx-imately 80% of subjects for training, 10% for validation and 10% for testing. Each partition was stratified to maintain the same proportion of dementia patients and healthy subjects. For subjects with longitudinal scans, all MRI examinations from the same individual were assigned the label corresponding to the most recent available clinical assessment. Notice that dur-ing training, we do not use the class, only the subject identity. Two di ff erent datasets were considered: one comprising whole-brain scans and another containing segmented brain re-gions. These were used to train and evaluate the two separate CBIR frameworks.

2.4.1. VAE-GAN 

We used a VAE to learn latent representations because of its inference e ffi ciency (Rombach et al., 2021; Pinaya et al., 2022). Specifically, we followed the VAE-GAN framework from Rom-bach et al. (2021); Pinaya et al. (2022) and trained two separate models for whole-brain and brain-regional data, respectively. We used the AutoencoderKL model from the MONAI li-brary (Cardoso et al., 2022; Rombach et al., 2021; Pinaya et al., 2022). It uses an encoder–bottleneck–decoder structure, where both encoder E and decoder D implement four residual convo-lutional layers with 64, 128, 128, and 128 filters, using group normalization. The discriminator C implements three convolu-tional layers of 32 filters each, using instance normalization. Each encoder block reduces the spatial resolution of the input volume through strided convolutions, progressively compress-ing the feature maps into a latent representation with 8 chan-nels. The resulting latent tensor size thus depends on the in-put field of view: for the whole-brain input, the downsampling yields a latent shape of [8 , 20 , 22 , 26], while for brain-region inputs, it results in [8 , 8, 8, 8]. 2.6. Interpretability 

Region-based retrieval is inherently interpretable because similarity can be directly linked to specific, anatomically de-fined brain regions. In contrast, whole-brain embeddings are high-dimensional and spatially distributed, making it di ffi cult to identify which regions drive similarity-based retrieval. To better understand the regional contributions underlying whole-brain similarity, we performed a correlation analysis between whole-brain and region-specific similarities. A set of 100 whole-brain query scans was randomly se-lected. For each query, we retrieved the top-50, top-200, and top-1000 most similar cases based on the whole-brain embed-ding, excluding scans from the same subject. For these re-trieved cases, cosine similarity scores were also computed in-dependently for each anatomical region using the region-based retrieval pipeline. To quantify each region’s contribution to whole-brain sim-ilarity, we computed the Spearman rank correlation between whole-brain similarity scores and region-specific similarity scores across the retrieved samples. Intuitively, a high correla-tion for a given region indicates that the region alone can largely explain the similarity ranking produced by the whole-brain em-bedding. Correlations were computed separately for each top-k

setting. Finally, to summarize variability in regional contributions, we computed the coe ffi cient of variation (CV), defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of the Spearman cor-relations. CV was evaluated both across regions for each query and across queries for each region. We interpret the CV values as follows: 

• Low CV across regions for a query indicates that all regions contribute consistently to retrieving the top-k

matches for that query. 

• High CV across regions for a query indicates that individ-ual regions vary in their influence on whole-brain similar-ity for that query. 

• Low CV across queries for a region indicates that this re-gion consistently contributes to the top-k retrieval across di ff erent queries, i.e., the retrieval process relies more heavily on this region in general. 

2.7. Bias evaluation 

Each dataset was acquired under di ff erent study protocols and included di ff erent populations. Moreover, within a dataset, MRI acquisition parameters (e.g., scanner manufacturer, field strength) also varied. Such di ff erences may introduce system-atic biases in retrieval performance. To investigate these e ff ects, the NeuroCBIR system was eval-uated using query subsets defined by scanner field strength (1.5T or 3T) and by manufacturer (GE, Philips, or Siemens). In these analyses, only images from a given subset served as queries, while retrieval was performed against the full database. For example, when evaluating 1.5T queries, all 1.5T images were used as queries, but the reference database included all images, keeping the pool of candidate images constant across subsets. For each query, the Prec@5 was computed as the propor-tion of relevant images among the top-5 retrieved results. Col-lecting the Prec@5 values across all queries yields a distribu-tion of retrieval performance for each subset (e.g., 1.5T, 3T, GE, Philips, and Siemens). Since the distributions of Prec@5 along queries did not follow normality, the non-parametric test of Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess statistically signif-icant di ff erences between subsets. Additionally, Cli ff ’s δ was calculated to quantify the e ff ect size, providing a measure of the magnitude of di ff erences independent of sample size. Accord-ing to conventional thresholds, |δ| < 0.15 is considered negligi-ble, 0 .15 ≤ | δ| < 0.33 is small, 0 .33 ≤ | δ| < 0.47 is medium, and |δ| ≥ 0.474 is large (Meissel and Yao, 2024). 92.8. Comparison with pretrained models 

To benchmark the performance of our proposed NeuroCBIR system, we evaluated several commonly used 3D feature extrac-tors trained on medical imaging cohorts. The tested models in-cluded standard 3D ResNet architectures (ResNet-10, ResNet-50, ResNet-101) from MONAI (Hara et al., 2018; He et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2019; Cardoso et al., 2022), a 3D-CNN pre-trained model for brain T1-weighted MRI for AD diagnosis (CNN-AD) (Turrisi et al., 2024), and a large-scale medical vi-sion model based on the transformer Swin UNETR (VoComni-L) (Hatamizadeh et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024; Cardoso et al., 2022). For all ResNet models and VoComni-L, we extracted features from the deepest encoder layers, followed by global average pooling to obtain fixed-size embeddings. Additionally, we evaluated the performance of our VAE-based embeddings projecting with UMAP (McInnes et al., 2020). Additionally, two 3D brain foundational models were added to the comparison: BrainIAC (Tak et al., 2026) and FOMO2JOMO (Munk et al., 2026). BrainIAC was trained on 48 965 brain MRIs with di ff erent contrasts (T1w, T2w, T2-FLAIR, contrast-enhanced T1w) across a broad spectrum of tasks. This model outperformed localized supervised train-ing and other pretrained models, particularly in low-data, few-shot settings and in high-di ffi culty prediction tasks, allowing for application in scenarios otherwise infeasible. The method leverages self-supervised foundation models pretrained using SimCLR and masked autoencoders on 3D brain MRI. For the present work, all images were adapted to fit with their prepro-cessing steps (Tak et al., 2026). In turn, FOMO2JOMO, was the winner of the MICCAI 2025 challenge FOMO25 This method uses a similar loss function to ours for training their model, i.e, they try to pull together images from the same subject while pushing apart those from di ff erent modalities. The method pre-trained a 3D CNN multi-modal UNet-style VAE with 18.9M parameters. This model uses patches of size [64 , 64 , 64] rather than the entire image (Munk et al., 2026). Thus, we used the central cropped brain volume to extract the mean values of the subject-level latent representations in the VAE bottleneck. The extracted embeddings from each model were used to per-form CBIR on the testing data, reporting the compression rate, the number for features, mAP@5, mS@5 and ρMS −SSIM .

2.9. Computational complexity 

The execution time of the NeuroCBIR system is critical for assessing its feasibility in real-time applications. In clinical practice, the dataset of embeddings is precomputed in advance. Therefore, during inference, the main factors that determine effi ciency are loading the Q2E module, processing the query to generate its embedding, and computing the pairwise cosine similarity with the precomputed embedding dataset, followed by sorting and retrieving the top matches. All execution times reported in the current study were ob-tained on a system with the following characteristics: PyTorch was configured to use 1 and 4 CPU threads. The platform was Linux 4.18.0 running on an x86_64 machine with an Intel ®

Xeon ® Gold 6226R CPU @ 2.90 GHz. 

2.10. Application-specific experimental setups 

To further assess the clinical relevance of the learned embeddings beyond retrieval performance, we conducted application-specific evaluations. These analyses test whether the NeuroCBIR framework captures meaningful biological and disease-related patterns in a fully zero-shot setting. Zero-shot evaluation refers to applying the learned Neu-roCBIR embeddings to new tasks, such as age estimation or disease risk inference, without any task-specific training or fine-tuning, allowing us to assess whether the embeddings capture clinically meaningful information in a fully generalizable and unbiased manner (Guo et al., 2025; Bazine et al., 2024). 

2.10.1. Zero-shot similarity-based age inference 

We evaluated whether the NeuroCBIR framework captures age-related morphological patterns without any age or disease supervision. To isolate normative aging e ff ects, the retrieval database was restricted to CN subjects. For scans with repeated acquisitions, only a scan per time point was retained to reduce redundancy. To mitigate the impact of sparse age coverage below 50 years, the retrieval database included only subjects older than 50. 4. Discussion 

In this study, we demonstrated that NeuroCBIR achieves high retrieval (re-identification) performance at both the whole-brain (mAP@5 ∼98.4) and brain-region levels, with most anatomical structures showing consistently strong results. The Spearman’s correlation between the embedding-space and MS-SSIM ( ρMS −SSIM ) further confirms that the learned embeddings preserve meaningful perceptual and semantic similarities be-tween inter-subject scans. These results indicate that the em-beddings capture anatomically meaningful patterns that are consistent not only within subjects but also across di ff erent indi-viduals. Evaluation across multiple large-scale datasets, vary-ing field strengths, and scanner manufacturers further demon-strates that the framework generalizes robustly under heteroge-neous acquisition conditions. Traditional CBIR systems, particularly for brain MRI, have been predominantly evaluated based on their ability to retrieve images sharing the same class labels, such as tumor types or di-agnostic categories like CN vs. AD (Huang et al., 2012; Onga et al., 2019; Nishimaki et al., 2022; Muraki et al., 2024; To-bari et al., 2025). However, class labels provide only coarse population-level grouping and do not reflect fine-grained inter-individual neuroanatomical variability. While e ff ective for image registration or quality assessment, such metrics emphasize local intensity correspondence rather than higher-level anatomical structure. Consequently, both class-based and pixel-level simi-larity metrics fail to capture subtle, subject-specific anatomical patterns that are crucial for meaningful brain MRI retrieval. We address these limitations by using same-subject images as ground truth for similarity, guiding the model to focus on subject-specific global /regional brain structure and pulling cor-responding scans closer in the embedding space. At the same time, we leverage a VAE-trained latent space that captures fine-grained anatomical patterns across subjects, enabling anatomi-cally similar scans to cluster while dissimilar ones are pushed apart. Learning compact latent representations with autoen-coders, including variational autoencoders, has been widely adopted in CBIR systems to support similarity-based retrieval beyond hand-crafted features (Rafiei and Iosifidis, 2023; Wang et al., 2004). The ablation analysis provides further insight into the role of the contrastive objectives. In agreement with pre-vious contrastive learning studies showing that negative sam-ple selection strongly influences representation quality (Chen et al., 2020; Khosla et al., 2020), our results indicate that the hard-negative and soft-negative terms contribute di ff erently. The hard-negative term primarily improves the alignment be-tween embedding similarity and anatomical similarity, increas-ing the correlation with MS-SSIM ( ρMS-SSIM : −0.01 to −0.71). The soft-negative term improves retrieval robustness by refin-ing similarity relationships, encouraging anatomically similar brains to lie closer to the query’s positive samples while main-taining separation from hard negatives (mAP@5: 79 .0% to 97 .0%). The publication most closely related to our work is that of Puglisi et al. (2024), which addresses the task of locating pre-vious scans of the same patient, commonly referred to as sub-ject re-identification. They proposed DeepBrainPrint, a frame-work that generates subject-specific fingerprints from 2D T1-weighted MR images. Their evaluation, conducted on ADNI and a synthetic benchmark comprising 142 images, defined re-trieval success as returning scans from the same subject among the top-ranked results. Reported performance was high, with mAP@3 values of 95.5% on ADNI and 91% on the synthetic dataset (Puglisi et al., 2024). While this work demonstrates the feasibility of subject-level MRI retrieval, our approach di ff ers in both scope and methodology. First, we extend retrieval to large-scale datasets comprising thousands of 3D T1-weighted MRIs, explicitly evaluating generalization across scanners and acquisition protocols. Finally, our region-based formula-tion allows retrieval to focus on anatomically localized patterns when regional e ff ects dominate over global structure, facilitat-ing task-specific stratification in subsequent analyses. Through the evaluation of NeuroCBIR on unsupervised tasks, including zero-shot age prediction and pathology strat-ification, we aim to demonstrate that the learned embedding features encode both age-related and disease-related anatomi-cal patterns. To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior studies that perform brain age estimation purely through unsu-pervised, zero-shot, retrieval-based embeddings without super-vised age labels during training. Our method achieves an MAE of ∼ 4.3 years and a Pearson correlation of r ∼ 0.5 across the evaluated brain regions. While our method is not tailored for age prediction, it achieved competitive results compared to su-pervised brain age regression models reported in the literature. For example, in (Dartora et al., 2024), we achieved MAEs be-tween 2.67 and 3 years with a regression model optimized for brain age. Similarly, on the external AIBL cohort, NeuroCBIR achieved an MAE of 4 .5 years, compared with 3 .8 years re-ported by the supervised brain age estimation model of Rajabli et al. (2025). Brain age estimation methods use standard in-terpretability methods, such as gradCAM (Dartora et al., 2024; Rajabli et al., 2025). Instead of competing with brain age re-gression methods, NeuroCBIR can be used to add interpretabil-ity. Indeed, since our framework estimates age directly from the top-k most similar brains in the database, it is possible to perform fine-grained analysis by inspecting di ff erences in age between the query and retrieved scans and possibly detecting outliers. Additionally, consistent with observations in supervised brain age models (Soumya Kumari and Sundarrajan, 2024; Dar-tora et al., 2024), we also report an AD-related age gap when predictions are based on CN reference datasets (see Figure 9). This shift reflects disease-related morphological patterns that resemble accelerated normative aging, despite the absence of explicit disease supervision during training. Regarding zero-shot pathology prediction, whole-brain em-beddings achieve a balanced accuracy of 56.3% and a MF1 score of 53.1% across CN, MCI, and AD. Most misclassifi-cations between CN and AD occur toward the MCI class, re-flecting its intermediate clinical status and supporting the bio-logical plausibility of the learned embedding space. On the external AIBL cohort, NeuroCBIR achieved balanced accura-cies of 75 .2% using whole-brain embeddings and 81 .9% using the left amygdala for the CN /AD classification task, without any retraining or domain adaptation. These results approach the 84 .0% reported by the supervised classifier of Wen et al. (2020), which is slightly better than their results on ADNI data (Pers-son et al., 2026). Prior unsupervised approaches have also ex-plored AD detection via clustering of MRI features (Inguanzo et al., 2023) or anomaly detection with autoencoder-based rep-resentations (Kim and Shin, 2026), showing that disease-related patterns can be captured without labeled training data. Impor-tantly, our framework demonstrates zero-shot multi-class clas-sification across CN, MCI, and AD using fully unsupervised embeddings, showing that meaningful disease-related structure can be captured without any training with task-specific labels. Researchers from the Division of Clinical Geriatrics at Karolinska Institutet created TheHiveDB (Muehlboeck et al., 2014), a system that allows users to retrieve images using spe-cific search criteria (e.g., diagnosis, age, sex, biomarkers, mem-ory tests, scanner, etc.). Notice that we decided not to consider any additional variable in the training of NeuroCBIR beyond image similarity to ease its integration into systems such as The HiveDB. NeuroCBIR can be integrated into TheHiveDB or other search systems to add an extra dimension of image similarity, which could be used to answer di ff erent research questions by filtering the results from NeuroCBIR with addi-tional criteria. Thus, with NeuroCBIR, users could evaluate images alongside associated clinical records. Moreover, new scans can be added directly to the database without retraining the model, enabling the system to continuously improve cov-erage and utility as additional data becomes available. Regard-ing pure re-identification, NeuroCBIR can be used for detecting images from the same subject that, due to human errors, were assigned to di ff erent subject IDs, something that is not uncom-mon in ADNI, for example. The relatively good performance of NeuroCBIR on zero-shot tasks of brain age and classifica-tion points to a correlation between image similarity, age, and 17 condition, which is interesting to further study in the future. A central motivation for adopting latent representations from VAEs rather than raw 3D brain MRIs lies in both computational effi ciency and semantic compactness. Direct processing of vol-umetric scans is prohibitively memory-intensive: for example, training the VAE with a batch size of just one whole-brain MRI required ∼ 63 GB of GPU memory in our experiments. Once trained, the encoder alone requires ∼ 20 GB to process a single image (batch size = 1). By precomputing the latent embeddings 

μ, the input size is reduced from [160, 176, 208] voxels to [8, 20, 22, 26], representing a drastic compression of the 3D vol-ume. This reduction makes it possible to perform contrastive training with significantly larger batch sizes that would other-wise be infeasible. Such e ffi ciency is crucial, since contrastive learning methods rely on large and diverse batches to achieve robust representations (Cheng et al., 2025). Despite these contributions, our study has several limitations. First, we restricted our analysis to T1w MRI. Thus, retraining would be needed for other sequences. Second, the approach re-lies on preprocessing steps, particularly skull-stripping, that are essential to prevent the embeddings from capturing face-related rather than brain-related patterns. These steps add additional time and complexity to a clinical workflow. New fast and ac-curate deep learning-based methods (e.g., Hoopes et al. (2022)) could reduce that issue. Moreover, although the system was evaluated on data preprocessed with pipelines slightly di ff erent from the training data, it remains unclear how robust the model is to substantially di ff erent preprocessing strategies. Future work should address these limitations by extending the framework to other clinically relevant brain MRI sequences, such as FLAIR scans, streamlining preprocessing, and explor-ing methods to improve the transparency and explainability of latent representations. Additionally, the inclusion of additional cohorts could expand the dataset of precomputed features and enable a more comprehensive use for both clinical and research applications. Wachinger et al. (2015) used cortical surfaces for subject re-identification using FreeSurfer. At a local scale, the shape of the hippocampus can also be potentially used for re-identification (DeKraker et al., 2022). Thus, an interesting av-enue to complement NeuroCBIR is to extract features from cor-tical surfaces. For usability, this approach will require fast and accurate methods for surface mesh extraction. However, de-spite promising results in the last few years (e.g., Zheng et al. (2025); Bongratz et al. (2024)), this remains a di ffi cult problem. This study primarily focuses on the development and techni-cal validation of the NeuroCBIR framework. While the method was evaluated for scan re-identification and explored in zero-shot clinical scenarios, a more comprehensive investigation of these and additional clinical applications is warranted. In-deed, the relatively good performance of NeuroCBIR on zero-shot tasks of brain age and classification suggests a correlation among image similarity, age, and condition, which is worth fur-ther study. Future work should assess its potential for finer-grained tasks, such as the identification and stratification of dementia subtypes (Inguanzo et al., 2023). In addition, Neu-roCBIR could serve as a perceptual and reasoning component within future AI systems: its embeddings and similarity-based retrieval may inform clinical or research decision-making, such as cohort selection or patient risk prioritization, providing a foundation for more agentic AI architectures with planning and action modules (Abou Ali et al., 2025). 

5. Conclusions 

In this study, we present NeuroCBIR, a content-based image retrieval framework for brain MRI that supports both whole-brain and region-specific retrieval. The system leverages la-tent embeddings from a variational autoencoder combined with contrastive learning, allowing e ffi cient and semantically mean-ingful representations of 3D T1w MRIs. NeuroCBIR was evaluated with >26 000 MRI scans from five large-scale and complementary datasets (ADNI, OASIS3, AIBL, MIRIAD, and SLIM), encompassing a wide range of ac-quisition protocols, scanner manufacturers, and field strengths. The results consistently demonstrated a high retrieval perfor-mance: whole-brain embeddings achieved mAP@5 ≥ 98 .4%, while region-specific retrieval provided strong performance across most brain anatomical structures. The Spearman’s corre-lation between the embedding-space and MS-SSIM ( ρMS −SSIM )further confirms that the learned embeddings preserve meaning-ful perceptual and semantic similarities between inter-subject scans. These findings confirm the robustness and generalization of the approach, even under heterogeneous imaging conditions. Additionally, the embeddings demonstrate to capture rich, subject-specific anatomical patterns that support subject re-identification in large datasets, zero-shot age prediction and multi-class pathology stratification without any supervised training. By leveraging a retrieval-based approach, our frame-work provides interpretable predictions and reveals meaning-ful disease-related structure, including accelerated aging pat-terns in AD and intermediate representations for MCI. Impor-tantly, because the embeddings are fully unsupervised and task-agnostic, they can be readily applied to a wide range of down-stream neuroscience applications, o ff ering a flexible and inter-pretable alternative to traditional supervised models while re-ducing reliance on large labeled datasets. The embedding extraction on a 4-core CPU required ∼18.7s per scan, whereas similarity search was virtually instantaneous (<0.01s). By making the code publicly available, the pretrained models and the precomputed features of >26 000 from five datasets, NeuroCBIR enables clinicians to retrieve structurally similar cases to support diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment decisions. For the research community, NeuroCBIR provides a reproducible platform for benchmarking and extending CBIR approaches in neuroimaging.
