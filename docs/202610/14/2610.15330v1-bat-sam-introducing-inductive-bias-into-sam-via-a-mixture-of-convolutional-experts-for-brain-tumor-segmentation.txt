Abstract —Accurate brain tumor segmentation from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is essential for diagnosis and treatment planning; however, manual delineation is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Existing approaches are either task-specific with limited generalization or rely on user-provided prompts during inference. We present BaT-SAM, a parameter-efficient adaptation framework that enables fully automatic brain tumor segmenta-tion with the Segment Anything Model (SAM). BaT-SAM injects local inductive bias into SAM’s frozen Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder via Conv-LoRA, where image features are processed by a sparsely gated mixture of multi-scale convolutional experts. This design compensates for the limited translation-equivariant inductive bias of vanilla ViTs while fine-tuning fewer than 5M parameters ( <1% of the full model). To remove the need for user prompts, we further introduce a lightweight self-prompting box regressor that is jointly optimized with the adapter using scheduled sampling and predicts tumor locations directly from image embeddings. On the BRISC2025 benchmark, BaT-SAM achieves a weighted mean IoU of 0.832 while outperforming state-of-the-art benchmarks. It further demonstrates strong cross-dataset generalization, achieving Dice scores of 0.702 on Figshare and 0.784 on the LGG MRI Segmentation dataset. 

Index Terms —brain tumor segmentation, segment anything model, low rank adaptation, mixture of experts 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Accurate segmentation of brain tumors from MRI images, including gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary adenomas, is critical for diagnosis, radiotherapy planning, and treatment monitoring. Despite recent advances, tumor delineation in clinical practice remains largely dependent on slice-by-slice manual annotation, a labor-intensive and time-consuming pro-cess. Automatic segmentation is particularly challenging due to substantial variability in tumor location, size, shape, and appearance across different tumor types. Moreover, tumor growth patterns differ considerably. For example, gliomas often exhibit diffuse, infiltrative boundaries that blend with surrounding edema, whereas meningiomas and pituitary ade-nomas are generally more localized but may still present

irregular morphologies. These factors contribute to significant inter-annotator variability, making manual delineation increas-ingly impractical. Therefore, developing accurate and robust automatic segmentation methods that generalize across tumor types, imaging scanners, and patient populations remains an important clinical and research objective. U-Net [25] and its variants [15], [21], [32] have been widely used for brain tumor segmentation. However, these models are typically trained from scratch using densely annotated pixel-level masks, whose acquisition is both labor-intensive and expensive. As a result, tumor segmentation datasets remain orders of magnitude smaller than general image benchmarks, causing models to overfit limited training data and generalize poorly to cross-domain settings. Furthermore, convolutional networks primarily rely on local receptive fields which lim-its their ability to capture long-range anatomical dependen-cies that are often crucial for accurately delineating tumor boundaries and subregions. A promising alternative is to adapt large-scale vision foundation models that have learned transferable segmentation priors. For example, the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [16], pre-trained on the SA-1B dataset containing over one billion segmentation masks, learns rich representations of object boundaries and fine-grained struc-tures. Therefore, rather than fully fine-tuning such foundation on limited medical data, it is more effective to efficiently adapt its pre-trained representations to the brain MRI domain. Adapting SAM to brain tumor MRI segmentation presents several challenges. First, a substantial domain gap exists between SAM’s pre-training on natural images and gray-scale brain MRI scans. Compared with natural images, T1-weighted MRI slices exhibit distinct intensity distributions, textures, and noise characteristics, while brain tumors often appear as low-contrast, diffuse lesions rather than the well-defined, high-contrast objects on which SAM was trained. As a result, zero-shot SAM generalizes poorly to brain tumor MRI, achieving average Dice scores of only 0.56–0.70 depending on the prompt type [2]. Second, fully fine-tuning SAM’s ViT encoder on limited annotated data is prone to overfitting [12], encouraging us to employ the parameter-efficient adaptation arXiv:2610.15330v1 [cs.CV] 11 Oct 2026 technique. Third, SAM is inherently designed for interactive segmentation that requires users to provide prompt in the form of points, bounding boxes, or masks during inference [30]. This requirement is incompatible with fully automatic clinical workflows which motivates us to develop an automatic prompt generation strategy for SAM [16]. To address these challenges, we propose BaT-SAM , arobust, fully automatic, and parameter-efficient framework for 

br ain tumor segmentation based on SAM . We adapt SAM by injecting Conv-LoRA modules [31] into its frozen ViT image encoder. Specifically, Conv-LoRA extends LoRA [13] by routing low-rank features through a sparsely gated mixture of multi-scale convolutional experts before projecting them back into the frozen backbone. By incorporating convolutional inductive biases into the transformer encoder, we compensate for the lack of translation equivariance in the ViT encoder and improve the representations of fine-grained tumor boundaries. Our approach achieves these benefits while updating fewer than 1% of SAM’s parameters. Within the BaT-SAM framework, we employ a single shared adapter and segmentation decoder to jointly segment gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary adenomas. To enable fully auto-matic inference, we introduce a lightweight self-prompting box regressor that is trained jointly with the segmentation network. The regressor predicts tumor bounding boxes directly from SAM’s image embeddings and passes them to the prompt en-coder, removing the need for manual prompts during inference. To further improve robustness, we adopt a scheduled sampling strategy that gradually replaces ground-truth box prompts with predicted boxes throughout training. This curriculum mitigates 

exposure bias (train–test prompt discrepancy), resulting in more reliable fully automatic segmentation during inference. We evaluate BaT-SAM on the BRISC2025 benchmark [11]. Despite updating fewer than 5M parameters, BaT-SAM achieves 0.832 mIoU and 0.887 Dice, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art Swin-HAFUNet [11], which reports a 0.824 weighted mIoU. We further evaluate BaT-SAM on two independent external datasets to assess its generalization ability, achieving Dice scores of 0.702 on the Figshare Brain Tumor dataset [8] and 0.784 on the LGG MRI Segmentation dataset [5]. In summary, we make the following contributions:  

> •

We propose a parameter-efficient adaptation framework, BaT-SAM, that injects local, translation-equivariant in-ductive biases into SAM’s frozen image encoder through a sparsely gated mixture of multi-scale convolutional experts, while updating fewer than 5M parameters.  

> •

We introduce a self-prompting box regressor that is trained end-to-end with a scheduled sampling strategy, removing the need for manually provided prompts and enabling a single shared adapter and decoder to segment all brain tumor types.  

> •

We demonstrate that BaT-SAM achieves state-of-the-art performance on the BRISC2025 benchmark and general-izes effectively to two independent brain MRI datasets in a zero-shot setting. II. R ELATED WORK 

Brain tumor segmentation. Convolutional encoder–decoder networks have long been the dominant paradigm for brain tumor segmentation. U-Net [25] established the standard encoder–decoder architecture with skip connections, while subsequent variants improved feature fusion through dense skip connections (U-Net++ [32]) and attention mechanisms (Attention U-Net [21]). nnU-Net [15] further demonstrated that careful pipeline optimization often outperforms archi-tectural modifications across biomedical segmentation bench-marks, including BraTS [4]. However, these methods rely on task-specific supervision, requiring extensive pixel-level an-notations while demonstrate limited generalization to domain shifts caused by variations in scanners and imaging protocols. 

Adapting SAM for medical imaging. Although the Seg-ment Anything Model (SAM) [16] learns strong promptable segmentation priors from natural images, its performance degrades substantially on medical images because of the large domain gap with MRI or CT imaging domain. MedSAM [20] bridges this gap by fine-tuning SAM on a large-scale, multi-modal medical image dataset with corresponding segmentation masks, achieving significant improvements over zero-shot set-tings. In parallel, SAM-Med2D [9] adapts SAM to 2D medical images by fine-tuning the prompt encoder and mask decoder while introducing lightweight adapters into the image encoder, showing strong generalization across a broad range of medical imaging tasks. 

SAM for brain tumor segmentation. Ali et al. [2] evaluated SAM in a zero-shot setting on two brain tumor MRI datasets and reported lower Dice scores of 0.56–0.70, depending on the dataset. They further showed that bounding box prompts consistently outperform point prompts for irregular tumor segmentation. Building on this observation, Ryu et al. [26] employed a vision–language model to generate box prompts before fine-tuning the mask decoder. However, the predicted boxes remain noisy, achieving a mean box IoU of only 0.23– 0.36, which limits segmentation performance. More recently, EffSAM [1] removed the prompt encoder entirely and adapted SAM for volumetric brain MRI using low-rank adaptation, and cross-slice attention. In contrast, we preserve SAM’s prompting paradigm by learning a lightweight self-prompting box regressor that enables fully automatic inference while efficiently adapting the frozen image encoder. 

Parameter-efficient adaptation. Low rank adaptation (LoRA) [13] adapts pre-trained models by learning low-rank updates to selected weight matrices, substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters compared with full fine-tuning. Conv-LoRA [31] extends LoRA by routing low-rank features through a mixture of multi-scale convolutional experts, injecting inductive biases into SAM. This strategy has recently shown promising results for adapting SAM to industrial X-ray CT defect segmentation [12], [28]. In this work, we build upon Conv-LoRA and extend it to brain tumor MRI segmentation. III. M ETHOD 

A. Overview 

Figure 1 provides an overview of the proposed BaT-SAM framework. BaT-SAM builds upon the ViT-H variant of SAM, which consists of an image encoder, a prompt encoder, and a mask decoder. To efficiently adapt SAM to brain MRI segmentation, we inject Conv-LoRA modules into the frozen ViT image encoder, introducing local translation-equivariant inductive biases. To enable fully automatic inference, we further introduce a lightweight box regressor that predicts tumor bounding boxes directly from the image embeddings and feeds them to the prompt encoder. We jointly train the box regressor and segmentation network using a scheduled sampling strategy. 

B. Conv-LoRA for Inductive Bias 

As SAM’s image encoder is a standard vision transformer, it lacks the local inductive biases inherent to convolutional networks. To address this limitation, we inject these inductive biases using Conv-LoRA. Specifically, we apply Conv-LoRA only to the combined query, key, and value (QKV) projection in each of the 32 transformer blocks of the image encoder. Given a frozen projection matrix W0, the trainable adaptation is defined as 

h = W0x + αr BAx, (1) where A ∈ Rr×d and B ∈ Rd×r are learnable low-rank matrices, r denotes the rank, and α is a scaling factor. Fol-lowing [13], we initialize B to zero so that the adapted model is identical to the frozen SAM. Conv-LoRA extends LoRA by transforming the low-rank bottleneck representation, z = Ax ,with a sparsely gated mixture of M multi-scale convolutional experts. Specifically, we reshape z into a spatial feature map and route it through experts with different effective receptive fields, obtained by lightweight upsampling and downsampling operations. A gating network predicts expert weights from globally pooled bottleneck features and dynamically selects the most suitable expert for each input. We use rank r = 2 

and M = 8 experts. We add an auxiliary load balancing loss on the gating network, to discourage the gate from collapsing onto a small subset of experts. 

C. Self-Prompting Box Regressor 

SAM requires user-provided prompts during inference. We remove this dependency by introducing a lightweight self-prompting box regressor that predicts tumor bounding boxes directly from the 256-channel image embedding using two convolutional blocks, global average pooling, and a two-layer MLP. E. Scheduled Sampling Curriculum

Datasets. We train and evaluate BaT-SAM on the BRISC2025 benchmark [11], which contains 3,933 training and 860 test T1-weighted brain MRI slices ( 512 × 512 ) with binary tumor masks. The dataset covers three tumor types (glioma, menin-gioma, and pituitary) across the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, with one annotated tumor per slice and a balanced class distribution. As shown in Table I, tumors occupy only 1.0–2.3% of each slice on average, placing BRICS2025 MRI scans in the small-object segmentation regime. To evaluate cross-dataset generalization, we additionally test BaT-SAM on Figshare [8] and the LGG MRI Segmentation dataset [5]. The LGG dataset contains FLAIR MRI slices from 110 patients with lower-grade glioma and corresponding binary tumor masks. Since the annotations are defined on the FLAIR sequence, we use only the FLAIR images and replicate the single channel three times to match the BRISC2025 input format. We restrict evaluation to the 1,373 tumor-containing slices, as BaT-SAM assumes a single tumor per image. 

Implementation Details. We resize all images to 1024 ×1024 ,the native input resolution of SAM ViT-H. We also normalize them using ImageNet statistics and apply random horizontal flipping during training. We freeze the SAM image encoder and prompt encoder, training only the Conv-LoRA parameters, mask decoder, and box regressor, resulting in fewer than 5M trainable parameters ( < 1% of SAM ViT-H’s 642M parameters). We optimize the model with AdamW, using a base learning rate of 3 × 10 −4 for the Conv-LoRA and mask decoder parameters and 3 × 10 −3 for the box regressor. We adopt a cosine learning rate schedule with linear warmup over the first 10% of training and clip gradients to a maximum norm of 1. Training is performed with bfloat16 mixed precision and gradient accumulation, as the global attention blocks in SAM ViT-H are memory-intensive at full resolution. We fine-tune the model using distributed data parallelism on two RTX 6000 GPUs. 

Evaluation Metrics. In this work, we report two metrics for mask quality. Intersection over union (IoU) measures the overlap between the predicted mask and the ground truth mask. B. Visualization 

Figure 2 presents qualitative results of BaT-SAM across all three tumor types and imaging planes in BRISC2025. The predicted masks closely align with the ground truth across diverse tumor morphologies, including irregular and elongated gliomas, where IoU remains above 0.8. Meningiomas, which are typically larger and more peripheral, achieve the highest overlap (IoU > 0.95 , Dice > 0.97 ) in the axial and coronal views. Despite their small size, pituitary tumors are also segmented accurately across all three planes. These qualitative results validate our proposed framework. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented BaT-SAM, a parameter-efficient adaptation framework for brain tumor MRI segmentation based on the SAM. BaT-SAM augments the frozen ViT encoder with Conv-LoRA, which injects local inductive bias through a sparsely gated mixture of multi-scale convolutional experts while up-dating fewer than 5M parameters ( < 1% of the full model). A self-prompting box regressor, jointly optimized with the segmentation model via scheduled sampling, eliminates the need for user-provided prompts at inference. A single shared adapter and decoder generalize across all three tumor types without class-specific models. On the BRISC2025 benchmark, BaT-SAM achieves a weighted mIoU of 0.832, outperforming state-of-the-art methods. It further achieves higher Dice scores on Figshare and LGG MRI Segmentation dataset, demonstrat-ing strong zero-shot cross-dataset generalization. In the future, we will investigate more robust localization strategies and broader training data diversity to further improve cross-domain generalization, especially for small and multifocal tumors.
