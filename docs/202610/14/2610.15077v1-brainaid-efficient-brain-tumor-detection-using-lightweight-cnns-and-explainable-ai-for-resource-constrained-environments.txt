Abstract—A brain tumor is an unusual growth of cells in
the brain that can be cancerous or not, affecting normal brain
function and causing serious risk to health. Existing solutions for
brain tumor classification, such as expert manual evaluations of
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, are generally incon-
sistent as different professionals have different criteria and can
be resource-intensive, leading to delayed diagnoses. Additionally,
while deep learning models have demonstrated potential, they
typically require significant computational resources, motivating
the need for more efficient and accessible diagnostic tools that
can be deployed in real-time healthcare settings. To address
these limitations, this study presents BrainAid, which makes
efficient use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and
Vision Transformers (ViTs) for effective classification of four-
class classification of glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and
no-tumor MRI scans, outperforming all the standard models such
as VGG16, Inception-V3, ResNet-50 and so, while integrating
Explainable AI (XAI) to improve interpretability. The BrainAid
is trained to classify tumors with a high precision of 98%,
achieving an F1 score of 98% and an accuracy of 98% using
publicly available dataset. The model is deployed on a Raspberry
Pi, demonstrating efficiency in low-resource environments. This
system shows the feasibility of real-time, edge-based tumor
classification, providing quick diagnostic support for medical
professionals and enhancing patient care in resource-limited
healthcare facilities and underserved areas.
Index Terms—Brain Tumor Classification, Convolutional Neu-
ral Network, Vision Transformers, Explainable AI, Internet of
Medical Things (IoMT).

A. Motivation:
In spite of the good accuracy attained by deep learn-
ing (DL) models in identification tasks, they demand more
computational resources, making the system computationally
expensive. Thus, leading to challenges for implementation in
resource-constrained locations like rural hospitals or mobile
diagnostic units, where state-of-the-art facilities are absent,
making it unsuitable for real-time applications. Therefore,
there is a need for developing light-weight models keeping in
mind the balance between model complexity and performance.
Also, these models often act as black boxes, making it difficult
for the user to trust the decision made by the model. As seen in
Figure 1, there is inconsistency in explainability as the model
concentrates on non-tumor areas more than tumor regions,
resulting in inaccurate grading. Therefore, this emphasizes the
need for transparency and accountability in AI systems.
Hence, this research focuses on creating a lightweight CNN-
based model customized for brain tumor classification that lays
a good balance between model complexity and performance.
Unlike traditional deep learning techniques, which rely on
computationally demanding pre-trained models, the proposed
framework is designed for situations that require limited
hardware resources. This development solves the limitations of
current high-performance DL models by providing a reliable
and scalable tool that can be utilized by healthcare profession-
als and non-specialists alike to accurately detect brain tumors
from MRI images, thereby increasing diagnostic reach and
consistency in real-world clinical settings.
B. Research Contributions:
The primary contributions of this study are stated below:
• BrainAid, an end-to-end IoMT framework that takes a
raw MRI scan to a four-class tumor label entirely on the
acquisition device, eliminating the need to transmit MRI
images to a remote inference server.
• A purpose-built CNN, not a pruned ImageNet back-
bone. Filter allocation sized directly for MRI collapses
the feature-extractor and classifier stages of existing
IoMT pipelines into one 3 million-parameter network.
• Explainability analysis beyond classification accuracy.
Cross-model Grad-CAM comparison exposes baselines
that reach correct labels while attending to non-tumor
tissue, a fail ure invisible to accuracy alone.
• Testbed validation on resource-constrained edge hard-
ware. A 13 MB TensorFlow Lite build runs on a Rasp-
berry Pi 4 at 0.05 s per scan while attaining 98% accuracy
and 98% F1 across the four classes.

by a combination of neural networks and support vector ma-

k-NN classifier. However, extracting relevant features from
MRI scans is a difficult task due to its high dimensionality
and variability. Minz et al. [32] proposed a binary classifier
using GLCM texture feature extractor and Adaboost, achieving
an accuracy of 89.9%. However, the system has been tested
with a very limited number of images. Ramasamy et al. [33]
used a histogram of oriented gradient (HOG) approach to

B. Transfer Learning based Diagnosis
Transfer Learning (TL) is a process in which a model
trained on one dataset is fine-tuned for a different task using
a target dataset. Hossain et al. [19] proposes a ensemble of
Inception-V3, Xception, VGG16 where the output of these
models is fed to a dense layer of a fully connected neural
network to give a prediction among 4 classes of the brain
tumors. Despite having an F1 score of 75%, the complexity
of this approach adds a lot of overhead to the processing unit
due to the size of the learning framework. A binary classifi-
cation method was proposed by Neamah [23] where feature
extraction of MRI scans is done through a modified ResNet-50
pre-trained model where the final layer is replaced by a spatial
pyramid pooling layer to classify the tumors. Nevertheless, the
proposed work does not provide any information on the type
of brain tumor the patient is suffering from, which is extremely
important for diagnosing the patient. Similarly, Preetha et al.
[24] proposed a binary classification system that makes use of
the EfficientNet-B4 model and Bayesian optimization to find
optimal hyperparameters achieving an accuracy of 99.33%.
Despite attaining high accuracy on one dataset, the work has
not been tested with other publicly available datasets to prove
its generalizability. A pre trained GoogLeNet method was used
by the authors in [27] to extract features of brain MRI images
which was later fed to softmax, SVM, k-NN to classify, from
which SVM classifier attained the highest F1 score of 97.35%.
This bimodel setup can complicate the workflow, increasing
computational overhead. Asif et al. [35] use DenseNet121,
NasNet Large, Xception, and InceptionResNetV2 for feature
extraction, in addition to a few dense layers and a softmax
layer, to identify tumors as benign or malignant with a 99.67%
accuracy. The key limitation of this work is the small dataset
of 1,900 images, which may restrict the model’s applicability
to larger clinical applications.
C. Convolutional Neural Network-based Diagnosis
A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a deep learning
architecture that is especially built to handle structured grid
data like videos and images. The approach made by Behera
[21] involves combination of simple linear iterative clustering
(SLIC) based superpixel algorithm and CNN for binary clas-
sification. However, for super pixelation, choosing the best
parameters is a challenging task as it is image-dependent.
Zhou et al. [22] proposed a multi-model graph convolution
network to capture spatial and contextual information for
tumor segmentation across multiple MRI sequences. However,
this method struggles to accurately identify small and scattered
tumor regions, which can lead to incomplete segmentation
results. Ranjbarzade et al. [36] proposed a classification ap-
proach that involves segmenting images into smaller pieces
for model training, lowering computation time with the use
of a Distance Wise Attention (DWA) algorithm. However, the
approach has limits when dealing with tumor volumes that
exceed one-third of the brain. In [37], an encoder-decoder
architecture with three layers was used to capture a variety
of characteristics, integrating Dice and focused loss functions.
An RF regression model trained on form, volume, and age
data obtained 56.7% training accuracy and 51.7% validation
accuracy. Kobayashi et al. [38] proposed a discriminatory
network for detecting regions of tumor by estimating the L2

III. BRAINAID: THE PROPOSED SENSING SYSTEM
We propose BrainAid, a framework that allows doctors and
radiologists to easily detect the type of brain tumor. The
system classifies brain tumors into four categories: gliomas,
meningiomas, pituitary, and no tumor. This categorization of
malignant tumors enables doctors to foresee future implica-
tions and provide appropriate treatment. Predictions are made
locally on the device, eliminating the need to send data to
a central server. Figure 2 shows a functional illustration of
the process. The detailed steps followed are discussed in this
section.
A. Dataset for Training
The dataset for this research [39] comprises of 7,023 T1-
weighted contrast-enhanced human brain MRI scans classified
into four categories: glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and
no tumor. This dataset contains three particular kinds of brain
tumors, facilitating perform classification tasks in automated
brain tumor detection algorithms. It has been split into two
groups: a training set of 5,712 images and a testing set of 1,311
images. The training set enabled the deep learning model to
acquire convoluted features for accurate tumor classification,
whilst the testing set delivered an unbiased assessment of
the model’s effectiveness. The study used a large dataset to
create and test a powerful brain tumor classification model
with practical applications.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
A. Experimental Setup
The model was trained on a Lenovo LOQ CPU with an
Intel Core i7 HX, 16 GB RAM, a 500 GB SSD, and GPU
acceleration offered by Kaggle’s NVIDIA T4 GPUs in a dual
configuration (T4 x2), aggregating 29GB. Python frameworks
include Keras, TensorFlow, and TensorFlow Lite. The chipsets
utilized to test the BrainAid prototype are the 64-bit quad-core
Cortex-A72 processors that exist in the Raspberry Pi 4.
B. Performance and Error Analysis of BrainAid
The proposed deep learning framework BrainAid underwent
through training and evaluation on a diverse set of sample
images, representing all target classes. The final results of the
CNN BrainAid model and ViT BrainAid model are illustrated
in Table III and Table IV, respectively. When benchmarked
against existing architectures, the BrainAid model exhibited
superior performance, with the next best model, ResNet-50,
achieving an accuracy of 94%. Consequently, while multiple
models were assessed for comparative purposes, BrainAid was
selected for final deployment due to its high performance and
lightweight in nature. Figure 5 shows the class-wise misclas-
sification across all four labels. The training process involved
experimenting with various combinations of hyperparameters.
Key performance indicators such as training and validation
accuracy, training and validation loss, and test accuracy-were
thoroughly analyzed to determine the optimal configurations.

VI. DISCUSSION
A. Clinical Relevance
Brain tumor classification is a critical aspect of neuro on-
cology, as early and accurate diagnosis is important for timely
clinical management and treatment planning [2]. Traditional
methods rely on radiologists to interpret MRI scans, which
is not only time consuming but also prone to diagnosis vari-
ability according to the practitioner’s experience. In addition,


10
the global shortage of radiologists is a significant concern,
particularly in underserved regions. For example, in some
parts of sub-Saharan Africa, there is only one radiologist per
million people, highlighting the critical need for accessible
diagnostic services in these areas [44]. Also, in Singapore,
there are 7.6 radiologists per 100,000 population, which is
lower than Europe’s 13 per 100,000 [45]. These statistics un-
derscore the pressing need for innovative solutions to address
the radiologist shortage and ensure timely medical imaging
services worldwide.
B. Innovation
The proposed model achieves an impressive F1 score and
accuracy of 98%, achieving competitive classification perfor-
mance compared with representative deep learning models. By
deploying BrainAid on a low-cost Raspberry Pi, we enable
real-time, on-site brain tumor detection in environments with
scarce high-end computational resources, such as rural clinics
and mobile diagnostic units. This demonstrates the potential
for deploying computer-assisted MRI analysis in settings with
limited computational resources, addressing a critical chal-
lenge in underserved areas, namely, the shortage of trained
radiologists, which often leads to delays in diagnosis and
treatment. Beyond improving accessibility, the model reduces
reliance on specialized medical professionals, making it a
valuable asset in regions with limited healthcare infrastruc-
ture. Additionally, the incorporation of Grad-CAM enhances
BrainAid’s transparency by offering visual explanations for
its classifications, fostering trust among medical professionals,
and assisting them in validating AI-driven decisions.
C. Limitations
Despite its strong performance, BrainAid has certain limi-
tations. Although ViT have been integrated in low-data sys-
tem, their performance is suboptimal compared to the CNN
model. Most likely due to the relatively small dataset. Larger
datasets are needed to fully exploit ViT capabilities. Although
the model has been rigorously tested in controlled settings,
real world deployment in hospitals and integration with ra-
diology workflows require further validation and regulatory
approval. Addressing these limitations will further strengthen
the model’s utility and increase its adoption in clinical practice.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this study, we explored various SOTA models for brain
tumor classification and subsequently proposed a lightweight
CNN and ViT architecture, named BrainAid. The proposed
model was benchmarked against established algorithms to
evaluate its performance. The architecture of BrainAid was
designed with lightweight convolutional layers, focusing on
achieving an optimal balance between computational effi-
ciency and classification performance. The lightweight nature
of the model, combined with strategies such as early stopping,
contributed to reducing the risk of overfitting while ensuring
robustness against complex image patterns. The results demon-
strate that BrainAid outperforms or achieves comparable per-
formance to the aforementioned SOTA algorithms in tumor
classification tasks with an accuracy and F1 score of 98%
each. Additionally, we conducted a comparative analysis of the
outputs from ViT-based models against those from traditional
CNN-based models to assess their relative strengths. To further
validate the effectiveness of BrainAid, we employed Grad-
CAM, an explainable AI technique, to visualize and analyze
the tumor detection capabilities of the model. The Grad-CAM
visualizations indicated that BrainAid was more accurate
and reliable in identifying tumor regions in medical images
compared to the other models. These findings underscore
the robustness of the proposed architecture and its potential
to contribute to more precise and interpretable brain tumor
classification.
