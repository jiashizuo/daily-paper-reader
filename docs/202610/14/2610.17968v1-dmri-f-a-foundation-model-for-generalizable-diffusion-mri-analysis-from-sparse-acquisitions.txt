dMRI-F: A Foundation Model for Generalizable Diffusion MRI
Analysis from Sparse Acquisitions

1University of Electronic Science and Technology of China.
*Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): fan.zhang@uestc.edu.cn;
Abstract
Diffusion MRI (dMRI) probes water diffusion within the brain, providing unique information about tissue
microstructure and white matter connectivity. High-quality dMRI enables advanced and accurate analy-
ses but demands dense acquisitions, resulting in long scan times and hardware constraints that are often
infeasible. Further, dMRI requires dedicated computational methods to support diverse analysis tasks (e.g.,
parcellation, registration, and synthesis) for both neuroscientific and clinical applications. Here, we present
dMRI-F, a foundation model that unifies dMRI data enhancement and downstream tasks across diverse
acquisitions and populations, while requiring only minimal acquisition input. dMRI-F is pre-trained with a
q-space masked reconstruction task: predicting densely sampled multi-shell data from only six volumes. It
learns generalizable latent feature representations of dMRI data, which serve as a core foundation for adapt-
ing to diverse downstream tasks, including brain parcellation, brain registration, and cross-modal synthesis.
Across nine independently acquired datasets, we demonstrate that dMRI-F outperforms state-of-the-art
methods with robust generalization across acquisitions, populations, and tasks. Our results demonstrate
dMRI-F’s versatility as a general-purpose framework that enables diverse dMRI applications from low-cost,
sparse acquisitions.
Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) is an advanced, non-invasive imaging technique that can uniquely
measure the random motion of water molecules within the brain, enabling in vivo characterization of tissue
microstructure1 and structural connectivity2. In dMRI, the acquired data, known as diffusion-weighted images
(DWIs), are collected by sampling q-space, where each sampled location corresponds to a DWI volume acquired
with a specific gradient direction (i.e., b-vector) and strength (i.e., b-value). From the DWIs, biophysical models
can be fit to derive microstructural measures (e.g., fractional anisotropy, FA)3, and fiber tractography can be
performed to map the brain’s structural connectivity4. Leveraging these properties, dMRI has found broad
utility in both neuroscientific research and clinical practice, with applications spanning brain development5,
aging6, neuroplasticity7, and cognitive function8, as well as lesion characterization9, disease monitoring10, and
neurosurgical planning11.
A key demand in dMRI is acquiring high-quality DWI data, combining dense q-space sampling, high spa-
tial resolution, and robust signal fidelity, to improve the accuracy of modeling subtle microstructural features
and complex fiber configurations. However, acquiring such high-quality data entails prolonged scan times and
substantial hardware demands. The Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) project12 exemplifies
this trade-off: its dMRI protocol achieves high 1.25 mm isotropic resolution, dense 270 diffusion directions across
three different shells, and multiple phase-encoding acquisitions for artifact correction, but requires customized
scanners and nearly one hour of scanning time per subject. While the HCP-YA data have proven immensely
valuable to the neuroimaging and neuroscientific community13–15, this level of quality remains impractical for
most routine studies, which typically acquire single-shell acquisitions with ˜2 mm isotropic resolution and far
fewer gradient directions—often as few as 6, the minimum required for conventional diffusion tensor modeling16.
Consequently, practical dMRI remains quality-limited, necessitating efficient DWI enhancement methods that
operate under routine acquisition constraints.
Deep learning has emerged as a promising approach for enhancing dMRI quality by inferring high-quality
DWI data from sparse acquisitions. Existing methods generally fall into three major categories based on their
primary objectives. The first focuses on angular super-resolution, where densely sampled diffusion signals encom-
passing a large number of gradient directions across multiple b-values are inferred from sparsely acquired q-space

data17–19. The second category emphasizes spatial super-resolution, aiming at enhancing the spatial resolution
of DWI data by generating fine-grained structural details from low-resolution scans20–22. The third category
addresses denoising, with the goal of reducing imaging noise to improve the fidelity of diffusion signals23,24.
Overall, these methods aim to capture the complex relationships inherent to the high-dimensional dMRI sig-
nal space—spanning both spatial (3D) and diffusion-encoding (q-space) dimensions—to enable prediction of
enhanced signal data. However, despite their success, most existing methods process the data as independent
DWI slices, limiting their ability to exploit its full high-dimensional spatial continuity. Furthermore, these meth-
ods are typically tailored to specific enhancement objectives and acquisition protocols, leaving a strong need for
a unified framework for broad applicability.
Beyond data-quality enhancement, dMRI also demands accurate and efficient methods for various com-
putational tasks. Unlike traditional anatomical MRI (e.g., T1w), dMRI uniquely characterizes water diffusion
using multi-directional, multi-shell acquisitions. The resulting large number of image volumes (DWIs) creates
substantial computational challenges and requires dedicated analytical methods. For example, in brain par-
cellation, recent studies have sought to perform the task directly on DWIs by extracting tissue-informative
microstructural contrasts, thereby bypassing the inter-modality registration required by conventional T1w-based
approaches25–27. Similarly, in brain registration, an emerging trend is to incorporate multiple complementary
dMRI-derived maps that provide distinct tissue and fiber orientation information to guide image alignment28–30.
Furthermore, cross-modal synthesis methods generate T1w images directly from DWIs when native anatomical
images are unavailable or cannot be reliably aligned with diffusion data31,32. Across these distinct tasks, the
common goal is to extract anatomically informative features directly from dMRI. Yet, the pursuit of feature
representations that are broadly applicable across various tasks remains underexplored.
Another demand in dMRI is generalizable methods across diverse acquisitions and populations. dMRI is
known to be highly sensitive to acquisition protocols, scanner types, and parameter settings, which often under-
mines a method’s generalizability across studies33,34. In related fields in neuroimaging, one common strategy
to improve generalizability is to train on large-scale pooled datasets that increase data variability35,36—though
this typically requires substantial data curation efforts and high computational costs. In dMRI, an emerging
alternative is to leverage high-quality reference datasets (e.g., HCP-YA) that capture a broad distribution of
diffusion properties, with the aim of generalizing to clinically typical acquisitions and populations. For example,
the strategy of training on HCP-YA data and applying to other datasets has proven successful across various
tasks, including brain segmentation37 and tract parcellation38–40, with demonstrated generalizability across
datasets spanning diverse genders, ages, and health conditions. Central to this strategy is the extraction of
informative contrasts that provide compact yet discriminative representations—essential for achieving robust
generalizability.
Here, we introduce dMRI-F, a foundation model for dMRI that jointly performs DWI enhancement and learns
generalizable latent feature representations, enabling diverse downstream computational tasks across varying
acquisition protocols and populations. dMRI-F employs a self-supervised learning (SSL) framework, pre-trained
through a q-space masked-reconstruction task that predicts densely sampled multi-shell DWIs from sparse single-
shell inputs (Fig. 1a). Architecturally, the model comprises an anatomy-aware encoder that learns latent features
reflecting the underlying anatomical structures from input volumes, and a coordinate-conditioned decoder that
predicts unseen volumes by jointly modeling these features with DWI spatial and q-space coordinates (Fig. 1b).
By generating enhanced, densely sampled data, dMRI-F enables advanced analysis of tissue microstructure and
white-matter connectivity from sparse inputs of only six volumes (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, by fine-tuning the
learned latent features, dMRI-F enables generalizable adaptation to diverse downstream tasks, including brain
parcellation, registration, and cross-modal synthesis (Fig. 1d). Using high-quality data from only 200 HCP-
YA subjects for training, dMRI-F achieves robust generalization for DWI enhancement and downstream tasks
across various acquisitions and populations (1,882 unseen subjects from the high-quality HCP-YA test set and
eight independent test datasets with clinically typical acquisitions) (Fig. 1e). Together, our results demonstrate
dMRI-F as a versatile, general-purpose computational framework for dMRI, offering a solid foundation for
diverse neuroscientific and clinical applications with minimal acquisition requirements.

Robust Generalization for DWI Enhancement Across Independently Acquired Test
Datasets
To assess whether dMRI-F generalizes beyond the HCP-YA training data under clinically realistic conditions, we
evaluated the trained DWI enhancement model on eight test datasets comprising 1,682 subjects (see Methods
and Supplementary Table 3 for demographic and acquisition details). These cohorts spanned a wide age range
from 10 to 95 years, included both healthy and multiple disease conditions, and were acquired across multiple
scanner manufacturers and protocols covering diffusion shells at 1,000, 1,500, or 2,000 s/mm2. In contrast to the
high-quality HCP-YA training set, these test datasets had lower spatial resolution, higher noise, and substantial
cross-site image-quality variation, providing a stringent test of dMRI-F’s generalizability across diverse data
conditions. We compared dMRI-F against PCConv—the top-performing method in the preceding benchmark
evaluation—under identical conditions for predicting the densely sampled DWI data from sparse inputs of six
volumes.
Across the eight test datasets, dMRI-F significantly outperformed PCConv across all quantitative metrics,
at both the DWI signal and derived microstructural measure levels (Fig. 3a). Notably, the improvement was
particularly evident on the ABIDE dataset, which was acquired at b = 1,500 s/mm2, a shell not observed
during model training on HCP-YA. Qualitatively, despite substantial variation in input quality, the DWIs and
FA images derived from dMRI-F were visually highly consistent with the reference data. The zoomed images
reveal that dMRI-F better recovered tissue boundaries and local anatomical patterns, whereas PCConv often
produced smoother predictions with less distinct anatomical contrast (Fig. 3b). Collectively, these results showed
that dMRI-F generalized robustly across heterogeneous imaging conditions and populations, with the ability to
extrapolate to diffusion shells beyond those encountered during training.
High Fidelity of Predicted Data for Preserving White Matter Connectivity and
Population Tissue Microstructure
We performed two post hoc analyses—fiber tractography and population-level microstructure comparison—to
assess whether the enhanced DWI data preserve information essential for downstream applications.
For the tractography analysis, we processed the nine evaluation datasets (HCP-YA and eight test datasets)
through a standard pipeline: fiber orientation distribution (FOD) estimation and whole-brain tractography
(using MRtrix43), followed by automated atlas-based tracts identification (using WhiteMatterAnalysis with the
ORG atlas15,44). This was applied to the predicted DWI data from our dMRI-F method and PCConv, as well as
the reference full DWI data. Results showed that dMRI-F achieved significantly more accurate FOD estimation
across all datasets (measured using the angular correlation coefficient), producing FOD maps visually similar
to the reference (Fig. 4a). Visual comparisons of the derived fiber tracts (the corticospinal tract in Fig. 4a
and additional tracts in Supplementary Fig. 2) further demonstrated that dMRI-F tractography more closely
resembled the reference, whereas PCConv frequently yielded sparser trajectories and, in some cases, failed to
reconstruct the fiber pathways.
For the microstructure analysis, building on the experiments demonstrating that the derived microstructural
estimates were highly consistent with those obtained from the full DWI (see Fig. 3 in the above experiment),
we further conducted two population-level comparisons: a sex comparison (female vs. male) and a disease
comparison (Alzheimer’s disease, AD vs. normal controls, NC). For the sex comparison, we included 20 age-
matched female–male pairs from the HCP-YA testing cohort, computed the mean fractional anisotropy (FA)
across the whole white-matter skeleton—an interpretable marker of sex-related white-matter organization45—
and compared it between groups using a two-sided t-test. For the disease comparison, we included 20 age-matched
AD–NC pairs from the ADNI46 dataset and compared mean skeleton MD (a highly sensitive measure for
detecting AD-related white-matter abnormalities47) between the two groups. We observed significantly higher
FA in females in the sex comparison and significantly higher MD in the AD group in the disease comparison,
with the detected group differences being highly consistent between the predicted and full-reference data (Fig.
4b).
Overall, these results suggest that dMRI-F-enhanced DWIs largely preserve fidelity for FOD estimation and
enable anatomically reliable tractography across varied acquisition conditions. Moreover, the enhanced data
appear to retain known population-level microstructure differences, consistent with those observed in the full
reference data.

dMRI-F Learned Feature Representations Enable Adaptation to Diverse
Downstream Tasks
The dMRI-F encoder enabled extraction of generalizable latent features across subjects, highlighting corre-
sponding anatomical patterns and tissue boundaries. As illustrated in Fig. 5, corresponding tissue structures
showed visually consistent contrasts in the principal components of the features across the example subjects,
e.g., ventricular regions appearing dark in component 1, deep white matter appearing dark in component 2, and
the white/gray matter interface appearing bright in component 3. This consistency suggested that the features
learned during pretraining captured stable structural information, providing a transferable basis for downstream
computational dMRI tasks.
In subsequent experiments, we evaluated downstream task adaptation by fine-tuning task-specific decoders
using the learned latent features for three distinct applications: (1) brain parcellation, which subdivides DWIs
into anatomically segregated regions; (2) brain registration, which aligns DWI volumes across different subjects;
and (3) cross-modal synthesis, which generates anatomical T1w images from DWIs. For each downstream task,
from an input with six DWI volumes, the pre-trained dMRI-F encoder was employed to extract latent features,
and a task-specific decoder was trained on a subset of 50 HCP-YA training subjects (see Methods). In the
following, we refer to these methods as dMRI-F-Parc, dMRI-F-Reg, and dMRI-F-Syn, respectively. Evaluation
of each method was performed on both the high-quality HCP-YA data and the eight clinically typical test
datasets, comparing their performance against representative SOTA methods in each task.
Brain Parcellation into Anatomically Segregated Regions
Brain parcellation is important in dMRI for region-specific quantification and anatomically accurate tractog-
raphy. Parcellation directly from DWI bypasses the need for additional anatomical scans, avoids error-prone
inter-modality registration, and accelerates processing time26,27. Our proposed dMRI-F-Parc leverages learned
feature representations to perform fine-scale parcellation of 101 anatomical regions corresponding to the Desikan-
Killiany (DK) parcellation48 (see Methods: Brain Parcellation). We compared with two SOTA methods: (1)
FastSurfer49, which performs parcellation from T1w images and registers the parcellation to DWI; and (2)
DDParcel27, which uses dMRI-derived microstructure measures as inputs for parcellation (See Methods and
Supplementary Table 4 for details).
As shown in Fig. 6, dMRI-F-Parc demonstrated consistent advantages across both the HCP-YA and clini-
cally typical test datasets. In the HCP-YA data, it achieved the highest Dice scores relative to the ground-truth
parcellation across cortical, subcortical, and white matter regions, significantly outperforming the compared
methods (Fig. 6a). This improvement was most pronounced in cortical regions—notoriously difficult due to
8


their complex cortical folding—where dMRI-F-Parc significantly outperformed the second-ranking DDParcel.
Visual comparisons showed that dMRI-F-Parc parcellations more closely matched the ground-truth anatomical
boundaries, particularly in the cortical regions (Fig. 6b). In the eight clinically acquired test datasets, dMRI-
F-Parc showed robust generalization, achieving significantly lower relative standard deviation (RSD) of FA (a
measure of regional parcellation homogeneity27) than DDParcel in each dataset (Fig. 6c). Visual inspection
supported these quantitative findings: dMRI-F-Parc produced regional tissue boundaries that were less dis-
placed and better aligned with visible anatomical structures across heterogeneous datasets (Fig. 6d). Together,
these results suggested that dMRI-F preserved parcellation-relevant anatomical information and enabled robust
regional delineation directly from DWI data across various datasets.

Brain Registration Between Subjects
Brain registration across subjects is a critical step in applications such as population-based analyses in health
and disease and the construction of brain atlases. In DWI registration, the goal is to spatially align brain
anatomical structures while preserving the consistency of local fiber orientations with the underlying white
matter tract anatomy. Our proposed dMRI-F-Reg addresses this by training a registration model that takes
learned features from subject pairs (moving and fixed) and predicts deformable fields to warp the moving
DWI data into the fixed space (see Methods: Brain Registration). We compared with two SOTA methods: (1)
VoxelMorph50, which is a widely used unsupervised framework to learn deformation fields from input image
pairs; and (2) SynthMorph51, which is a synthesis-based registration method that generalizes robustly across
contrasts, resolutions, and anatomies (See Methods and Supplementary Table 5 for details).
Quantitatively, dMRI-F-Reg achieved the highest Dice coefficient across cortical, subcortical, and white
matter regions (Fig. 7a). Visual comparison on example HCP-YA subjects (Fig. 7b) shows that dMRI-F-Reg
produced superior anatomical alignment, with warped anatomical regions more closely matching the fixed space
than those from the compared method. The largest improvement was observed in the cortical regions, a partic-
ularly challenging area due to its highly folded geometry and substantial inter-subject variability. Furthermore,
when warping fiber tracts (derived using TractSeg40), dMRI-F-Reg produced the greatest spatial overlap (Fig.
7c), demonstrating more accurate alignment of tract-specific white-matter anatomy. Across all eight testing
datasets, dMRI-F-Reg demonstrated statistically robust and spatially precise registration, achieving significantly
higher Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) scores than SynthMorph (Fig. 7d). The qualitative improvements at
10


tissue boundaries (Fig. 7e) indicate that dMRI-F-Reg better preserves structural similarity, likely due to its abil-
ity to model complex, non-linear deformations without over-smoothing—a common limitation in learning-based
approaches like SynthMorph.

Cross-modal synthesis serves as an important tool for enriching dMRI with supplementary anatomical con-
text, thereby supporting comprehensive neuroimaging analyses. While dMRI offers distinct information about
water molecule diffusion properties, it lacks the explicit anatomical details as provided by T1w images. The pro-
posed dMRI-F-Syn addresses this by learning mappings from the DWI-derived feature representations to the
corresponding T1w data, enabling the prediction of synthetic T1w images from new DWI data (see Methods:
Cross-modal Synthesis). We compared with two SOTA methods: (1) Brain-ID31, which learns contrast-agnostic
anatomical representations to synthesize T1w images for any imaging input (b0 is used in our experiment);
(2) DeepAnat32, which synthesizes T1w images directly from DWI using a U-Net and a hybrid generative
adversarial network (see Supplementary Table 6 for details).
On the HCP-YA data, dMRI-F-Syn demonstrated superior T1w synthesis performance, yielding signifi-
cantly higher SSIM, higher PSNR, and lower NRMSE against the ground-truth T1w images, than all compared
methods (Fig. 8a). Moreover, the synthetic T1w images generated by dMRI-F-Syn enabled accurate corti-
cal morphology estimation, as evidenced by significantly reduced cortical thickness differences relative to the
11


ground-truth (Fig. 8b). Generalization across the seven test datasets was then assessed quantitatively by com-
paring cortical morphometry between the synthesized and acquired T1w images. dMRI-F-Syn achieved the
lowest mean cortical-thickness difference in every dataset (Fig. 8c). (Note that DeepAnat failed to produce
cortical surface reconstructions in several datasets due to abnormal intensity values, though it achieved rela-
tively small cortical-thickness differences in the datasets where it succeeded.) Consistent with these quantitative
findings, visual inspection showed that dMRI-F-Syn produced T1w images with sharper tissue boundaries and
better-preserved local anatomical features (Fig. 8d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dMRI-F-Syn
effectively synthesizes T1w images that preserve anatomical information critical for both voxel-level reconstruc-
tion and downstream cortical-thickness estimation, even when applied to heterogeneous acquisitions with lower
image quality.
Outlook and Conclusion
This work presents dMRI-F, which learns generalizable representations from sparse DWI inputs to support data
enhancement and downstream analyses across heterogeneous acquisitions and populations. The enhanced DWIs
could broaden the use of sparsely sampled datasets for studying structural connectivity and tissue microstructure
across development, aging and disease. The ability to generate DWIs at arbitrary diffusion directions across
multi shells could also support the training of other deep learning models with different sampling requirements.
Beyond data enhancement, the learned representations could reduce reliance on additional anatomical scans and
cross-modal registration in downstream analyses. These capabilities could enable more comprehensive diffusion
imaging analyses from shorter acquisitions, potentially reducing patient burden and improving accessibility.
Building on these capabilities, future work could extend dMRI-F to populations with tumours and other lesions,
with particular attention to the preservation of pathological anatomy. Further studies could also explore the
potential of the learned representations for predicting disease status, severity and treatment response, extending
their use beyond the parcellation, registration and synthesis tasks evaluated here. Finally, integration with
scanner reconstruction workflows and prospective evaluation using shortened acquisition protocols could help
establish the reliability and practical value of dMRI-F in clinical settings.
Methods
The proposed dMRI-F is a foundation model for dMRI that performs DWI enhancement while supporting diverse
downstream tasks across acquisition protocols and populations. It employs the popular self-supervised learning
(SSL) framework with a Masked Autoencoder (MAE)52 design. SSL has emerged as a powerful paradigm for
training foundation models by transforming unlabeled data into supervised learning signals through carefully
designed pretext objectives53–55. By exploiting the intrinsic structure of unlabeled data, SSL pre-training learns
generalizable features that can be effectively transferred to a wide range of downstream tasks56,57. Among
various SSL strategies, masked reconstruction, as exemplified by MAE52, has proven particularly effective. Its
success relies on two closely related components: a pre-training task that defines what information should be
hidden and recovered, and a model architecture capable of exploiting the remaining observations to infer the
missing content. Further, the learned representations can be transferred to other computational tasks beyond
pre-training. In the rest, we first describe the design of our pre-training task and the model architecture used
to solve it, followed by the fine-tuning process for adapting the learned latent features to downstream tasks.
Pretraining for DWI Enhancement
Pretraining Task
The goal of our pre-training is to reconstruct missing DWI volumes from sparse input, thereby enabling effective
DWI enhancement. In natural images and standard 3D medical images, reconstruction is commonly achieved by
masking image patches or volumetric blocks. dMRI, however, presents a different setting: the acquired data form
a 4D volume spanning three spatial dimensions and an additional diffusion-encoding dimension, where each DWI
volume corresponds to a specific q-space sampling point determined by the gradient direction (b-vector) and
strength (b-value) (see Fig. 1a). Following the masked reconstruction principle, we formulate the pre-training
task as angular masked reconstruction, which recovers unobserved diffusion signals from sparse observations.
Specifically, let X ∈RH×W ×D×G denote the complete set of DWI signals acquired at non-zero b-values,
where H, W and D are the spatial dimensions, and G is the diffusion-encoding dimension (i.e., the total number
of acquired DWI volumes across all gradient directions and b-value shells). The b0 volumes are excluded from
both the pretraining inputs and reconstruction targets but are retained for subsequent analyses, including
microstructural estimation, FOD estimation, and tractography. We define a masking operator M that selects
a sparse subset of q-space coordinates qs, yielding the q-space subsampled input volumes X = M(X). The
pre-training task is then defined as:
ˆX(qt) = Fθ(qs, X, qt),
12


where qt denotes the target q-space coordinate, and Fθ learns to predict the missing DWIs from the sparse input.
In our pre-training setting, the input X contains only 6 arbitrarily selected DWI volumes at b = 1,000 s/mm2,
while the reconstruction target comprises the full DWI data, including 270 DWIs across b = 1,000, 2,000, and
3,000 s/mm2.
Network Architecture
To solve the pre-training task, we design an SSL network based on the MAE architecture to recover the masked
DWIs. In conventional spatial masked reconstruction tasks, the objective is typically to recover masked patches
from the observed context by exploiting spatial dependencies within the image. Our angular masked recon-
struction task, however, requires modeling additional context, because DWIs acquired under different q-space
locations are not independent but rather reflect the same underlying tissue anatomy. Recovering the masked
DWIs thus requires jointly modeling the relationships among spatial coordinates, underlying tissue anatomy,
and q-space coordinates, motivating an architecture that integrates anatomical context with spatial and q-space
coordinates.
Implicit Neural Representations (INRs)58 are particularly suited to this requirement. INRs are a class of
neural networks that learn continuous, coordinate-based mappings from input coordinates to output signals,
enabling flexible and high-fidelity representation of complex data. Neural radiance fields (NeRF)59 is a landmark
implementation of the INR paradigm, where an INR is trained to represent a 3D scene as a continuous function of
spatial coordinates and viewing directions, enabling scene prediction from novel viewpoints. The design of NeRF
inspires our approach to modeling DWI signals as continuous functions of spatial and q-space coordinates. In
related work, recent dMRI studies have demonstrated the success of INRs for continuously modeling fiber orien-
tation distributions and data super-resolution60–63. However, these approaches typically require subject-specific
optimization and are not designed for direct transfer across acquisition protocols or heterogeneous datasets. In
our study, we developed dMRI-F as a unified pretrained framework that takes sparsely sampled DWI volumes
as inputs and reconstructs complete DWI volumes at unobserved q-space locations. Through q-space masked
reconstruction over a large cohort of training subjects, its encoder learns to extract structural representations
consistently from unseen subjects and heterogeneous datasets without subject-specific optimization.
Specifically, we adopt an encoder–decoder architecture that learns a coordinate-conditioned mapping across
subjects. Given the input DWI data X consisting of K volumes, the encoder Eϕ extracts a structural feature
field Φ that captures anatomical information shared across DWIs. At each spatial location x, the implicit
mapping network within the decoder models the relationship among the local structural feature Φ(x), the K
corresponding q-space coordinates η(qs)K

Fine-Tuning for Downstream Tasks
Following q-space masked reconstruction pre-training, we adapted the learned feature representations to three
downstream tasks: brain parcellation, brain registration, and cross-modal synthesis. For each task, the pre-
trained encoder served as a feature extractor. At each training iteration, six DWI volumes were randomly selected
from the b = 1,000 s/mm2 shell of each subject and used as encoder inputs. A task-specific decoder was then
fine-tuned on a subset of 50 HCP-YA subjects randomly selected from the pre-training set. For the decoder, we
adopted a U-Net architecture optimized with a task-specific loss function. To evaluate performance, we compared
against SOTA methods in each task, on the high-quality HCP-YA dataset and the eight independently acquired
test datasets with clinically typical acquisitions.
Brain Parcellation
For brain parcellation, the goal was to predict a voxel-wise anatomical label from the input DWI volumes. In
our study, we trained a parcellation decoder that mapped the extracted features to a parcellation comprising
101 anatomical labels corresponding to the FreeSurfer Desikan–Killiany (DK) parcellation48. The decoder was
trained using a combination of cross-entropy and Dice losses:
LParc = LCE + LDice,
where the cross-entropy term LCE provided voxel-wise supervision for anatomical classification, and the Dice
term LDice promoted spatial overlap between the predicted and reference regions while reducing the influence
of class imbalance.
We compared the SOTA brain parcellation methods, FastSurfer49 and DDParcel27. FastSurfer aggregates
information from neighboring T1w slices and combines predictions across three anatomical planes to generate
whole-brain parcellations. DDParcel integrates complementary microstructural information from diffusion-
derived parameter maps at multiple levels of the network to generate high-quality anatomical parcellations. For
evaluation, we adopted strategies from a previous DWI parcellation study27. For HCP-YA, the advanced acqui-
sition protocol supported accurate registration between anatomical T1w images and DWIs. We therefore used
FreeSurfer parcellations derived from T1w images and registered to DWI space as the reference, and quantified
performance using Dice. For the testing datasets with clinically typical acquisitions, lower image quality and
less reliable cross-modality registration prevented the construction of comparably accurate reference parcella-
tions in DWI space. We therefore assessed regional homogeneity using the relative standard deviation (RSD)
of FA, defined as the within-region standard deviation divided by its mean. Lower RSD values indicate greater
anatomical and microstructural coherence within the predicted regions.
Brain Registration
For brain registration, the goal was to estimate a non-linear deformation field aligning the DWI data of a
moving subject to a fixed subject. To estimate this field, the features extracted from the moving and fixed
subjects were jointly processed by a registration network. The predicted deformation field was then applied
to the entire moving-subject DWI series to warp it into fixed-subject space. The network was optimized using
image-similarity and deformation-smoothness losses:
LReg = LMSE + Lgrad,
where the MSE term LMSE promotes intensity agreement between the warped moving and fixed b0 images,
and the gradient term Lgrad constrains spatial variations in the displacement field to encourage smooth and
anatomically plausible deformations.
Registration performance was evaluated against the SOTA registration methods: VoxelMorph50 and Synth-
Morph51. VoxelMorph learns a direct mapping from image pairs to dense deformation fields using a convolutional
neural network, enabling rapid deformable registration in a single forward pass. SynthMorph learns registration
from images synthesized with diverse contrasts and deformations, using a label-overlap objective to promote
anatomical alignment independent of image intensities. This training strategy supports registration across MRI
14


contrasts, including those not encountered during training. Registration performance was evaluated at comple-
mentary anatomical and white-matter tract levels. For HCP-YA, FreeSurfer-derived cortical, subcortical, and
white-matter parcellations from each moving subject were warped into fixed-subject space using the deforma-
tion field predicted by each method. Their spatial overlap with the corresponding fixed-subject parcellations
was quantified using the Dice coefficient. To assess tract alignment across the complete diffusion acquisition,
each deformation field was also applied to all moving-subject FOD volumes. TractSeg was then applied to the
warped moving-subject FODs and the fixed-subject FODs, and the resulting tract segmentations were compared
using the same metrics. For the eight test datasets, registration performance was assessed using normalized
cross-correlation (NCC) between the warped moving and fixed b0 images, complemented by visual inspection
of local anatomical correspondence.

For T1w image synthesis, we compared with the Brain-ID31 and DeepAnat32. Brain-ID learns contrast-

levels, supporting T1w synthesis through a lightweight task-specific output layer. In our comparison, b0 images

supporting anatomical segmentation. For HCP-YA, accurately aligned T1w reference images enabled voxel-wise
evaluation of the three methods using SSIM, PSNR and NRMSE. Preservation of cortical morphology was further
assessed by applying FastSurfer to the synthesized T1w images and comparing the resulting cortical thickness
maps with the FreeSurfer-derived HCP-YA references. Cortical thickness differences were also visualized for a
representative subject. For the independent datasets, inconsistent alignment between the acquired T1w images
and DWIs precluded reliable voxel-wise comparison. We therefore applied FastSurfer to both the synthesized
and acquired T1w images and compared the resulting cortical thickness estimates to assess anatomical fidelity
at the cortical-surface level.
Datasets and Implementation
Training Data
We selected the HCP-YA data as the pre-training dataset and included 200 young healthy adults (age,
28.84 ± 3.51 years; 118 females and 82 males). Of these, 180 subjects were used for training and 20 for vali-
dation (see Extended Data Fig. 1 for the ablation study on training data size). Imaging was performed on a
customized Siemens 3 T Connectome Skyra scanner using the high-quality HCP acquisition protocol. The dMRI
data were acquired with TE = 89.5 ms, TR = 5,520 ms and a 1.25-mm isotropic voxel size. Each subject had 288
volumes, comprising 18 b0 images and 270 DWIs distributed equally across three shells at b = 1,000, 2,000,
and 3,000 s/mm2. Images were acquired with both left-to-right and right-to-left phase encoding to facilitate cor-
rection of echo-planar imaging distortions. The b = 1,000 s/mm2 shell was used to construct the sparse input,
reflecting its broad availability in conventional and legacy dMRI protocols, whereas the complete multi-shell
acquisition served as the prediction target. The corresponding T1w images were acquired with TE = 2.14 ms,
TR = 2,400 ms and a 0.7-mm isotropic voxel size. We used data processed with the HCP minimal preprocessing
pipelines, which corrected EPI and eddy-current distortions, subject motion and gradient nonlinearities, and
registered the dMRI data to the native T1w structural space at 1.25-mm isotropic resolution. This processing
provided precise within-subject alignment between the dMRI and T1-weighted images. The minimally prepro-
cessed DWIs were further denoised using Patch2Self24 to suppress residual noise and improve the clarity of
anatomical structures.
Testing Data
To evaluate the generalizability of dMRI-F beyond the pre-training data, we used the HCP-YA test set together
with eight test datasets: ABIDE64, ADNI46, ADNI-DOD65, HABS66, ICBM67, SCAN68, NIFD69, and PPMI70.
Together, the HCP-YA test set and the eight test datasets span a broad age range and include healthy par-
ticipants as well as individuals with neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, psychiatric, and trauma-related
conditions, with data acquired using diverse scanners and diffusion protocols. Detailed sample sizes, demographic
15


and diagnostic characteristics, scanner information, and acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary
Table 3. The original studies were approved by their respective institutional review boards or ethics committees,
and all de-identified data were used in accordance with the corresponding data-use agreements. Participants in
the HCP-YA test set and the eight test datasets did not overlap with those used for pre-training. Data from the
HCP-YA test set were processed using the same HCP minimal preprocessing pipelines71 described above. For
the test datasets available in DICOM format, the images were converted to NIfTI while preserving the associ-
ated b-values and diffusion gradient directions. All dMRI data from the eight test datasets underwent correction
of motion- and eddy-current-induced distortions using DTIPrep (v1.2.11)72. Brain masks were generated using
CNN-Diffusion-MRIBrain-Segmentation (v0.3)73. Patch2Self was then applied to the complete diffusion series
to generate the denoised reference data, whereas the six sparse input DWIs were selected from the corresponding
undenoised acquisitions. Finally, all dMRI data from the eight test datasets underwent visual quality assessment.
Implementation
The input DWIs were min–max normalized using the intensities across DWIs for each subject. During pre-
training, we randomly reduced the spatial resolution of the input DWIs as data augmentation to promote the
learning of more generalizable representations. All models except the registration model received patches of 64
× 64 × 64 voxels as input. For registration, the extracted feature patches were first reassembled into whole-
brain feature volumes to preserve spatial continuity, then processed by the registration network. The structural
encoder, volumetric decoder, and task-specific decoders for all downstream tasks were implemented using 3D U-
Net architectures. The learning rate was set to 1×10−4. All models were implemented in PyTorch, and training
and inference were performed on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs.

DWI prediction performance on the HCP-YA test set was evaluated after separately removing the SH
encoding or the volumetric decoder. In the variant without SH encoding, q-space coordinates were passed
directly to the decoder without SH transformation. In the variant without the volumetric decoder, the decoder’s
implicit mapping network directly predicted voxel-wise signal intensities to form the target DWI, rather than
generating latent codes for subsequent volumetric decoding. The complete model achieved higher SSIM and
PSNR and lower NRMSE than either ablated variant, supporting the contribution of both components to DWI
prediction accuracy.

Extended Data Fig. 1 — Effect of training data size on DWI enhancement performance.
To examine how the amount of training data affects DWI enhancement, we varied the training data size from
100 to 200 and 500 subjects and evaluated the resulting dMRI-F models on the HCP-YA test set. Performance
improved with larger training datasets, as reflected by higher SSIM and PSNR and lower NRMSE. For all box
plots, the center line denotes the median, the box spans the interquartile range, and the whiskers extend to the
most extreme values within 1.5 times the interquartile range of the box. White crosses indicate means, connected
by black lines to illustrate trends across training cohort sizes; plus signs denote outliers beyond the whiskers.
Extended Data Fig. 2 — Effect of the number of input diffusion directions on DWI prediction
performance.
Separate dMRI-F models were trained with 6, 10, 20, or 25 input DWI volumes to assess how the number of
sampled diffusion directions affects model prediction accuracy. Each model was evaluated on the HCP-YA test
set using the same number of input volumes as during training. Enhancement performance generally improved as
the number of input directions increased. For all box plots, the center line denotes the median, the box spans the
interquartile range, and the whiskers extend to the most extreme values within 1.5 times the interquartile range
of the box. White crosses indicate means, connected by black lines to illustrate trends across input direction
counts; plus signs denote outliers beyond the whiskers.
