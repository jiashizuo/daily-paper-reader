Abstract
Importance
The search for reliable neuroimaging biomarkers of mental disorders has remained
elusive despite decades of research. Why clinically consequential disorders yield
weak neuroimaging separability remains unresolved. Drawing on statistical
thermodynamics and the metabolic costs of neural coding, we propose that psy-
chiatric disorders produce constrained deformations of a largely shared repertoire
of brain activity patterns, limiting the class-specific information available for
classification.
Observations
We develop a framework linking physical resource constraints, landscape defor-
mation, distributional separation, and attainable classification, and examine its
components using human neuroimaging, synthetic data, and an exactly speci-
fied physical model. In resting-state fMRI from 1952 FOR2107 participants (823
with major depressive disorder; 1129 healthy controls), held-out energy-landscape
estimates and nonlinear classifiers agreed across 13 comparisons (r = 0.996).
Similar results emerged at both FOR2107 centers and in 900 SRPBS partici-
pants spanning multiple psychiatric diagnoses. In synthetic data, classification
improved with distributional separation and approached the optimum. In an
equilibrium three-well model, statistical energy equaled physical potential energy
scaled by inverse temperature. Modest deformation redistributed state occupancy
while optimal snapshot classification remained weak. A literature map found
insufficiently comparable evidence to test the human metabolic constraint.
Conclusions
Clinically consequential differences may arise within a largely shared neural
repertoire. The thermodynamic framework connects physical deformation to clas-
sification under specified assumptions; its metabolic implementation in humans
remains untested. Testing this mechanism requires joint measurements of brain
activity, bodily physiology, and metabolism.

1 Introduction
Psychiatric disorders can profoundly alter mood, cognition, motivation, and behav-
ior [1, 2]. Yet reliable neuroimaging biomarkers remain elusive despite decades of
intensive research. Even large, harmonized studies lack individual-level markers with
clinically relevant accuracy. Structural and functional correlates are typically weak,
heterogeneous, and extensively overlap between affected and unaffected individuals [3].
In major depressive disorder (MDD), analysis of 4,021 individuals from three clinical
cohorts found weak but replicable neuroanatomical effects [4]. Large multisite stud-
ies likewise report modest average neuroimaging effects, and diagnostic performance
remains similarly modest despite varied preprocessing, features, modality, and archi-
tecture [5]. Independent multicenter functional and structural imaging analyses concur
[6, 7].
This plateau—the failure to recover a reliable individual diagnosis—raises a ques-
tion more fundamental than classifier choice: how much diagnostic separation exists in
the measured distributions? The mathematical limit is simple. For a fixed measurement
and representation—for example, adults aged 20–65 years with depression, imaged
with BOLD fMRI and summarized by functional connectivity—the class-conditional
distributions determine the best attainable discrimination. Ranking observations
by the likelihood ratio yields the optimal ROC curve [8]. A flexible decoder may
approach that optimum, but cannot recover class information absent from its input.
Achieved performance therefore depends on sample, estimation, and algorithm; the
population-level limit depends on the representation—and its underlying biology [9].
That limit does not explain large clinical differences despite overlapping images.
Dynamical-systems accounts offer one biological explanation [10, 11]. Psychiatric
disorders need not create distinct neural architectures or entirely new brain-state
repertoires. Small geometric or flow changes in a shared dynamical system can
alter state occupancy, persistence, transition probabilities, and perturbation responses
[12, 13]. Because behavior depends on these temporal properties, these changes can
profoundly affect function while only modestly altering static or cross-sectional repre-
sentations. Consistently, MDD studies report altered temporal variability, occupancy,
and transitions of functional brain states [14].
Drawing on statistical thermodynamics, metabolic costs of neural coding, and the
classification bound above, we hypothesize that psychiatric disorders—examined pri-
marily through MDD in FOR2107 and secondarily through multi-disorder comparisons
in SRPBS—produce energy-limited deformations of a largely shared activity landscape
constrained by connectomic architecture (Section 2; Fig. 1). Neural representations
are physical: membrane potentials, spikes, synaptic activity, and circuit modification
require energy [15, 16]. The brain encodes sensory, interoceptive, and social regulari-
ties with finite metabolic supply. In this proposal, input reshapes a shared repertoire,
connectomic organization restricts activity propagation directions, and metabolism
limits the extent, speed, and stability of those deformations [17, 18]. The constraint is
not globally scalar. Energy demand differs across neural populations, and circuits for
cognitive control and effort-based decision-making are particularly costly, so reduced
energy availability should tilt the landscape unevenly [16]. Peripheral and interoceptive
3


signals further modulate node-specific energy status, coupling the accessible reper-
toire to organismal homeostasis rather than to a purely central budget [19]. Behavior
emerges from supported trajectories; neuroimaging incompletely observes their distri-
bution. Both datasets provide one time-averaged connectivity snapshot per person;
fitted distributions therefore describe group-level snapshot overlap.
We test this distributional consequence in four steps. First, in functional-
connectivity data from 1,952 participants in the Marburg–M¨unster FOR2107 cohort
[20], we compare separability hierarchies from held-out maximum-entropy likelihood-
ratio estimates and nonlinear classifiers across thirteen age, recorded-sex, MDD,
and symptom contrasts, and test replication in each independently acquired center.
We repeat this comparison in 900 participants from the multisite SRPBS dataset,
which includes MDD, autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, and
healthy controls [21]. Second, in controlled simulations with known source distribu-
tions, we test whether landscape separation determines attainable AUC and whether
nonlinear classifiers approach the source-defined optimum. Third, an exactly solvable
shared triple-well model examines how modest deformations can redistribute state
occupancy while snapshot classification remains weak. Fourth, we review the metabolic
and neuroimaging-classification literatures [22] to assess whether human evidence can
test the link between physical energy budgets and classification limits. These analyses
distinguish established distributional findings from the biological mechanism requiring
multimodal experiments.
2 Theoretical framework
We propose that psychiatric disorders alter psychological function through resource-
constrained deformations of a largely shared neural repertoire. Dynamical accounts
motivate changes in stability, occupancy, and transitions [10, 11]; neural models
explain how connectivity, coupling, and noise shape this repertoire [12, 13, 23]. Altered
functional-network dynamics and co-activation probabilities in depression motivate
examining its statistical expression [14, 24]. The metabolic costs of signaling and
cognition motivate, without establishing, a physical constraint on these deformations

3 Results
3.1 Generative and discriminative analyses recover the same
hierarchy of neuroimaging separability
The same 120-variable representation, tree-structured maximum-entropy model, and
RBF support-vector classifier were applied to two resting-state cohorts. FOR2107
is the primary sample; the smaller, independent multi-disorder SRPBS sample tests
generality of the hierarchy.
3.1.1 Marburg–M¨unster FOR2107 cohort
We tested the framework’s distributional consequence in 1,952 FOR2107 participants:
1,129 healthy controls and 823 participants with MDD. Each participant contributed
4,950 functional connections among 100 brain regions. Thirteen binary comparisons
covered recorded sex, age thresholds of 30, 40, and 50 years, healthy-control ver-
sus MDD status, and eight diagnosis-stratified contrasts involving BDI-I depression
severity or SCL-90-R global symptom burden. Within-diagnosis medians defined
sample-relative symptom strata rather than clinical cutoffs.
6


Representation learning and model fitting used only training folds in five repeats
of stratified five-fold cross-validation. Connectivity was compressed into a 120-
dimensional binary representation, and class-specific maximum-entropy distributions
were fitted on a common maximum-information spanning tree. Fitted statistical-
energy differences ranked held-out participants. We compared this generative score
with an RBF support-vector classifier trained on the same fold-specific representation
(Appendices B and C).
Both approaches recovered nearly identical classification-difficulty hierarchies
(Fig. 2A). Across the thirteen contrasts, mean AUC was 0.661 for maximum entropy
and 0.669 for the RBF classifier, with an outcome-level correlation of r = 0.996. Age
was most distinguishable: maximum-entropy AUCs were 0.770, 0.804, and 0.796 for the
three thresholds. Recorded-sex AUC was 0.714, versus 0.613 for healthy-control versus
MDD classification and 0.598 to 0.628 for symptom-defined comparisons. Fold-level
estimates retained this demographic-versus-clinical ordering.
Held-out marginal agreement supported the models’ capture of first-order and
pairwise structure in the binary representation (Appendices C.7 and E.2; Appendix
Fig. E1). Analyses repeated separately in the non-overlapping Marburg and M¨unster
subsets also reproduced the broad hierarchy and close generative–discriminative agree-
ment, despite independent acquisition by different teams and scanners (Appendix E.8;
Appendix Fig. E4a,c).
These results support the distributional argument: clinical contrasts remained
weakly distinguishable with either generative or discriminative estimation. Neverthe-
less, fitted maximum-entropy AUC was a conditional reference. The RBF classifier
slightly exceeded it for some outcomes, and classifiers using uncompressed connectivity
exceeded it further (Appendix E). The finding therefore establishes agreement in sep-
arability hierarchies within the analyzed representation, not a universal classification
ceiling.
3.1.2 SRPBS multi-disorder dataset
To test the generality of FOR2107 agreement, we repeated the 120-variable analysis in
900 participants from the SRPBS Multi-disorder MRI Dataset: 500 healthy controls,
220 participants with MDD, 125 with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and 55 with
schizophrenia-spectrum disorder (SSD). Each analysis stage used only cross-validation
training data. The analysis included recorded sex, the same three age thresholds, all
six pairwise diagnostic comparisons, and four BDI-II comparisons between healthy
controls and participants with MDD, divided into high- and low-symptom groups
(Appendix E.4).
Across fourteen comparisons, mean AUC was 0.691 for maximum entropy and
0.706 for the RBF support-vector classifier applied to the same binary states. The
methods’ AUCs were strongly correlated (r = 0.975; Fig. 2B). Maximum-entropy
AUCs ranged from 0.717 to 0.740 for the demographic comparisons. HC versus MDD
reached 0.642, and the four BDI comparisons ranged from 0.539 to 0.676. The other
diagnostic comparisons varied more widely (0.611–0.827), highest for MDD versus
ASD. As in FOR2107, demographic contrasts were more separable than HC versus
MDD and the symptom-stratified HC–MDD comparisons. Other diagnostic contrasts
7


were more variable, with some exceeding demographic AUCs. Replication concerned
close agreement between maximum-entropy and support-vector classifier estimates
across comparisons.
Diagnostic-group distributions across SRPBS sites and acquisition protocols limit
AUC interpretation, particularly for ASD and SSD. These values may reflect site
and protocol differences alongside diagnosis. The main result is close maximum-
entropy and support-vector classifier AUC agreement for identical comparisons and
binary brain-state data. Full comparison definitions and uncertainty intervals appear
in Appendix E.4.

3.2 Known source distributions determine attainable
classification in synthetic data
We next examined classification under known generating distributions. Fixed healthy-
control and MDD maximum-entropy models defined a path through their natural
parameters: α = 0 reproduced the reference source, α = 1 the fitted MDD source, and
larger values extrapolated along the same deformation direction. Samples were drawn
directly from these tree-structured distributions, whose likelihood ratio supplied the
Bayes-optimal score (Appendix D).
Classification increased monotonically along this path (Fig. 2C). At α = 0, the
distributions coincided and both the finite-sample oracle and held-out RBF classifier
performed near chance. At α = 1, finite-sample oracle AUC was 0.781 and classi-
fier AUC was 0.720. The classifier followed this trajectory below the oracle at weak
and intermediate separations; both approached one as the sources became nearly dis-
joint. Separately fitted Marburg and M¨unster source models reproduced this pattern
(Appendix E.8; Appendix Fig. E4b,d).
The synthetic endpoint concerns model-generated samples, not held-out human
observations; its AUC need not equal the empirical MDD AUC. The experiment
tests whether, under known source distributions, the energy-difference score sup-
plies the population-optimal ranking and nonlinear classification approaches it as
separation increases. The remaining gap reflects finite-sample learning and classifier
approximation of that ranking.

3.4 Existing human evidence does not yet connect metabolic
differences to classification limits
Finally, the targeted evidence map assessed whether published human studies could
test the proposed metabolic link [22]. Coverage was scored on a grid of nine conditions
by five measurement classes—FDG-PET glucose metabolism, 31P-MRS ATP/phos-
phocreatine, quantitative ASL cerebral blood flow, CMRO2, and ketone/lactate
dynamics—giving 45 cells. Only one cell, schizophrenia FDG-PET, supplied a usable
pooled metabolic effect (Appendix F.6). Matching metabolic and classification records
produced thirteen candidate pairs, but none were commensurable enough for a cor-
relation. Within pairs, six contrasted different populations or illness stages. Across
pairs, the thirteen metabolic halves fell into six quantity classes—global absolute
glucose metabolism, regional absolute glucose metabolism, regional relative glucose
metabolism, creatine-kinase rate constants, CMRO2, and mixed units—with no class
holding more than three pairs and no common scale on which diagnoses could be
ordered. No metabolism–AUC correlation was therefore computed (Appendix F.7).
Reported metabolic effects depended strongly on spatial scope, normalization, mea-
sured quantity, modality, and sample definition (Appendix F.8; Fig. 4). Demonstrating
measurement non-equivalence rather than a pooled estimate, this analysis supports a
qualitative conclusion: aggregate evidence lacks commensurate measurements to test
the proposed metabolic constraint. This identifies the missing empirical link without
supporting or refuting the hypothesis.
4 Discussion
This paper addresses the recurring disproportion between large behavioral and clinical
differences and small, extensively overlapping neuroimaging effects [3, 5]. Dynamical-
systems accounts offer an explanation [10, 11, 29]. If psychiatric differences arise
through changes in occupancy, persistence, and transitions within a largely shared
repertoire, a cross-sectional representation may change only modestly even when expe-
rience and behavior change substantially [12, 13]. The question extends beyond where
brain activity is measured to the dynamics generating and maintaining its distribution.
We hypothesize that internal and external inputs deform this activity landscape
under joint connectomic and physical resource constraints. These constraints may
restrict brain-generated and sustained distributions, consequently limiting discrimi-
nation from fixed measurements. This framework connects that biological proposal
to an established statistical result: class-conditional distributions determine optimal
classification performance [8, 25]. It offers a possible physical explanation for weak
separability despite clinical consequences.
Empirical, synthetic, and mechanical analyses examine complementary links. In
FOR2107, generative and discriminative readouts recovered almost identical hierar-
chies: age and recorded sex were substantially more distinguishable than diagnosis
or symptoms. This pattern replicated separately in Marburg and M¨unster. In the
smaller SRPBS sample spanning additional diagnoses, generative and discrimina-
tive AUCs again agreed closely across comparisons. Demographic contrasts exceeded
HC versus MDD and symptom-stratified HC–MDD separability, but some other
11


diagnostic contrasts exceeded demographic separability. SRPBS therefore supports
agreement between methods across broader comparisons, not a uniform demographic-
over-diagnostic hierarchy. Uneven diagnostic distributions across SRPBS sites preclude
interpreting diagnostic contrasts as pure diagnostic effects. In simulations, known
source distributions defined the classification optimum, which nonlinear classifiers
approached as separation increased. In the mechanical system, a modest deforma-
tion redistributed occupancy while snapshot classification remained weak. Together,
these findings suggest classification plateaus reflect limited measured distributional
separation alongside estimation and algorithmic limitations. The mechanical example
also establishes a work–distribution–classification connection under explicit physical
assumptions; its AUC-matched landmarks illustrate compatibility with the empirical
pattern rather than independently predicting it.
Biological interpretation requires distinguishing statistical from physical energy.
Maximum-entropy energy expresses negative log probability, not glucose use, ATP
turnover, oxygen consumption, or heat [17, 18]. Its correspondence to inverse-
temperature-scaled physical energy follows from the mechanical system’s equilibrium
assumptions and cannot simply be transferred to the open, driven brain [26]. Neverthe-
less, neural distributions are physically implemented: signaling and circuit modification
require energy, while architecture constrains how activity propagates [15, 23, 30]. This
motivates testing whether physical resources constrain accessible landscape changes’
extent, rate, and persistence.
Such a constraint need not act uniformly. Metabolic demands differ across neural
processes and circuits [16], while peripheral and interoceptive signals couple neural
resource availability to organismal physiology [19, 31, 32]. The budget is therefore
distributed across an embodied system. Analysis in Appendix A.9 formalizes this
by combining available resources, directional deformation costs, and their effects on
the measured distribution. Crucially, those costs must be calibrated independently of
distributional divergence. The bound is testable but does not estimate FOR2107 or
SRPBS metabolic expenditure.
Human evidence could not supply this connection: the targeted map found no com-
mensurable candidate metabolism–classification pairs: within pairs the halves often
describe different populations or stages, and across pairs the metabolic halves share
no common scale (Appendix F). Reported metabolic effects also depended on spatial
scope, normalization, modality, and population definition (Fig. 4). These findings iden-
tify a measurement gap rather than evidence against the hypothesis. Establishing this
link requires measuring input, structure, calibrated energy use, activity distributions,
and behavior in the same individuals at compatible scales.
A focused experimental program could use repeated measurements to relate
metabolic status to occupancy, dwell times, transitions, and standardized perturba-
tion responses [29, 33]. Controlled changes in resource availability would provide a
stronger test than cross-sectional associations, provided their effects on arousal, vascu-
lar responses, and neural signaling are distinguished. The central prediction concerns
the range of distributional changes accessible under measured resource constraints.
Reliable violations would challenge a specified quantitative bound after checking its
assumptions and calibration. An absent simple expenditure–separation correlation
12


would be less decisive: systems need not operate at their resource limit. Behav-
ioral measurements would establish whether constrained changes affect symptoms and
function.
Several limitations remain. Diagnostic heterogeneity, measurement noise, temporal
averaging, and discarded information can also produce overlap. Empirical maximum-
entropy references depend on the binary representation and sparse model; more
informative features may improve discrimination. Between-person connectivity distri-
butions also do not directly establish within-person attractors or a shared dynamical
landscape [34]. These results neither identify metabolic constraints as causing weak
classification nor exclude informative subgroups or longitudinal signatures. They
establish a distributional argument and physically realize the proposed connection.
For psychiatry, substantial dysfunction may emerge within a largely shared reper-
toire. Progress requires measuring repertoire organization, change, and which changes
the organism can sustain. The framework formalizes this possibility and identi-
fies human measurements needed to determine whether physiological constraints
contribute to persistent neuroimaging classification limits.

5 Conclusion
Profound clinical differences need not produce sharply distinguishable brain images.
Our findings support a distributional explanation for weak classification and motivate
a broader hypothesis: psychiatric disorders deform a largely shared neural repertoire
within structural and metabolic constraints. The human analyses establish agree-
ment in measured separability; the mechanical system demonstrates how occupancy
can change while snapshot discrimination remains weak. Testing the proposed phys-
iological constraint requires joint measurements of brain activity, architecture, and
metabolism. Progress depends on understanding which neural changes produce clinical
consequences and how much of that change our measurements can reveal.

A.1
Notation and scope
This appendix supplies the derivations used in Section 2 and the exact mechanical
analysis in Section 3.3. Conditions are indexed by g ∈{0, 1}. In the mechanical exam-
ple, Ug(x) denotes physical potential energy, β = (kBT)−1 is inverse temperature, and
pg(x) is the associated equilibrium density. In the maximum-entropy analyses, Eg(z)
denotes dimensionless statistical energy and pg(z) is a fitted probability mass func-
tion over binary latent states. These uses of “energy” become mathematically identical
for an equilibrium Boltzmann system after multiplication of physical energy by β.
For fMRI, however, Eg is a negative-log-probability representation and is not a direct
measurement of ATP turnover, glucose use, oxygen consumption, or dissipated heat.
The mechanical derivation therefore establishes the deformation–work–distribution–
classification chain under explicit equilibrium assumptions; the empirical analysis tests
its distributional and classification consequences only.

to distribution parameters is required. This is the local bound summarized in Eq. (3);
the remainder assumes fixed positive-definite C and a sufficiently small budget. The
quantity B represents metabolic energy only when the input cost is independently
calibrated in physical units. Thus, this derivation identifies the additional assumptions
needed to connect physical accessibility to distributional and classification limits; it is
not used to infer metabolic expenditure from the FOR2107 distributions.

B.3
Image preprocessing and functional-connectivity
construction
T1-weighted data underwent bias-field correction with fsl anat and brain extraction
with SynthStrip [37]. Functional data were processed using FSL 6.0.7.6 [38]. The pre-
processing workflow comprised MCFLIRT motion correction, BET brain extraction,
5-mm full-width-at-half-maximum Gaussian spatial smoothing, and temporal high-
pass filtering with a 100-s cutoff period. Functional images were registered to the
participant’s T1-weighted image and normalized to 2-mm MNI152 space using 12-
degree-of-freedom affine registration. Within-subject spatial independent-component
analysis was performed with MELODIC using automatic dimensionality estimation,
followed by FSL-FIX removal of components classified as motion, physiological, or
scanner artifact [39]. Both datasets were preprocessed using an identical pipeline.
Regional time series were extracted using the Schaefer cortical parcellation with
100 regions assigned to 17 functional networks [40, 41], provided in 2-mm FSL MNI152
space. For each region, the BOLD signal was averaged across all voxels with valid sig-
nal, excluding voxels outside the functional field of view, and the resulting time series
were z-scored. Functional connectivity was then computed as the Pearson correlation
between all pairs of regional time series, yielding a 100 × 100 undirected connectivity
matrix per participant. Each participant was summarized by the upper triangle of this
matrix, which produced 100 × 99/2 = 4,950 features per participant. All downstream
models used this 100-region, 4,950-feature representation.

Appendix C
Empirical maximum-entropy and
machine-learning analysis
This section details the fold-local generative and discriminative pipelines compared in
Fig. 2A,B; their additional empirical results are reported in Appendix E.
C.1
End-to-end validation design
Each of the 13 FOR2107 outcomes and 14 SRPBS outcomes was evaluated using
five independently seeded repeats of stratified five-fold cross-validation, yielding 25
held-out estimates per outcome. Every data-dependent operation was restricted to
the outer training fold: feature scaling, autoencoder fitting, latent-state construction,
topology learning, class-conditional maximum-entropy estimation, preprocessing for
the discriminative classifier, and classifier fitting. Held-out participants were used only
for evaluation. This fold-local design prevents leakage from representation learning
and graph selection.
Fold AUCs were averaged across the 25 held-out evaluations. Ninety-five percent
confidence intervals were calculated from 10,000 class-stratified subject-level bootstrap
30


samples while retaining the repeated held-out predictions belonging to each resam-
pled participant. A maximum-entropy fit was considered numerically valid only when
the optimizer reported convergence, all held-out scores were finite, and the maximum
absolute gradient did not exceed 10−3. Optimization was initially limited to 500 iter-
ations. In a post hoc numerical sensitivity check, each fold that did not meet these
criteria was refitted once with a limit of 2,000 iterations, using the same data split,
learned representation, model settings, and random seed. Retry was determined solely
by numerical fit status and not by AUC. The retried results were adopted only if
every refitted fold met the same criteria. Final summaries used the original predictions
for initially valid folds and the replacement predictions for retried folds; confidence
intervals were recalculated from these final predictions.
C.2
Binary autoencoder
Within each outer training fold, the 4,950 connectivity features were centered and
scaled using training-participant statistics. A symmetric autoencoder mapped the
standardized vector through a 240-unit nonlinear hidden layer to 120 bounded latent
activations. The decoder mirrored the encoder. During the forward pass, each activa-
tion was thresholded at zero to form a hard binary state; a straight-through gradient
estimator enabled optimization through the discrete bottleneck. Reconstruction was
computed from the binary state itself, ensuring that the representation fitted by
maximum entropy was also the information available to reconstruct connectivity.
The autoencoder minimized mean squared reconstruction error for 100 epochs
using mini-batches of 128. Negative activations and non-negative activations defined
the two binary values. The fold-specific continuous activations, thresholded states, and
decoded connectivity were retained for the comparison analyses.

Appendix D
Fixed-source synthetic benchmark
D.1
Purpose and source distributions
The synthetic analysis tested the ceiling interpretation under correct model speci-
fication and provides the methods for Fig. 2C. Completed empirical autoencoders,
connectivity scaling parameters, class-conditional maximum-entropy parameters, and
graph topologies were selected as fixed sources and were not relearned when construct-
ing the source distributions. Two source families were examined: the 120-variable tree
with 119 edges and the 22-variable full-pairwise model with 231 edges. Their his-
torical held-out diagnosis AUCs before synthetic generation were 0.628 and 0.582,
respectively. These values describe the selected source fits, not the finalized repeated-
cross-validation estimates in Table E5. Samples generated from these fitted sources
need not reproduce held-out human AUCs.
D.2
Controlled landscape deformation
For each source family, the HC natural parameters θ0 and MDD natural parameters
θ1 defined the path in Eq. (A37). Thus α = 0 reproduced the HC source, α = 1
reproduced the fitted MDD source, 0 < α < 1 interpolated between them, and α > 1
extrapolated along the same direction. The grid used increments of 0.1 from 0 to 2
and increments of 0.5 from 2.5 to 7.
At every α, five independent datasets were generated, each containing 1,000
reference-class and 1,000 target-class observations. The tree model supplied exact inde-
pendent junction-tree samples; the 22-variable model was sampled from its exactly
enumerated categorical distribution. Binary samples were additionally decoded to
4,950 connectivity features when a reconstructed-connectivity analysis was required.

E.5
High-dimensional sparse versus low-dimensional
full-pairwise models
Both pipelines began with the original 4,950-element functional-connectivity vector.
The 120-variable tree model exceeded the 22-variable full-pairwise model in all 13 com-
parisons, with a mean AUC advantage of 0.050 and a range of 0.023–0.083. Separate
descriptive full-cohort reconstructions also showed greater fidelity after binary encod-
ing at 120 variables than at 22 variables, with mean participant-level correlations of
0.958 and 0.877, respectively. These reconstruction values describe the full-cohort net-
works, rather than held-out reconstruction by the fold-specific autoencoders. Because
latent dimensionality, autoencoder capacity, and interaction structure differed, the
comparison does not isolate their individual contributions. The results are consistent
with the larger representation preserving useful connectivity information despite the
restriction to a tree.
The discriminative analyses showed the same advantage for information passed
through the binary bottleneck. Mean RBF-SVC AUC was 0.669 versus 0.632 on the
38


120- and 22-variable binary representations, and 0.666 versus 0.624 on connectiv-
ity decoded from those representations. Both comparisons favored the 120-variable
pipeline in every outcome. The classifier applied directly to original connectivity pro-
vided a common baseline, with mean AUC 0.712 in both analyses, because this pipeline
bypassed the autoencoder. Its higher performance shows that even the 120-variable
representation omitted information useful for classification. The advantage over 22
variables therefore concerns the information retained by the compressed pipelines.
E.6
Choice of latent dimensionality and treewidth
We retained d = 120, w = 1 to give priority to reproduction of the latent dis-
tributions while preserving useful discrimination. A separate configuration analysis
compared sparse models with 20–480 latent variables and treewidths 1–5 across diagno-
sis, recorded sex, and the age-40 comparison. Scaling, autoencoder training, topology
learning, and parameter estimation were confined to the corresponding training splits.
Fit was assessed through held-out first-order activation probabilities and co-activation
probabilities on the selected graph edges. Residuals were standardized by their sam-
pling standard errors, and root-mean-square errors were averaged across moment
types, classes, folds, and outcomes. This criterion measures marginal agreement rather
than agreement of the complete joint distribution.
The fit-priority rule retained configurations within one standard error of the best
marginal fit, then ranked them by equally weighted AUC, with complexity used to
break ties. It selected d = 120, w = 1. The rules giving greater priority to AUC
selected d = 240, w = 1. In this configuration analysis, the mean standardized marginal
error was 1.158 at 120 variables and 1.206 at 240 variables. The larger representa-
tion therefore offered somewhat better discrimination at the cost of poorer fit under
this criterion. These configuration comparisons support the rationale for the reported
model; their results are distinct from the repeated cross-validation estimates of the
fixed pipelines.
The fixed-pipeline analysis across all 13 outcomes showed modest gains from dou-
bling the latent size: mean maximum-entropy AUC increased from 0.661 to 0.672,
binary-latent SVC AUC from 0.669 to 0.680, and decoded-connectivity SVC AUC from
0.666 to 0.675. Gains were larger for age and recorded sex; HC versus MDD increased
from 0.613 to 0.619. Increasing treewidth from 1 to 2 at 120 variables slightly improved
pairwise marginal reproduction but did not improve discrimination: mean maximum-
entropy AUC decreased from 0.661 to 0.657. We therefore retained the tree as the
simpler dependence model, and 120 variables as a compromise between marginal fit
and the additional discrimination available at larger latent sizes.

E.8
Replication in non-overlapping acquisition-center subsets
To assess whether the pooled findings depended on combining acquisition centers, we
repeated the empirical and fixed-source synthetic analyses separately in Marburg and
M¨unster. The two subsets contained 1,006 participants in Marburg (642 HC and 364
MDD) and 946 in M¨unster (487 HC and 459 MDD). They comprised different partici-
pants acquired by separate local teams using the scanners described in Appendix B. All
empirical preprocessing of the connectivity features, autoencoder fitting, binary-state
construction, topology learning, and class-conditional estimation were repeated within
the outer training folds of the respective center. Each center used the same 13 compari-
son definitions and five repeats of stratified five-fold cross-validation. Diagnosis-specific
symptom medians were recomputed within each center, so the high/low strata were
relative to that center’s sample. These analyses evaluate reproducibility within two
disjoint subsets of the pooled cohort; they do not evaluate transfer of a model trained
at one center to participants at the other.
Both centers reproduced the broad hierarchy in Fig. 2A: age and recorded sex were
more separable than diagnosis and symptom-defined contrasts. Across the 13 compar-
isons, the correlation between maximum-entropy and binary-latent RBF-SVC AUCs
was r = 0.997 in Marburg and r = 0.995 in M¨unster (Appendix Fig. E4a,c). Mean
maximum-entropy and RBF-SVC AUCs were 0.653 and 0.663 in Marburg, and 0.641
and 0.645 in M¨unster. HC-versus-MDD maximum-entropy AUC was 0.617 and 0.588,
respectively. Thus, the broad ordering and agreement between maximum-entropy and
RBF-SVC AUCs were reproducible across centers, although individual AUCs and the
ordering of closely performing contrasts varied.
40


All 325 fold-level two-class maximum-entropy fits in each center passed the numer-
ical validity criteria. Held-out marginal checks used the same procedure as the pooled
analysis (Appendix C.7; Appendix Figs. E2 and E3). Mean first-order and pairwise
correlations were 0.843 and 0.775 in Marburg, and 0.818 and 0.747 in M¨unster. Corre-
sponding mean root-mean-square errors were 0.072 and 0.068 in Marburg, and 0.073
and 0.069 in M¨unster, compared with 0.052 and 0.051 in the pooled cohort. These
results show that substantial marginal correspondence persisted when each center was
analyzed independently, while absolute errors were larger. Differences in dispersion
can reflect smaller held-out class sizes, sample composition, and independently learned
representations as well as model fit.
For synthetic replication, each center supplied its own full-subset autoencoder and
HC/MDD tree distributions, fitted separately from those of the other center. These
distributions defined center-specific natural-parameter paths using the same α grid,
five independent datasets per grid point, and 1,000 observations per class described
in Appendix D. The native binary-latent RBF-SVC was evaluated by five-fold cross-
validation within each generated dataset. Both benchmarks reproduced the increase in
separability with stronger landscape deformation and convergence toward an AUC of
one (Appendix Fig. E4b,d). At α = 1, finite-sample oracle and RBF-SVC AUCs were
0.846 and 0.772 for Marburg, and 0.804 and 0.721 for M¨unster. The mean classifier
curves remained at or below the finite-sample oracle for positive α, with the sepa-
ration most evident at weak and intermediate deformations. These are benchmarks
of separately fitted source distributions, rather than additional empirical estimates of
diagnostic AUC.
E.9
Interpretive boundary
The mathematical and empirical analyses support two distinct conclusions. For data
generated from the specified maximum-entropy distributions, the likelihood-ratio AUC
is the highest theoretically achievable AUC. For FOR2107 and SRPBS, the fitted
energy-difference AUC estimates the separability captured by one particular 120-
variable tree model, with every step fitted using only the cross-validation training
data. The agreement with RBF-SVC performance shows that the two methods give
nearly the same ordering of comparison difficulty within each dataset. It does not
show that metabolic expenditure has been measured, that SRPBS differences associ-
ated with study site are specific to diagnosis, or that the reported values limit every
possible neuroimaging representation.
Appendix F
Targeted evidence map of brain
metabolism and diagnostic classification
F.1
Aim and status of the analysis
This appendix documents the fourth step introduced in Section 1, reported in
Section 3.4, and discussed in Section 4. The purpose of this analysis was to deter-
mine whether published aggregate evidence could test the hypothesized association
between the magnitude of a diagnosis-related alteration in brain energy metabolism
41


and the attainable performance of a brain-based diagnostic classifier. We designed
it as a targeted evidence map with an exploratory quantitative component; it does
not attempt to review all metabolic abnormalities in psychiatry systematically. The
prespecified protocol was deposited on the Open Science Framework [22] (https:
//doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/PXWSK).
The unit of analysis was a population-matched pair consisting of (i) a standardized
diagnosis-versus-healthy-control effect for a direct brain energy/metabolism measure
and (ii) a held-out diagnosis-versus-healthy-control AUC based on brain-derived fea-
tures. A diagnosis-level correlation would have been considered only if sufficiently
comparable pairs met the predefined eligibility criteria and the comparability cri-
teria described below. Because no pair satisfied all criteria, no metabolism–AUC
meta-regression or correlation was performed. Supplementary Tables S1–S8, including
Tables S1b and S8b, are provided in the supplementary data deposit accompanying
this article, not embedded in this manuscript.
F.2
Information sources and search strategy
PubMed was the principal bibliographic source. The registered protocol covered
major depressive disorder, schizophrenia-spectrum and other psychotic disorders, post-
traumatic stress disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity
disorder, and autism spectrum disorder, and specified PubMed searches for recent
metabolism and proxy studies. The completed map additionally covers panic disorder,
social anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and clinical
high risk for psychosis, and its coverage and supplementary searches were run with-
out a date limit; these extensions and the quantitative-ASL coverage column are
exploratory additions to the registered scope. Conditions outside the coverage grid
were also included exploratorily, for two purposes: selected additional psychiatric and
neurological conditions illustrate measurement dependence in Fig. 4, and two of them,
anorexia nervosa and Alzheimer’s disease, also enter the candidate-pair analysis.
The metabolism search combined diagnostic terms with terms for glucose
metabolism, cerebral glucose metabolism, FDG, FDG-PET, fluorodeoxyglucose,
positron-emission tomography, oxygen metabolism, high-energy phosphates, ketone
metabolism, and related measures. The classifier search combined diagnosis and
healthy-control terms with brain-feature terms (MRI, functional MRI, functional
connectivity, PET, MRS, EEG, and fNIRS), classification terms, and ROC/AUC
terms. Coverage for Appendix Fig. F5A was mapped with a grid of 1406 struc-
tured condition-by-measure queries (every condition-term variant crossed with every
measurement-term variant, including each measurement class’s plain modality name
and standard wordings of each condition), executed without a date limit; null results
were retained (901 of the 1406 queries returned no records). Every record retrieved
for a cell without eligible studies was screened at title and abstract level, as was every
meta-analysis retrieved for a cell coded “studies exist”, which could have supplied a
usable pooled effect (255 decisions on 228 records; Supplementary Table S8b). Cell
codes were assigned from these screened records together with the extraction records
(Supplementary Table S1) and studies located by targeted supplementary searches
42


(Supplementary Table S1b, which records how each study was found). Of the 87 inde-
pendently located supporting records that carry a PMID, the grid retrieved 75 for at
least one of the cells they support, and it retrieved at least one supporting record for
27 of the 31 cells with eligible studies; the remaining four cells rest on studies found
by the supplementary searches. The query strings with per-query record counts are
provided in Supplementary Table S8.
F.3
Screening and eligibility
Records were deduplicated and screened against the following criteria at title and
abstract level and then on the full text where it was accessible, otherwise on the
abstract; for the coverage grid, every screening decision is deposited with its reason
(Supplementary Table S8b). Human metabolism studies were eligible if they compared
a psychiatric group with healthy controls using a brain-derived metabolic measure and
supplied sufficient quantitative or qualitative information to code the contrast. Classi-
fier studies were eligible if they used brain-derived features to distinguish a diagnosis
from healthy controls and reported an extractable ROC AUC or AUROC. Studies
focused only on treatment response, prognosis, symptom severity, within-diagnosis
subtypes, diagnosis-versus-diagnosis classification, peripheral markers, or combined
brain/peripheral models without a separable brain-only estimate were excluded.
F.4
Hierarchy of metabolic measures
The preregistration defined a three-tier hierarchy of measures according to their prox-
imity to physical energy metabolism; the completed map retained this hierarchy and
extended Tier 1 to include ketone metabolism:
1. Tier 1, direct metabolism or bioenergetics: FDG-PET or absolute cere-
bral glucose metabolism; cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO2); ketone
metabolism; ATP- and phosphocreatine-related measures; and 31P-MRS bioener-
getics.
2. Tier 2, metabolic or neurochemical proxies: glutamate, Glx, GABA, lactate,
N-acetylaspartate, arterial-spin-labeling perfusion, and cerebral blood flow.
3. Tier 3, indirect energetic or landscape proxies: TSPO-PET, neuroinflam-
mation, glial or astrocytic markers, network-control energy, transition energy, and
statistical energy-landscape measures.
The attempted metabolism–AUC pairing was restricted to Tier 1. Quantitative
ASL cerebral blood flow was retained as a separate coverage column because quantified
flow that has not been normalized away can support calibrated metabolic inference,
but it was not treated as interchangeable with glucose consumption, oxygen con-
sumption, or phosphate turnover. Lactate, a Tier-2 quantity, enters the coverage
grid only through the ketone/lactate column, and only when measured dynamically
(Appendix F.6).
43


F.5
Extraction and harmonization rules
For each metabolism record, the extraction captured diagnosis and subgroup defini-
tion, healthy-control comparator, sample size, anatomical scope, acquisition modality,
physical quantity, absolute or normalized units, effect direction, reported or calculable
standardized effect, and source identifier. Hedges’ g or Cohen’s d was retained when
reported or directly calculable from group means, standard deviations, and sample
sizes. Peak statistics, voxelwise or clusterwise test statistics, t or F values without
sufficient conversion information, and qualitative statements were not treated as com-
parable diagnosis-level effect sizes. For each classifier record, the extraction captured
sample and site structure, feature modality, validation design, classification target,
AUC definition, and held-out AUC.
Candidate pairs were formed by matching the two halves on diagnosis and, where
the literature allowed, on population definition and stage of illness. A candidate pair
was eligible for quantitative analysis only if both halves referred to a compatible pop-
ulation, the metabolism half was a standardized effect for a direct energetic measure
expressed on the scale shared by the other pairs (the same physical quantity, unit con-
vention, and spatial scope, so that diagnoses could be ordered), and the classifier half
reported the required held-out diagnosis-versus-control AUC.

Coverage analysis
For Appendix Fig. F5A, each of 45 cells (nine conditions by five measurement classes)

F.9
Interpretation and implications for study design
This map identifies the evidence gaps that currently prevent a quantitative test of
the proposed association. The literature assembled in this targeted map did not
supply matched, commensurate estimates with which to test whether metabolic dis-
placement predicts distributional distinguishability or diagnostic AUC. Finding no
analyzable pair says nothing either way about the biological hypothesis itself. The most
46


direct test is a same-participant design: the same participants, a common diagnosis
and stage definition, calibrated energetic measurements, connectivity and functional
activity measured at compatible scales, controlled or recorded inputs, and held-out
classification evaluated within the same sampling frame. Diagnosis-level comparisons
of independently estimated summaries, of the kind this map was designed to sup-
port, remain admissible in principle; they require metabolic estimates on one scale,
preferably absolute, across diagnoses, and classifier samples matched to the metabolic
samples in population and stage, neither of which the present literature supplies.
Longitudinal perturbation designs would additionally permit estimation of whether
independently measured resource availability predicts the accessible range of distribu-
tional changes. As discussed in Section 4, this tests a constraint rather than requiring
expenditure and divergence to correlate in every condition.
