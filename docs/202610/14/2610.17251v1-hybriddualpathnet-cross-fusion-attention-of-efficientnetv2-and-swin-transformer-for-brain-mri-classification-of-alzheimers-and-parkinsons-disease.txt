Abstract —Separating Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Parkinson’s Disease (PD), and Healthy Controls (HC) on a single structural MRI is a genuinely awkward problem. The disease signatures overlap on the cortex, PD scans are always in short supply, and— the part most papers quietly skip—how the data is partitioned tends to decide the headline number more than the network does. We study HybridDualPathNet, which pairs EfficientNetV2-S (local texture) with Swin-Tiny (global context) and fuses them through a Cross-Fusion Attention (CFA) unit that decides, scan by scan, how much of each stream to trust, and we run it through two experiments. The first is the conventional one: train and test on the Kaggle APD-3-Class set alone under an image-level split, where the model reaches 98.25% accuracy. The second is stricter: one model trained and tested on Kaggle and ADNI together, 17,859 slices, under a per-source patient-wise split so no subject’s slices land on both sides. There the same network scores 73.4% accuracy and 0.880 macro AUC, and splitting the result by source exposes the real story—91.2% on Kaggle-style scans but only 59.2% on ADNI. Temperature scaling pulls expected calibration error from 0.130 to 0.015, split-conformal prediction gives 89.5% coverage, and multi-method saliency confirms the network looks at brain tissue rather than skull. The 25-point gap between the two experiments is, honestly, the most useful result here. 

Index Terms —Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, brain MRI, data leakage, patient-wise split, cross-dataset generaliza-tion, ADNI, cross-fusion attention, calibration, Grad-CAM++. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Neurodegenerative disease keeps adding to the load on health systems everywhere. Alzheimer’s is now diagnosed in upward of 55 million people [1], Parkinson’s in roughly ten million more [2], and both totals keep rising as the population ages. A T1-weighted structural scan is usually the first thing a neurologist reaches for: it is cheap, carries no ionizing dose, and tracks morphological change well enough to be worth reading. The catch is that AD and PD leave structural marks that look a lot alike, and radiologists themselves part ways on the borderline cases [3]. An automated reader that could pre-screen these scans would take real pressure off overstretched clinics, and deep networks routinely post accuracies in the high nineties on public benchmarks. We were, frankly, suspicious of those numbers, and this paper grew out of that suspicion. Two habits in the literature quietly inflate reported accuracy. First, the image-level split: MRI contains many adjacent slices per patient, and if a random split scatters them across train and test, a model can pass simply by recognizing a brain it has already seen. Second, single-dataset evaluation, which never asks whether a model tuned to one scanner survives contact with another. So we report two experiments back to back—the ordinary Kaggle-only setup first, then a leakage-controlled two-cohort setup— using the same network for both, so the reader can see exactly how much the protocol, not the model, is worth. Three familiar technical gaps sit underneath all of this. 

Gap-1 (Architecture). CNNs read fine local texture well but miss how distant regions relate; Vision Transformers capture that global context but blur local detail. No prior work on this benchmark fuses both through a mechanism that adapts the blend per scan. 

Gap-2 (Data). PD is badly under-represented—only 906 slices on Kaggle, and just 967 in our combined set against thousands of AD and CN. Plain oversampling invites memo-risation; undersampling throws away signal. 

Gap-3 (Calibration). Accuracy and F1 are usually where the reporting stops. For screening that is not enough: the dangerous case is a network that is wrong while sounding certain, so we treat calibrated probabilities as part of what the model has to deliver. Our contributions are as follows.  

> •

The CFA fusion unit, whose per-sample, per-dimension gate mixes the convolutional and transformer streams through 8-head attention in place of a static concate-nation, letting the network lean on local hippocampal texture for AD-like scans and on global context for PD-like ones without any hand-set rule, at a cost of under a million extra parameters.  

> •

A complete single-dataset (Kaggle) study —training, component ablation, a comparison against eight pub-lished methods, calibration, and Grad-CAM++ saliency— arXiv:2610.17251v1 [cs.CV] 12 Oct 2026 reaching 98.25% accuracy, reported first as the conven-tional image-level baseline that most prior work on this benchmark is measured against.  

> •

A leakage-controlled, two-cohort study on Kaggle and ADNI together (17,859 slices), split per source and by patient so that no subject’s slices cross partitions, with results reported by source so the cross-scanner domain gap is measured rather than quietly averaged away.  

> •

A direct comparison of the two experiments showing the same network—identical weights, loss, and training recipe—drops from 98.25% to 73.4% once leakage is removed, the clearest evidence here of how much single-dataset, image-level evaluation inflates the accuracies routinely reported on this task. II. R ELATED WORK 

For most of the past decade convolutional networks set the pace here. ResNet [4], DenseNet, and the EfficientNet fam-ily [5] land somewhere around 89–94% on AD/PD data [12], and EfficientNetV2 [6] tightened the speed–accuracy balance with its fused-MBConv stages. Their shared weakness is structural: convolutions relate nearby pixels well but struggle across a whole slice. Vision Transformers [7] and the Swin Transformer [8] close that gap with self-attention, though pure transformers are data-hungry and wobble on the small cohorts typical of neuroimaging—G¨ uven [11] still reached the mid-nineties with one. Hybrids that join a CNN and a transformer have become popular for exactly this reason: Hussain et al. [9] report about 97% with a CNN–Swin combination, and Velu and Jaisankar [10] 97.4% with an HDNN on APD-3-Class. Almost all of these, however, glue the two feature streams with fixed weights and report a single accuracy on a single dataset. The recurring problem is not the architecture but the protocol—imbalance is patched rather than measured, calibra-tion is ignored, and the patient-versus-image split often goes unstated. Guo et al. [15] and Angelopoulos and Bates [16] give us the tools to talk about confidence honestly, and Grad-CAM++ [14] lets us check where a model actually looks; we lean on all three. A quieter thread runs through this literature, and it concerns how these models are tested rather than how they are built. Surveys of deep learning in medical imaging have repeatedly warned that a large share of reported accuracies rest on splits that allow slices—or even augmentations—of the same patient to appear in both training and test [3], and that a model tuned on one cohort rarely transfers to another, because scanner, acquisition protocol, and population all shift the underlying image statistics. Cross-dataset generalization and domain adaptation [17] have grown into subfields of their own for exactly this reason: single-source accuracy turns out to be a weak predictor of how a system behaves in the wild. Public initiatives such as ADNI [18] were assembled in part to make this kind of multi-site scrutiny possible. Our two-cohort, patient-wise protocol sits squarely in this line of work—we are less interested in adding one more point of headline accuracy than in reporting a figure that survives the move to an unseen scanner, and in showing plainly how far the usual numbers fall when that move is finally made. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

This section describes the parts common to both experiments—the network, the augmentation and imbalance handling, and the training recipe. Only the dataset and the split change between Sections IV and V. 

A. HybridDualPathNet and Cross-Fusion Attention 

Each slice passes through two ImageNet-pretrained en-coders in parallel (Fig. 1). EfficientNetV2-S produces a 1280-d local descriptor FL; Swin-Tiny a 768-d global descriptor FG.Both are projected to a shared 512-d space with LayerNorm and GELU, giving L and G. Rather than concatenate, CFA lets the local stream query the global one. An 8-head attention block uses L as query and G as key and value, A =MHA( L, G, G ); a sigmoid gate then decides, per dimension and per scan, how far to lean toward the attended global signal versus the raw local one:

(2) The point of the gate is that an AD-leaning slice, where hippocampal texture matters, can pull toward the CNN, while a scan that needs wider context can pull toward the transformer—without us hard-coding the choice. A shal-low head ( 512 →256 →128 →3 with LayerNorm, GELU, and dropout of 0.35/0.175) reads the fused vector. During training, two auxiliary linear heads sit on the raw encoder outputs at 0.3 loss weight each, giving gradients a direct path into the backbones early on; they are dropped at inference. The model totals about 50.76 M parameters. 

B. Augmentation and Class Imbalance 

MRI varies across scanners and protocols, so we sim-ulate that on the fly with a 12-transform Albumenta-tions pipeline: geometric jitter (flips, ±20 ◦ rotation, shift-scale-rotate, ElasticTransform, GridDistortion), photomet-ric shifts (brightness/contrast, GaussNoise, GaussianBlur, CLAHE, RandomGamma), and CoarseDropout, which masks random patches so the network cannot lean on a single lucky region. A fresh combination is drawn every epoch. Validation and test use only resize and normalize; test-time augmentation adds mild rotation and brightness but never flips, which would corrupt left/right laterality. PD is the minority everywhere, so we fight the imbalance on two fronts: a WeightedRandomSampler keeps mini-batches roughly balanced without duplicating images, and a class-weighted focal loss [13] ( γ=2 .0; αAD =0 .843 , αCN =0 .717 , αPD =2 .383 )concentrates gradients on the hard, rare cases. 

C. Training Recipe 

The total loss is L = Lmain + 0 .3 Laux-local + 0 .3 Laux-global ,with label smoothing of 0.05. Optimization uses AdamW with a layer-wise rate— 3×10 −5 for the pretrained backbones, Fig. 1. HybridDualPathNet. Two pretrained encoders feed the Cross-Fusion Attention module, whose per-sample sigmoid gate γ blends local and global features before a shallow classifier. The auxiliary heads add deep supervision only while training. 

3×10 −4 for the fusion and head—so we nudge the ImageNet weights rather than overwrite them. A OneCycleLR schedule with 10% warm-up, weight decay 10 −4, FP16 mixed precision, batch size 16 with gradient accumulation of 4 (effective 64), and a fixed seed of 42 keep every run inside a 15 GB Colab T4 budget. We stop early on validation accuracy, average several randomized augmentation passes at test time, and apply temperature scaling fitted on the validation set. Every slice is resampled to 224 ×224 and standardised with the usual ImageNet channel statistics, because both backbones are initialised from ImageNet. IV. D. Calibration, Uncertainty, and Robustness 

Raw softmax confidence was mildly off, so we fit a single temperature on the validation set ( T =0 .60 ); this cut expected calibration error almost nine-fold, 0.130 to 0.015, with match-ing drops in MCE and Brier score (Table XI, Fig. 15). Monte-Carlo dropout (ten passes) gives 73.3% overall but 76.3% once we keep only the 90% most-confident predictions, so the uncertainty is meaningful. Split conformal prediction at α=0 .10 , averaged over twenty random calibration splits, returns 89.5% ± 0.8% coverage with an average set size of 1.49. Under three synthetic corruptions the same checkpoint degrades gently—from 73.5% clean down to 60.4% at the heaviest motion blur, around 67% under Gaussian noise, and 69–72% under brightness shifts—never approaching the 33% chance floor. 

E. Does It Look at the Brain? 

High accuracy on a leaky benchmark can hide a model that keys on skull or background, so we checked with Grad-CAM++, Integrated Gradients, and Occlusion together (Fig. 16). The three work at different scales, so their raw pixel overlap is low, but their region rankings correlate pos-itively and all three land on brain parenchyma rather than the skull rim or the black surround. As a sanity check on CFA itself, the learned gate behaves sensibly—its mean shifts by class (AD 0.37, CN 0.28, PD 0.17), confirming the fusion is adaptive rather than constant. VI. D ISCUSSION 

Placing the two experiments side by side is the sharpest part of this work. The Kaggle-only confusion matrix (Table V) is almost perfectly diagonal—17 errors in 972—while the combined one (Fig. 13) shows a heavy Control →AD block that appears only once ADNI’s unseen scans enter the test. The weights are the same; what changed is the split and the second cohort. That single contrast, and the 91.2% versus 59.2% source breakdown behind it, is exactly what an image-level single-dataset accuracy hides. A few mechanisms are worth naming. The CFA gate adapts between classes—higher for AD, where local hippocampal texture matters, lowest for PD—which is the behaviour it was designed for, and it beat plain concatenation by a clear margin in the Experiment I ablation. The imbalance strategy also clearly worked: in both experiments PD, the rarest class, ended up the best identified. But the honest headline is the domain gap. The model is genuinely strong inside the Kaggle distribution and genuinely weak on an unseen cohort, and the failure is specific—ADNI controls read as AD—rather than a general collapse, which points at intensity and scanner differences rather than the architecture. Read together with the calibration and conformal results, the practical reading is that this system is a plausible pre-screening aid within a known distribution, able to flag the cases it should not decide alone, but not something to point at an arbitrary new scanner. It assists a radiologist; it does not replace one. There is a methodological point here we want to make plainly. Nothing about the two experiments differs except the data scope and the split, so the 25-point drop is neither noise nor a weaker model—it is simply the cost of measuring honestly, and it is a cost the field mostly avoids paying. We would argue that reporting it is more useful to a clini-cian deciding whether to trust a tool than yet another near-perfect single-dataset accuracy. The calibration and conformal numbers make that argument concrete: because the model’s confidence is well-behaved after temperature scaling, and its prediction sets widen on the cases it finds hard, a deployment could route the low-confidence or wide-set scans—many of which are exactly the ADNI controls it struggles with—to a specialist while clearing the confident majority automatically. That selective behaviour, rather than a headline accuracy, is what would let the system sit safely inside a real workflow. It is also worth noting that the per-class story is stable across both experiments: PD is the easiest class in each, and the AD– Control pair is always the pivot, so the architecture’s strengths and weaknesses are consistent even as the absolute numbers move—the split changes how well the model generalises, not what it finds hard. 

Limitations. The model works on 2D axial slices and cannot see volumetric biomarkers; ADNI’s subject pool is small and has no PD; Kaggle lacks subject IDs; and there is no prospective clinical validation yet. VII. F UTURE SCOPE 

The obvious next step is to close the gap rather than merely report it. Because the pipeline is already source-aware and supports leave-one-dataset-out evaluation, domain-adversarial training [17] and ComBat-style intensity harmonization can be measured directly against the 59.2% ADNI baseline es-tablished here. Beyond that we would like to move from 2D slices to 3D volumes with a volumetric backbone [19], extend the CFA idea to extra modalities such as DTI and PET where paired data allows, add cohorts like PPMI and OASIS, and push the uncertainty work toward deep ensembles and concept-level explanations [20]. Two of these matter more than the rest. Closing the Kaggle-to-ADNI gap is the immediate priority, and because our splitter already keeps the sources separate, a leave-one-dataset-out protocol gives a clean way to check whether an adaptation method genuinely transfers rather than just memorising a second distribution. Adding PD scans to the ADNI side—or bringing in a third cohort such as PPMI that actually contains PD—would let us test whether the near-perfect PD result survives outside Kaggle, which the present data simply cannot tell us. Further out, prospective validation, where the model reads scans as they arrive in a clinic and radiologist agreement serves as the reference, is the only thing that would move this work from a benchmark result toward something usable; we treat every direction above as a step in that direction rather than an end in itself. A different lever on the PD shortage is to make more of it. Conditional GANs [ ?] or diffusion models [ ?] can synthesise plausible PD-like slices, and while we are wary of training on generated data—it is easy to reproduce the texture of a class without reproducing its pathology—a carefully gated pipeline, in which synthetic scans are screened by fidelity metrics and a neuroradiologist before they ever enter training, could ease a scarcity that no amount of resampling truly cures. In a similar spirit, self-supervised pretraining [21] on the large pool of unlabelled MRI that hospitals already hold would give the encoders a starting point grounded in medical images rather than natural photographs. We suspect that would help most exactly where the model is weakest today—on the unfamiliar ADNI distribution—since the features learned that way are less tied to any one scanner’s intensity profile. Two further directions look further out but matter for real deployment. Federated learning would let several hospitals improve the model jointly, especially on the minority PD class, without any of them sharing raw patient scans, which sidesteps the privacy and governance hurdles that usually stall multi-site medical AI before it starts. And because neurodegeneration is a process rather than a snapshot, a longitudinal variant trained on serial scans from the same patient could watch for accelerating atrophy and flag change earlier than any single-timepoint classifier can—which is where the clinical value of imaging is highest, well before symptoms are obvious. Each of these would also give the calibration and uncertainty machinery already in place something more useful to act on, since a confidence estimate is worth far more when it is attached to a trajectory than to a lone slice. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We did not set out to invent a new module; we set out to measure an existing one honestly. Under the ordinary Kaggle-only, image-level protocol HybridDualPathNet reaches 98.25%—in line with the best published results—but under a per-source patient-wise split across Kaggle and ADNI the same network scores a defensible 73.4%, with well-behaved calibration and uncertainty and saliency that sits on anatomy rather than artefacts. The number that matters is the 91.2% versus 59.2% split between the two cohorts: an explicit, reproducible measure of how far the model generalizes, and a gap the field usually hides behind a single average. Reporting it, rather than the 98.25% the same network can produce under a leaky split, is the point of the paper. More broadly, we think the two-experiment format is itself a small contribution. Running one network under a leaky and a leak-free protocol, and publishing both side by side, turns the usual single accuracy into a measurement of how far that accuracy can be trusted—which is arguably the number a clinician actually needs. The entire pipeline runs on a single free-tier GPU with openly available data, so reproducing or extending either experiment costs nothing but time. For a result whose central claim is about honest evaluation, keeping the barrier to reproduction that low feels like the right bar to set. What we are ultimately left with is a model that is candid about its own limits. Inside a known scanner distribution it performs at a level competitive with the published state of the art, and it arrives with the calibration, uncertainty, and saliency evidence a careful reviewer or clinician would reasonably ask for before trusting it at all. Step outside that distribution and it degrades—but, importantly, it says so, both in the source-stratified accuracy and in the conformal sets that widen on the cases it cannot resolve. A system that surfaces its own uncertainty this plainly is, to our minds, closer to clinically useful than one that quotes a higher single number and quietly buries the rest. If this study nudges even a little toward reporting brain-MRI classifiers under leakage-free, multi-cohort protocols as a matter of course, we would consider that a more durable contribution than the architecture itself.
