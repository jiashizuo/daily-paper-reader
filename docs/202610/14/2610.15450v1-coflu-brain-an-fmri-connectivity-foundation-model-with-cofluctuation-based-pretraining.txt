## Abstract 

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) commonly characterizes large-scale brain organization through functional connectivity (FC). How-ever, an observed FC matrix is not a fixed brain graph but a statistical estimate determined by the finite BOLD frames used to compute it. Con-ventional FC-based approaches typically represent each scan with a single Pearson-correlation matrix, leaving frame-resolved variations in regional interactions unexploited. To address this limitation, we introduce CoFlu-Brain, which organizes framewise cofluctuation observations into multiple retained and dropped views, each summarized as an FC matrix. The model learns connectivity structure reproducible across retained views and pre-dicts the representations of the corresponding dropped views, while adopt-ing SIGReg from LeJEPA to prevent representation collapse. We pretrained CoFlu-Brain on 13,444 subjects from eight dataset groups and evaluated its representations via frozen-encoder linear probing on seven classification and two age-prediction tasks across seven datasets. CoFlu-Brain showed strong transfer across demographic, developmental, and clinical prediction tasks, outperforming the evaluated released models on most tasks. Ran-dom frame partitioning improved aggregate transfer, while the complete objective achieved the strongest overall performance. Code is available. 

## 1 Introduction 

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) provides a non-invasive view of large-scale functional brain organization and its relationships with cognition, behavior, and disease. As large multi-site fMRI collections continue to grow, self-supervised pretraining offers a path toward transferable brain representations without requiring downstream task labels during pretraining (Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024; Gijsen et al., 2026). A common route from fMRI measurements to brain-network representations is to parcellate the brain into regions of interest (ROIs), extract a regional BOLD time series from each ROI, and estimate the statistical dependence between regional signals. Pearson correlation organizes these pairwise dependencies into a compact and interpretable functional connectivity (FC) matrix. By explicitly representing interregional functional coupling, FC has become a widely used input for brain-network modeling and connectome-based pretraining (Kan et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2026a). An observed Pearson-FC matrix, however, is not a sampling-independent brain graph but a scan-level statistical estimate formed from a finite BOLD sequence. Its value can vary with the frames included in the estimation, such that different frame compositions produce non-identical but statistically related FC estimates. 1arXiv:2610.15450v1 [cs.NE] 11 Oct 2026 Preprint

Conventional FC aggregates all frames into a single connectivity estimate, whereas CoFlu-Brain uses different compositions of framewise cofluctuation observations to construct multiple FC views. Existing FC representation models typically operate on a single Pearson-FC matrix or graph for each scan (Kan et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2026a), leaving frame-resolved variation unused during representation learning. Framewise cofluctuation provides a finer-grained description of regional interactions by characterizing the joint standardized activity of regional pairs at individual frames (Zamani Esfahlani et al., 2020; Faskowitz et al., 2020). This perspec-tive makes it possible to construct multiple training views from different compositions of framewise observations. This gap raises a central question: can different compositions of framewise cofluctuation observations provide self-supervision for learning stable and transferable connectivity rep-resentations? We hypothesize that retained frame sets sampled from the same segment provide different views of subject-specific cofluctuation structure. Although the resulting FC views may differ numerically, their shared connectivity structure should remain repro-ducibly inferable. Moreover, a retained view should contain sufficient information to predict the representation of the corresponding non-overlapping dropped view. The former encour-ages consistency across retained views, whereas the latter captures predictive relationships between retained and dropped observations. Motivated by this gap, we propose CoFlu-Brain , a cofluctuation-based approach to self-supervised FC pretraining. For each fMRI segment, CoFlu-Brain repeatedly partitions the frames into retained and dropped sets, organizes their framewise cofluctuation observations into separate FC views, and uses the dropped-view representations as prediction targets. Its joint-embedding objective promotes consistent representations across retained views while requiring each retained representation to predict its corresponding dropped-view represen-tation in latent space. We evaluate the resulting representations through multi-dataset pretraining, frozen transfer, and systematic ablations. Our contributions are: 1. We propose a cofluctuation-based FC pretraining approach that constructs retained and dropped views from framewise observations and couples consistency across retained views with retained-to-dropped latent prediction. 2. We develop CoFlu-Brain , an FC foundation model pretrained on multi-cohort fMRI data, and demonstrate strong frozen-transfer performance across demographic, developmental, and clinical downstream tasks. 3. We conduct a systematic empirical analysis across nine downstream tasks, ex-amining the contributions of the learning objectives and the effects of data coverage, branch count, and temporal sampling, while assessing seed robustness for the pri-mary and temporal-sampling comparisons. 2Preprint 

## 2 Related Work 

fMRI representation models differ first in what they model. Time-series methods recon-struct masked BOLD signals, predict masked latent targets, or align representations across temporal views (Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024; Gijsen et al., 2026). They preserve temporal dynamics, but interregional connectivity remains an implicit property of the learned activity representation. Voxel-level models instead operate directly on 4D fMRI volumes (Wang et al., 2026b; 2025; Xia et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026a). They retain fine-grained spatial information without relying on a predefined atlas, but require high-dimensional inputs and do not use connectivity as the primary modeling unit. FC models represent each scan as a connectivity matrix or graph, directly encoding interre-gional coupling (Kawahara et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021; Kan et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2026a;b). Most treat the resulting Pearson-FC matrix as the object to encode, reconstruct, augment, or align. This overlooks that FC is a finite-sample estimate whose values depend on its supporting frames (Noble et al., 2019; Zamani Esfahlani et al., 2020; Faskowitz et al., 2020). Dynamic-FC methods instead model temporal variation across win-dows or graph sequences (Hutchison et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2021), but address evolving connectivity states rather than reproducibility across alternative estimates. CoFlu-Brain retains the interpretability of FC modeling while extending its learning unit from one scan-level estimate to multiple related estimates. These views arise from comple-mentary frame observations and preserve a direct relationship to FC estimation, promoting representations stable across frame compositions and predictive of held-out observations. Self-supervised methods also differ in how they define their learning signal. Masked au-toencoding reconstructs omitted input content, whereas JEPA predicts masked targets in latent space (He et al., 2022; Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2026a; Dong et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2023). Joint-embedding methods such as BYOL and DINO instead align representations across views, while VICReg and SIGReg regularize the fea-ture distribution against collapse (Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Bardes et al., 2022; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025). Other directions include metadata-conditioned diffusion pretraining across brain states (Xia et al., 2026a), organizing pretraining domains and downstream transfer through measured learning relations (Xia et al., 2026c), and jointly examining data size, model size, and training duration (Ye et al., 2026). These studies complement the design of FC-specific training views explored here. CoFlu-Brain integrates these principles through FC-specific view relations. Consistency across retained estimates promotes stability under frame resampling; prediction from re-tained to complementary dropped estimates captures information predictive of held-out observations; and SIGReg maintains representation diversity. Together, these components use the finite-sample structure of FC to define which views should agree and which comple-mentary evidence should be predicted.

## 4 Results 

4.1 Experimental setup 

CoFlu-Brain was pretrained on 13,444 subjects from eight dataset groups: CHCP, HCP, ABCD, ABCC, HBN, PNC, MDD, and AOMIC PIOP1/2 (Ge et al., 2023; Van Essen et al., 2013; Casey et al., 2018; Feczko et al., 2021; Alexander et al., 2017; Satterthwaite et al., 2014; Yan et al., 2019; Snoek et al., 2021). All inputs were represented as Schaefer-400 cortical time series (Schaefer et al., 2018), and 200-frame segments were used for pretraining. Downstream evaluation comprised two in-cohort tasks from HCP and ABCD, whose unlabeled scans contributed to pretraining, and seven out-of-cohort tasks from SALD, PPMI, ADNI, ABIDE, and ADHD200, which were excluded from pretraining (Van Essen et al., 2013; Casey et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2018; Marek et al., 2011; Weiner et al., 2010; Di Martino et al., 2014; ADHD-200 Consortium, 2012). No downstream labels were used during pretraining. Frozen representations were evaluated with logistic regression for classification and ridge regression for age prediction. For age prediction, nMAE was computed after standardizing the target using the training-fold mean and population standard deviation. Cross-dataset ranks used weighted F1 for classification and nMAE for age prediction. Endpoint ranks were averaged within each cohort, and the dispersion of method mean ranks was evaluated using two million method-label permutations within cohort blocks. Full details are provided in Appendix A. 

4.2 Main Results 

We evaluated CoFlu-Brain against four released fMRI representation models: BrainGFM, BrainMASS, BrainLM, and the Large Connectome Model (Wei et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2024; Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2026b). CoFlu-Brain led eight of nine tasks and achieved the best rank across the seven evaluation cohorts (1.14; Table 1).

4.4.1 Random versus ordered frame partitioning 

To compare temporal sampling strategies, we evaluated random and ordered partitions at matched retained-frame ratios of 0.4 –0.7 . Random partitioning sampled retained frames throughout the sequence and used the complement as the dropped view; ordered partitioning used the first 𝑘 and remaining 𝑇 − 𝑘 frames. Both used identical standardization, FC construction, architecture, and objective, differing only in frame indices. This comparison assesses the empirical effect of the two frame-partitioning strategies on downstream transfer. On seed-averaged performance, random partitioning outperformed ordered partitioning across all seven evaluation cohorts (exact sign test, 𝑝 = 0.016 ; Figure 4).

## 5 Discussion 

CoFlu-Brain shows that relationships among FC estimates supported by different frame com-positions provide useful self-supervision. Its representations transferred across demographic, developmental, and clinical tasks, while random partitioning improved aggregate transfer. The complete objective combines retained-view consistency, dropped-view prediction, and distributional regularization as complementary signals. 

Limitations and future directions. CoFlu-Brain uses the framewise cofluctuation de-composition of Pearson connectivity to construct multiple FC estimates with explicitly con-trolled frame support. Because each view aggregates the selected framewise products into a subset-specific FC matrix, the current model does not preserve the timing, order, or am-plitude of individual cofluctuation events. Its scope is instead to learn from structured relationships among finite-sample FC estimates: reproducibility across retained-frame com-positions and prediction of complementary held-out estimates. Future work could com-bine this estimate-level objective with order-aware or edge-time-series encoders to capture transient dynamics. Biological validation, causal analysis, and integration with voxel-level features remain important directions beyond the predictive evaluations considered here.

## A Experimental Details 

A.1 Datasets and downstream tasks 

Pretraining datasets. The pretraining corpus contained 13,444 usable subjects from eight dataset groups. Only fMRI inputs, without downstream labels, were used during pretraining. 

Preprocessing. We started from cohort-specific preprocessed derivatives: the HCP min-imal preprocessing pipeline for HCP and CHCP, ABCD-HCP derivatives for ABCD and ABCC, fMRIPrep derivatives for AOMIC PIOP1/2, and available MNI-space preprocessed derivatives for HBN, PNC, and the multisite MDD data. Thus, low-level operations, in-cluding motion correction and any nuisance-signal handling, were inherited from the corre-sponding source pipeline rather than re-estimated with a single cross-cohort pipeline. We then applied a common input harmonization procedure: all fMRI volumes were represented at 2-mm isotropic resolution, and only datasets with a source TR different from 0.72 s were temporally interpolated to 0.72 s using cubic B-spline interpolation. Regional signals were extracted using the Schaefer-400 atlas, and each regional time series was Z-standardized along the temporal dimension. Files with exactly 200 frames were used directly; during pre-training, contiguous 200-frame windows were sampled from longer series, whereas sequences shorter than 200 frames were excluded without zero-padding. 

Chinese Human Connectome Project (CHCP). CHCP contributed healthy-adult resting-state scans acquired with multiple phase-encoding directions (Ge et al., 2023). 

Human Connectome Project (HCP). HCP contributed young-adult resting-state scans to pretraining (Van Essen et al., 2013). It also provided an internal downstream task for sex classification; the original data partitions remained separated by subject. 

Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD). ABCD contributed pediatric resting-state scans to pretraining (Casey et al., 2018). A separate internal downstream eval-uation predicted sex, with participant identities kept disjoint across the original partitions. 

ABCC Data Share. ABCC contributed a large pediatric imaging collection (Feczko et al., 2021). Subjects overlapping with ABCD were removed from ABCC before construct-ing the pretraining manifest. 

Healthy Brain Network (HBN). HBN contributed pediatric and adolescent scans span-ning a broad range of mental-health and learning characteristics (Alexander et al., 2017). 

Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC). PNC contributed neurodevelop-mental resting-state scans from youth and young adults (Satterthwaite et al., 2014). 

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). The multisite MDD collection contributed resting-state scans from participants with recurrent depression and controls (Yan et al., 2019). 

Amsterdam Open MRI Collection (AOMIC). AOMIC PIOP1 and PIOP2 con-tributed young-adult resting-state scans (Snoek et al., 2021). 

Downstream datasets and tasks. Seven datasets provided nine downstream tasks. HCP and ABCD were treated as in-cohort evaluations because their unlabeled scans con-tributed to pretraining; no downstream labels were used. The remaining cohorts were excluded from the pretraining mixture and provided out-of-cohort transfer evaluations. 

Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI). PPMI was used for three-class diagnostic-group classification (Marek et al., 2011). 15 Preprint 

Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). ADNI provided two binary classification tasks: Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment (Weiner et al., 2010). 

Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE). ABIDE was used for autism-spectrum-disorder classification (Di Martino et al., 2014). ABIDE and ABIDE2 were both excluded from pretraining. 

ADHD-200. ADHD-200 provided ADHD diagnosis classification and age prediction (ADHD-200 Consortium, 2012). The complete cohort was excluded from pretraining. 

Southwest University Adult Lifespan Dataset (SALD). SALD was used for lifespan age prediction and was excluded from pretraining (Wei et al., 2018). A.2 Baseline models 

The linear-probing comparison used four ROI-based fMRI representation models. Each encoder was kept frozen, and its scan-level representation was evaluated with the same task-specific linear-probe family used for CoFlu-Brain. Each model used its native input parcellation and feature extraction pathway. 

BrainGFM. BrainGFM pretrains a shared graph encoder across multiple parcellations us-ing graph contrastive learning and masked graph autoencoding (Wei et al., 2026a). We used the authors’ released pretrained checkpoint with Schaefer-100 inputs (Schaefer et al., 2018). Raw Pearson-FC rows served as node features, and FC values above 0.3 defined the graph adjacency. The encoder’s graph-level output was used as the frozen scan representation. 

BrainMASS. BrainMASS constructs augmented FC views by dropping BOLD observa-tions and learns with masked-ROI modeling and latent representation alignment (Yang et al., 2024). In CoFlu-Brain, each partition forms a complementary retained–dropped FC pair; multiple retained estimates are aligned, and each paired dropped-view representation is pre-dicted. We used the authors’ released Schaefer-100 checkpoint (Schaefer et al., 2018) and released downstream feature path: Transformer-encoded FC rows followed by dimensionality reduction and flattening. 

BrainLM. BrainLM is a masked-autoencoding foundation model for regional fMRI time series (Ortega Caro et al., 2024). We used the released 111M-parameter ViT-MAE check-point with its native 424-region input. The concatenation of the final class token and mean patch representation was used as the frozen scan feature. 

Large Connectome Model. The Large Connectome Model (LCM) learns connectome representations through multitask brain–environment interactions (Wei et al., 2026b). We used the released AAL-116 backbone and decoder checkpoints (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002), 16 Preprint computed raw Pearson FC following the released input convention, and mean-pooled the final decoder-query states to obtain a frozen scan representation.
