Abstract —Accurate assessment of Alzheimer’s disease stages from MRI is important for understanding disease progression and supporting the development of computer-aided analysis tools. Deep learning models have shown promising results for Alzheimer’s disease classification from MRI. However, when eval-uated on single datasets, it is difficult to determine whether the observed performance reflects consistent architectural behavior or dataset-specific effects. In this work, we present a multi-dataset benchmarking framework with standardized preprocessing and evaluation. We compare five architectures: ResNet-50, ReXNet-150, Swin Transformer, ViT-Base, and ViT-Large. The models are evaluated across three MRI datasets representing severe class imbalance, train-test distribution mismatch, and large-scale augmentation. The results show broadly consistent performance patterns across datasets, with ViT-Large achieving the best performance on all three datasets and reaching up to 99.84% ac-curacy and 99.86% macro F1. Performance differences between architectures are most pronounced under distribution mismatch and considerably smaller in the augmented-data setting. Across datasets, lower class-level performance is observed primarily among adjacent early-stage categories, particularly Very Mild and Mild impairment. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating deep learning architectures across multiple data conditions rather than relying on single-dataset performance. 

Index Terms —Alzheimer’s disease, MRI classification, deep learning, multi-dataset benchmarking, distribution mismatch, class imbalance, transformer models, convolutional neural net-works 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegen-erative disorder that affects millions of people worldwide and places a growing burden on healthcare systems [1], [2]. Structural MRI provides a non-invasive way to detect disease-related changes in the brain [2]. Accurate staging is important for disease assessment. In MRI-based classification tasks, deep learning models have demonstrated great accuracy, frequently above 95% on benchmark datasets [1]. However, these results are often reported on a single dataset. As a result, it remains difficult to determine whether observed performance reflects consistent model behavior or dataset-specific effects. Similar concerns have been observed in external validation studies of artificial intelligence (AI) systems in radiology [3], [4]. It is difficult to tell whether differences in performance between architectures are consistent across diverse data situations. MRI datasets have different sizes, class distributions and conventional MRI designs. Synthetic balancing and augmen-tation can change the properties of the data and cause distri-butional shifts. However, real-world datasets are often imbal-anced [5], [6], [7]. Transformer based designs use attention processes for capturing larger dependencies, while convolu-tional models are based on local spatial inductive biases [8]. Architectural choices may influence the way models respond to changes in distribution, imbalance, and size of data. The classification task is made more difficult by the char-acteristics of Alzheimer’s disease. The stages of the disease’s progression range from Very Mild to Moderate impairment. Even experts may find it challenging to identify the subtle structural changes that characterize early stages [9], [10]. Consequently, classification difficulty is not uniform across classes, and adjacent early-stage categories may be particularly difficult to distinguish. Systematic comparisons across multiple datasets are still limited, despite the growing interest in deep learning for classifying Alzheimer’s MRI. Thus, it is not clear whether the relative ranking of model performance is consistent across datasets or how much they are sensitive to factors such as train-test distribution mismatch, data augmentation, and class imbalance. This gap leads to the following research question: 

Are performance patterns of CNNs and vision trans-formers for Alzheimer’s MRI classification con-sistent across datasets with different distributional properties, and how are these patterns affected by dataset-specific characteristics? 

In order to address this question, we present a multi-dataset benchmarking approach that compares transformer-based and convolutional models under standardized preprocessing and evaluation. To facilitate efficient training in each context, the framework incorporates optimization parameters specific to datasets and architectures. We evaluate five benchmark ar-chitectures: ViT-Base, ViT-Large, Swin Transformer, ReXNet-150 and ResNet-50. We assess the models across three datasets, large-scale augmentation, synthetic balancing with train-test distribution mismatch and severe class imbalance. This setting enables us to compare the trends in performance across quite different data conditions in a particular evaluation framework. We use this framework to study how architectural design, model capacity, and dataset characteristics are associated with classification performance. In particular, we examine changes arXiv:2610.33416v1 [cs.CV] 24 Oct 2026 in model ranking, sensitivity to distribution mismatch, and class-level performance across disease stages. The contributions of this work are:  

> •

We present a multi-dataset benchmarking methodology with standardized preprocessing and evaluation for sys-tematically comparing deep learning architectures under different data conditions, including class imbalance, train-test distribution mismatch, and large-scale augmentation.  

> •

We show that model performance patterns are broadly consistent across the evaluated datasets, with ViT-Large achieving the strongest overall performance on all three datasets and Swin Transformer consistently ranking among the top models.  

> •

We analyze how the magnitude of performance differ-ences changes across dataset conditions and show that architecture-level differences become more pronounced under the distribution-mismatch setting.  

> •

We identify adjacent early-stage categories, particularly Very Mild and Mild impairment, as the classes with lower and more variable class-level performance across the evaluated models and datasets. Deep learning has become a widely used approach for clas-sifying Alzheimer’s disease (AD) from structural MRI, with advances in both convolutional and transformer-based models. Early work demonstrated that 3D CNNs can classify AD from a single MRI scan under controlled conditions [2]. Later studies highlighted an important limitation of this literature: results are often difficult to compare because of differences in preprocessing, data splits, and evaluation protocols [1]. Recent reviews also show increasing interest in comparing CNN and transformer-based approaches for Alzheimer’s disease classification from MRI [23]. Standard CNN backbones such as ResNet and ReXNet remain useful baselines because of their spatial inductive bias and computational efficiency [11], [12]. Transformer-based models represent a different modeling paradigm, employing attention to capture long-range depen-dencies. Vision Transformers (ViT) performed well at scale, and Swin Transformer introduced hierarchical attention with better computational efficiency [8], [13]. Training strategies for data-efficient transformers have been explored to improve optimization when large-scale labeled data are limited [24]. CNN-transformer and transformer-based architectures have been explored in the AD domain to capture local anatom-ical structure and broader spatial relationships [16], [15]. Recently, transformer-based attention mechanisms have also been used for 3D brain MRI classification [21]. Although these approaches are promising, many studies evaluate a limited number of architectures on individual datasets which makes it difficult to determine whether reported improvements are due to the benefits of the architecture or the effects of the dataset. These concerns become particularly important when model behavior is compared across datasets or evaluated on external cohorts. Previous studies have shown that strong internal performance may not translate to independent populations. Bron et al. [14] reported reduced performance under cross-cohort evaluation, while Song et al. [22] emphasized the importance of multi-cohort, multi-vendor, and multi-protocol validation. More broadly, medical imaging studies associate generalization failures with dataset shift caused by differences in acquisition protocols, population characteristics, and hidden biases [18], [19], [6]. In MRI, scanner-specific characteristics can further affect model performance across sites [25], [17], [7]. Data construction and augmentation can also influence model evaluation. Generative adversarial networks provide a general framework for generating synthetic data [5], and synthetic samples are often used to expand training data or reduce class imbalance. However, synthetic balancing and heavy augmentation can alter the training distribution and may not fully represent real clinical conditions. Data leakage is another important concern in MRI classification, particularly when related or visually similar samples occur across training and testing splits, which can inflate reported performance [3]. In addition, differences in preprocessing, imbalance handling, optimization, and training strategies can complicate perfor-mance comparisons across studies [1], [8], [24]. For a clearer comparison among existing approaches, Table I summarizes representative studies in terms of datasets, models, and key findings. Although prior studies have evaluated CNN, transformer, and hybrid models for Alzheimer’s MRI classification, differ-ences in datasets, preprocessing, training settings, and evalua-tion protocols limit direct comparison across studies. Existing work has also focused largely on reporting performance within individual datasets or on external validation of a particular model, leaving comparatively less evidence on how the relative behavior of multiple architectures changes across different dataset conditions. We therefore evaluate representative CNN and transformer architectures across three datasets using con-sistent preprocessing and evaluation, while adapting training strategies to the characteristics of each dataset. This design allows us to examine whether architecture rankings and class-level performance patterns remain broadly consistent under severe class imbalance, train-test distribution mismatch, and large-scale augmentation. 1) Dataset I: Alzheimer MRI Disease Classification Dataset: This dataset, available on Hugging Face [26], con-tains brain MRI images labelled by four diagnostic categories: Non Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. The original dataset consists of 5,120 training images and 1,280 test images, for a total of 6,400 samples. The dataset has a highly imbalanced class distribution, with Non Demented images forming the majority and only 64 Moderate Demented samples in total (46 train, 9 validation, and 9 test). This extreme imbalance provides a challenging class-imbalanced setting for model evaluation.

3) Dataset III: Augmented Alzheimer MRI Dataset: This Kaggle dataset [28] contains Alzheimer’s MRI images in four classes: Non Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. It includes original and augmented images generated from an existing Alzheimer’s MRI dataset. In this study, only the augmented images are used. Because these samples are generated from a small num-ber of original images, they may introduce redundancy across dataset splits and inflate performance if highly similar samples appear in both training and testing partitions [29], [30]. Thus, Dataset III results should be interpreted as augmented-data performance rather than evidence of clinical generalization. After stratified partitioning, the dataset contains 23,788 train-ing, 5,098 validation, and 5,098 test images, totaling 33,984 samples. Dataset III is much larger than Dataset I and has mod-erate class imbalance, with similar class proportions across splits.

C. MRI Data Preparation 

All MRI images are processed with a standardized pipeline to ensure consistent input representation across datasets and ar-chitectures. The images are scaled to 224 ×224 pixels to match the requirements of the pretrained model, converted to PyTorch tensors, and scaled from [0 , 255] to [0 , 1] . The images are normalized with ImageNet channel-wise mean and standard deviation to align with the pretrained feature distributions and allow efficient transfer learning. MRI images are grayscale, so each image is replicated in three channels to match the pretrained models’ input format. This preprocessing pipeline reduces input-related variability when comparing architectural and training configurations. Data augmentation is only performed on the training set to improve generalization and reduce overfitting. The augmen-tation pipeline incorporates random cropping, horizontal and vertical flipping, random rotations, color jitter, grayscale con-version, and RandAugment to introduce spatial and intensity-based variability. Although MRI images are grayscale, color jitter and grayscale conversion are retained in the implemented pipeline after channel replication, where they function as intensity-level perturbations rather than true color transforma-tions. Validation and test images are processed deterministi-cally only to ensure consistent and reproducible evaluation. The complete pipeline for preprocessing, augmentation, model training and evaluation is represented in Figure 2. The figure illustrates how the three MRI datasets are first standard-ized through resizing, channel conversion, normalization, and training-only augmentation, followed by model fine-tuning and evaluation across the five benchmark architectures. A. Benchmark Architectures 

We evaluate five representative architectures: ResNet-50, ReXNet-150, Swin Transformer, ViT-Base, and ViT-Large. These models cover both convolutional and transformer-based approaches, enabling comparison between local spatial feature extraction and global attention-based modeling for Alzheimer’s disease MRI classification. They also differ in depth and parameter size, allowing us to examine perfor-mance patterns associated with model capacity. This selection provides a balanced benchmark of standard CNNs, efficient CNNs, hierarchical transformers, and Vision Transformer vari-ants. Their key architectural characteristics are summarized in Table IV. 

1) ResNet-50: ResNet-50 [11] is a deep convolutional neu-ral network that uses residual shortcut connections to improve gradient flow and reduce the vanishing-gradient problem in very deep models. Instead of learning a direct mapping, each residual block learns a residual function, which makes optimization more stable and allows the network to benefit from increased depth. The architecture consists of an initial convolutional stem followed by four stages of bottleneck residual blocks, where each block uses 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and 

1 × 1 convolutions to reduce, process, and restore feature di-mensions efficiently. ResNet-50 has also been applied directly to Alzheimer’s MRI classification, supporting its use as a relevant CNN baseline for this task [35]. In this study, ResNet-50 is used as the main CNN reference model because its residual design provides a widely adopted and robust baseline for comparing convolutional feature learning with transformer-based architectures in MRI classification. 2) ReXNet-150: ReXNet-150 [12] is an efficient convolu-tional architecture that aims at improving the representational capacity by reducing channel bottlenecks in CNN feature maps. The model progressively expands the channels’ size along the layers, so the feature representations are not ex-cessively compressed in the forward propagation. This design is motivated by the observation that narrow intermediate rep-resentations may limit the information flow and reduce the ex-pressive power of lightweight CNNs. ReXNet is also designed with efficient-CNN design principles similar to MobileNet-style architectures, but with a special focus on reducing the representational bottlenecks through progressive channel expansion [36], [37]. In this work, we use ReXNet-150 as an efficient CNN baseline to evaluate if better channel allocation and parameter-efficient convolutional design can bring benefits for MRI-based Alzheimer’s disease classification. 

3) Swin Transformer: The Swin Transformer [13] is a hierarchical vision transformer designed to improve the ef-ficiency of standard self-attention. Instead of computing at-tention globally over all image patches, it restricts self-attention to non-overlapping local windows, which substan-tially reduces computational complexity. To allow information exchange across neighboring windows, Swin introduces ashifted-window mechanism in which the window partition is shifted between successive layers. This enables cross-window interactions while preserving efficient local attention. The architecture also progressively reduces spatial resolution and increases feature dimensionality, producing a multi-scale rep-resentation similar to convolutional feature hierarchies. Such hierarchical and window-based modeling has made Swin-based architectures useful in medical image analysis, including 3D medical representation learning and MRI-based segmenta-tion tasks [38], [39]. In Alzheimer’s disease analysis, Swin-based hybrid models have also been explored for structural MRI diagnosis [16]. In this study, the Swin Transformer is included to evaluate whether hierarchical attention can capture both local anatomical variations and broader structural context in MRI-based Alzheimer’s disease classification. 

4) Vision Transformer (ViT-Base): The Vision Transformer (ViT) [8] encodes an image as a sequence of fixed-size patches, instead of convolutional feature maps. The input image in ViT-Base is split into 16 × 16 non-overlapping patches, which are then flattened, linearly projected to embedding vectors and the positional encodings are added to them. The resulting patch sequence is then processed by stacked transformer encoder blocks consisting of multi-head self-attention and feed-forward layers. This design enables each patch token to attend to other parts of the image during representation learning. This global attention at the patch level is beneficial for stage classification as the structural difference between the Alzheimer stages can be present in many brain regions instead of one localized region. Recently, ViT-based feature extraction has also been used in Alzheimer’s MRI studies for 3D MRI classification [21]. The ViT-Base has 12 encoder layers, 12 attention heads and 86M parameters. In this study, it serves as the baseline transformer model for assessing global self-attention in MRI-based Alzheimer’s disease classification. 

5) Vision Transformer (ViT-Large): ViT-Large [8] follows the same patch-based transformer design as ViT-Base but increases model capacity substantially. It uses 24 encoder lay-ers, 16 attention heads, and approximately 307M parameters, compared with 12 encoder layers, 12 attention heads, and approximately 86M parameters in ViT-Base. Larger vision transformers can learn more expressive patch-level interac-tions, and prior work on scaling vision transformers shows that increasing model size, data, and compute can improve visual representation quality [40]. In the context of Alzheimer’s MRI classification, this larger capacity may help capture subtle stage-related structural differences distributed across brain regions. In this study, comparing ViT-Large with ViT-Base allows us to assess whether increased transformer capacity improves classification performance under different dataset conditions.

C. Loss Function and Optimization 

All models are trained using cross-entropy loss with label smoothing to improve calibration and reduce overconfident predictions [43]. A smoothing value of 0.02 is used for Dataset I, while 0.1 is used for Datasets II and III to provide stronger regularization for the larger training sets. Class imbalance is handled with dataset-specific strategies. For Dataset I, which has severe imbalance, we use a tempered 

WeightedRandomSampler , with sampling weights computed as inverse class frequency raised to α = 0 .5. No class weights are added to the loss in this setting. For Dataset II, inverse-frequency class weights are incorporated directly into the loss function. Dataset III has only moderate imbalance and is trained without explicit imbalance correction. Models are optimized using AdamW across all architectures and datasets [44]. AdamW extends Adam by decoupling weight decay from the adaptive gradient update, providing explicit regularization during fine-tuning [45]. Using the same optimizer across all configurations also reduces optimizer-related variation when comparing the evaluated architectures. The complete learning-rate and weight-decay settings are summarized in Table V. Learning-rate scheduling is adapted by dataset. Cosine an-nealing is used for Dataset I [46], while ReduceLROnPlateau 

is used for Datasets II and III, reducing the learning rate when validation macro F1-score plateaus. To improve stability and efficiency, mixed precision training, gradient accumulation, and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 are applied on Datasets I and III. 

D. Training Configuration 

We train all models for a maximum of 20 epochs with a mini-batch size of 32. Validation performance is monitored during training, and early stopping is used to mitigate overfit-ting. Training stops if the validation score does not improve for 4 epochs on Dataset I or 5 epochs on Datasets II and III. The validation metric is selected based on dataset characteristics: Balanced Accuracy is used for Dataset I because of severe class imbalance, while Macro F1 is used for Datasets II and III. For each experiment, the model with the best validation score is selected for final testing. Model-specific optimization settings are listed in Table V. B. Results on Dataset I 

Dataset I shows severe class imbalance across diagnostic categories. The test set consists of 914 samples, where Non Demented (457 samples) and Very Mild Demented (320 sam-ples) are the majority classes, while Moderate Demented has only 9 samples. This imbalance makes evaluation challeng-ing because models must learn discriminative features for underrepresented disease stages from very sparse examples. Therefore, overall accuracy alone may not fully describe model behavior, as strong performance on majority classes may mask weaker performance on minority classes. Hence, macro F1-score and per-class results are particularly useful for interpreting performance on this dataset. Each individual model performs well, achieving accuracy between 97.16% and 98.47% and macro F1-score between 97.79% and 98.85%. Among individual models, ViT-Large performs best (98.47% accuracy, 98.85% macro F1), fol-lowed by Swin Transformer (98.36%, 98.75%). These results suggest that both global and hierarchical attention mecha-nisms are effective in capturing disease-relevant structural patterns. Among convolutional models, ReXNet-150 (97.81%, 98.38%) surpasses ResNet-50 (97.48%, 97.79%), while ViT-Base (97.16%, 98.15%) shows relatively balanced predictions despite lower overall accuracy, as reflected in its macro F1-score. Despite severe class imbalance, performance remains con-sistently high across individual models. As shown in Table IX, all models achieve an F1-score of 1.00 for the Moderate De-mented class in this split. However, this class contains only 9 test samples, so the result should not be overinterpreted. Lower F1-scores are generally observed for the Very Mild Demented 

and Mild Demented categories, indicating the difficulty of distinguishing adjacent early disease stages. 

C. Results on Dataset II 

Dataset II is the most challenging setting because of the mismatch between a balanced training set and a highly im-balanced test set, where No Impairment accounts for 640 samples and Moderate Impairment only 12 out of 1,279 test samples. ViT-Large obtains the best performance (97.81% accuracy, 98.30% macro F1), followed by the Swin Trans-former (97.19%, 97.60%). ReXNet-150 (95.70%, 95.80%) and ResNet-50 (94.06%, 93.00%) perform lower, suggesting greater sensitivity of these convolutional models to distribution mismatch in this setting. ViT-Base shows the largest degradation, with 91.30% accu-racy and 86.00% macro F1. The large gap between accuracy and macro F1 indicates uneven performance across classes under distribution mismatch. As shown in Table IX, ViT-Base obtains an F1-score of 0.72 for the Moderate Impairment 

class, while ViT-Large and Swin Transformer achieve 1.00 in this split. However, the Moderate Impairment class contains only 12 test samples, so these class-level results should be interpreted cautiously. Lower F1-scores are also observed for the Very Mild Impairment and Mild Impairment categories for several models, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing adjacent early stages under distribution mismatch. 

D. Results on Dataset III 

Dataset III contains augmented MRI images with a larger sample size and less severe class imbalance, resulting in a 5,098-sample standard test set. The larger sample size and reduced imbalance provide a more stable setting for training and evaluation. All individual models achieve very high accu-racy, ranging from 98.35% (ViT-Base) to 99.84% (ViT-Large), and macro F1-scores ranging from 98.50% to 99.86%. The difference between the best and worst individual models is much smaller than in Dataset II, suggesting that the larger sample size and less severe class imbalance are associated with smaller performance differences across architectures. E. Cross-Dataset Study 

The three datasets provide complementary evaluation set-tings for examining model behavior under class imbalance, distribution mismatch, and data augmentation. Dataset II is the most challenging of the three and produces the largest performance decline for most models, with balanced training data evaluated against a highly imbalanced test distribution. The magnitude of this decline varies considerably across ar-chitectures. ViT-Base shows the largest decrease, dropping by 5.86 accuracy points from Dataset I to Dataset II, while ViT-Large decreases by only 0.66 points. This suggests that model capacity may contribute to more consistent performance under the evaluated distribution mismatch, although architectural and optimization differences may also influence the result. Performance differences between architectures become more pronounced under this challenging setting. The per-formance gap is relatively small on Datasets I and III but substantially larger on Dataset II, indicating that architec-tures differ in their sensitivity to the evaluated distribution mismatch. Lower class-level performance is also generally observed for adjacent early-stage categories, particularly Very Mild and Mild , highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing subtle structural differences during early disease progression. This pattern indicates that performance consistency cannot be inferred from results on a single dataset alone, even when the same architectures achieve high absolute accuracy. The wider spread observed on Dataset II also suggests that more challenging data conditions can reveal architectural differences that remain less visible in easier or more heavily augmented settings. F. Summary of Experimental Results 

Model performance patterns remain broadly consistent across the three datasets, although the magnitude of per-formance differences varies with dataset conditions. ViT-Large and Swin Transformer consistently rank among the strongest individual models, while performance differences become more pronounced on Dataset II under train-test distri-bution mismatch and less pronounced on the larger augmented Dataset III. Across the evaluated settings, lower class-level performance is observed mainly among adjacent early-stage categories, particularly Very Mild and Mild impairment. The near-ceiling performance on Dataset III suggests that architectural differ-ences become less apparent under augmented-data conditions, although these results may be influenced by redundancy or visually similar samples [3], [29], [47], [48]. Taken together, the findings indicate that both architectural characteristics and dataset conditions influence performance. VI. L IMITATIONS 

The benchmark models achieve near-ceiling performance on Dataset III. However, this result should be interpreted with caution because Dataset III is based on augmented MRI images derived from a limited set of original samples. Visually similar or source-related images may appear across training, validation, and test splits, which can reduce evaluation diffi-culty and inflate performance. Therefore, Dataset III results should be treated as augmented-data performance rather than evidence of clinical generalization. Another limitation is that all datasets use similar four-class Alzheimer’s disease staging labels and preprocessed structural MRI images. Therefore, the findings may not fully generalize to independent clinical cohorts, multi-site hospital data, or scans acquired under different imaging protocols. In addition, this study focuses only on image-level classification and does not include longitudinal information, clinical variables, cog-nitive scores, or demographic factors. Although preprocessing and evaluation are standardized, some optimization settings are adapted by dataset and architecture. Therefore, observed performance differences should be interpreted as comparative results under the selected fine-tuning configurations rather than as isolated causal effects of architecture or model capacity. VII. C ONCLUSIONS 

In this work, we present a multi-dataset benchmarking framework for Alzheimer’s disease stage classification from MRI using five convolutional and transformer-based archi-tectures across datasets with severe class imbalance, train-test distribution mismatch, and large-scale augmentation. ViT-Large achieves the strongest performance across all three datasets, while Swin Transformer consistently ranks among the top models. Performance differences are largest on Dataset II and much smaller on the augmented Dataset III, showing that dataset characteristics strongly influence the separation between architectures. Lower class-level performance is ob-served mainly among the Very Mild and Mild categories, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing adjacent early disease stages. These findings show that evaluating models across multiple datasets provides a more reliable view of per-formance consistency than relying on a single dataset. Future work should include independent clinical cohorts, subject-level splits, multiple scanners and sites, and longitudinal or multimodal patient information. REFERENCES [1] J. Wen, E.
