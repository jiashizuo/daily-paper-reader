# EigenGr adCAM with AugSmooth: Enhancing Explainability and Pr ecision in Multi-Stage Alzheimer 's Disease Detection 

Arsham Azam 1,2 , Sagar Ashraf 4, Tayyaba Farhat 1,2 , Rasikh Ali 1,2 , Sheeraz Akram 1,2,3

Abstr act Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurological disorder associated with memory problems, cognitive decline, and behavioral disturbances, impacting millions of patients across the world. Proper multi-step diagnosis is essential for allowing clinical intervention in due time, but available neuroimaging diagnostic techniques often do not provide adequate sensitivity in detecting early-stage AD, mild cognitive impairment, and moderate dementia. The deep learning models have proved effective in MRI-based AD classification; nevertheless, their black box nature hinders further practical implementation as medical experts need interpretability of results provided by an artificial intelligence. In this work, we present a method incorporating eigenvector-based Grad-CAM and gradient smoothing to obtain interpretability in four-class AD staging from 2D axial MRI images. The proposed technique involves performing principal component analysis on gradient-weighted convolutional layers. Thus, better localization of saliency regions can be achieved compared to the conventional class activation mapping approach. Saliency weights are subject to Gaussian noise before decomposition to stabilize resulting heatmaps. To address severe class imbalance in the Moderate Dementia category, synthetic minority-class samples are generated using Wasserstein GAN with Gradient Penalty, producing a fully balanced training distribution across all four AD stages. Custom-designed convolutional neural networks are trained using the Best Alzheimer MRI Dataset, which leads to obtaining 97.2% test accuracy, 97.0% balanced accuracy score, and 0.944 Matthews Correlation Coefficient, along with AUC values from 0.96 to 0.99 for all the classes. Heatmaps produced by the model consistently identify the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex – areas which serve as neuroimaging biomarkers in AD diagnosis – which indicates that the decision-making process performed by the model is clinically sound. The experimental findings prove that the suggested architecture improves not only diagnostic capabilities but also model interpretability.

## 1. Intr oduction 

> Backgr ound

AD is a degenerative neurological condition that involves accumulation of amyloid-beta protein plaques and tau protein neurofibrillary tangles, thus causing neuronal loss, loss of memory, cognitive impairment, and behavioral disturbances [5][6]. It is considered to be the major cause of dementia worldwide and affects up to 7.2 million American people aged above 65 years; there are projections stating that by 2060, the number of AD patients will reach 13.8 million and worldwide cases of AD will exceed 152.8 million by 2050 [1][3]. AD is also very costly and total expenditure of US healthcare system for this particular disease is expected to amount to 384 billion USD in 2025 [1]. AD develops in clinical and biological stages, ranging from normal cognition through MCI to moderate and severe dementia with measurable atrophy of hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, revealed with structural MRI [5][6]. The MCI phase is considered the most crucial period in terms of the possibility for intervention, and accurate identification of the disease at this stage can significantly change the prognosis and quality of life of the patient and the caregiver. Traditional diagnostic pathways such as neuropsychological evaluation, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid, and manual inspection of neuroimages are costly, require highly trained personnel, and are not sensitive enough to detect the structural differences of early-stage AD, often failing to distinguish between MCI and moderate dementia. Deep learning technology development revolutionized the field of medical image processing, making possible automated feature extraction from MRI images that surpass traditional methods in both precision and speed [80][81]. > Figur e 1: Comprehensive overview of traditional AD detection methods, risk factors, diagnostic challenges, and AI-driven approaches Existing Pr oblems

Even with tremendous accuracy gains, the deployment of deep learning algorithms in clinical practice to diagnose AD is hindered by three basic and interrelated issues. First, the black-box problem of deep learning models. Most accurate deep learning models do not provide information on what brain areas or image features led to the diagnosis, thereby posing a Page 3 of 41 

significant challenge in terms of validation of AI-based results by healthcare specialists who have to prove and explain these decisions to the patients and other clinical staff [87][88]. It is not an issue of ease of use but rather of patient safety as the models that are correct but for the wrong reasons cannot be spotted or fixed without interpretation tools [30][31]. The second issue is the inability of current explanation techniques to work with neuroimaging tasks. Grad-CAM and its related methods have become the leading form of visualization used in CNN-driven classification of medical imaging data [34]; however, typical applications generate low-resolution heatmap visualizations that regularly fail to highlight those areas of the brain identified over many years of neuroscience literature as the key parts of the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex that exhibit pathological changes in the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease [33][52]. Additionally, gradient saliency maps are highly sensitive to small changes to the input, leading to significantly different patterns of activation, and thus unreliable explanations [44][83]. The first critical issue is that the current state of XAI evaluation in the AD imaging field relies heavily on visual assessment of heatmaps' quality that lacks any quantitative validation according to the neuroanatomical ground truth making no objective evaluation basis for clinicians regarding AI explanations [46][56]. The second serious issue is class imbalance. Current AD MRI datasets include 12 examples in the "Moderate dementia" category vs. 640 in "No impairment," which gives the ratio 50:1 and makes accuracy an irrelevant metric [24][25]. 

> Resear ch Gap

The existing literature points out to a clear and specific gap: There is no prior research that has used the eigenvector decomposition of gradient-weighted convolutional feature maps using PCA with activation-weight-space Gaussian smoothing for multi-stage AD staging from 2D axial MRI. All the existing Eigen-CAM implementations are limited to general computer vision benchmarking without any neuroimaging applications [42]. All the existing AD heatmap validations have been done qualitatively, without any faithfulness measurement using insertion or deletion AUC [56][85]. No prior work has shown that WGAN-GP balanced training and PCA-smoothed gradient attribution together improve classification performance and heatmap neuroanatomy. 

> Resear ch Objectives

The research aims to achieve four main goals. Firstly, to create a custom CNN that would be able to accurately classify four classes of AD staging using 2D axial MRI images and solve the problem of class imbalance using WGAN-GP synthetic augmentation. Secondly, to use eigenvector-based class activation mapping and extend it by including Grad-CAM gradient weights into PCA decomposition of convolutional feature maps to get better class-discriminative localization compared to non-gradient methods. Thirdly, to introduce a gradient-weight-space Gaussian perturbation and averaging technique called AugSmooth to stabilize the obtained saliency maps and make them less sensitive to small input changes. Fourthly, to validate the developed approach both in terms of classification accuracy and heatmap neuroanatomical localization. Page 4 of 41 

> Contr ibutions

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: 1. We present EigenGradCAM with Augmented Smoothing (AugSmooth), the first approach that combines gradient attribution with eigenvectors and weight-space noise reduction in interpreting four-class Alzheimer's disease staging using 2D axial MRI slices. 2. AugSmooth is a novel gradient smoothing technique that employs Gaussian perturbation on Grad-CAM weight vectors before performing PCA decomposition, fundamentally different from the input-pixel-based SmoothGrad method, which results in stable principal eigenvectors while not modifying the input data distribution [44]. 3. Augmentation of Eigen-CAM [42] with class-wise Grad-CAM gradient weights before PCA provides the class discriminatory capability and better localization than Grad-CAM [34]. 4. We use WGAN-GP based synthetic augmentation to solve the problem of the class imbalance of the Moderate Dementia category, resulting in an evenly distributed four-class training set and making per-class evaluation possible [24]. 5. We confirm that the EigengradCAM technique with AugSmooth heatmaps consistently identifies the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, proving the neuroanatomical consistency of the model's decisions with clinical AD markers [5][6]. 7. We provide a completely reproducible pipeline of deep learning in medical AI research from WGAN-GP data balancing through CNN training, EigengradCAM attribution, and AugSmooth heatmaps stabilization [80]. > 2. Liter atur e Review A. Epidemiological Context and Clinical Motivation

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the major cause of dementia globally and a rising global public health problem. The Facts and Figures 2025 Alzheimer's Disease states that there are 7.2 million people older than 65 years of age suffering from Alzheimer's dementia in the United States, which is a number that will increase to 13.8 million by 2060 [1]. Worldwide, the GBD 2019 report estimated 57.4 million cases of dementia in 2019, projecting the number to increase to 152.8 million by 2050, mainly due to ageing and population growth [2]. The total cost of AD healthcare payments in the United States was 384 billion USD in 2025 [1]. Between 1990 and 2021, there was a significant increase in the age-standardized prevalence, with high fasting plasma glucose being the major modifiable risk factor and East Asia carrying the highest regional burden [3][4]. This progression occurs in clearly defined stages ranging from normal cognitive state to MCI to AD, in which varying stages of shrinkage of the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex can be observed using structural MRI [5][6]. Such biologic staging gives credence to the clinical importance of AI-based multiple-stage MRI diagnosis, since early diagnosis in MCI is the most important time for disease modification therapy. 

> B. Deep Lear ning for Alzheimer 's MRI Classification

Deep learning has revolutionized AD diagnosis by means of neuroimaging, facilitating automatic extraction of hierarchical spatial features from MRI scans without manual feature engineering and consistently outperforming classical machine learning techniques [80][81]. The CNNs have reached high accuracy thresholds on four-class MRI benchmarks: Sorour et al. reached 99.92% with CNN-LSTM [7], Li et al. reached 99.6% with Xception [8], and Polater et al. validated MobileNetV2 at 99.92% on the same four-class benchmark [10]. Zia-Ur-Rehman et al. utilized DenseNet-201 with transfer learning for classification of five AD stages with 98.24% accuracy with augmentation [9], whereas Mousavi et al. classified 6,735 MRI images with 99% accuracy [90]. ImageNet-pretrained models with transfer learning have been the prevailing technique when labeled neuroimaging data is scarce [11][12]. Arafa et al. reached 99.95% accuracy with CNN and VGG16 cross-validation [13], Alruily et al. combined VGG16, MobileNet, and InceptionResNetV2 to achieve 97.93% [14], while Vinukonda et al. suggested using IDeepLabV3+ segmentation with EResNext with 98.12% accuracy [15]. Amuthan et al. reviewed the application of CNN, RNN, and hybrid deep learning methods on MRI, CT, and PET scans, stating that interpretability is the main challenge of applying models clinically [16]. The crucial point about all these papers is the lack of independent evaluation. Almost every paper trains and tests on different variations of the same Kaggle benchmark with different augmentation techniques, with no regard for the significant undersampling of the Moderate Dementia category. Bayahya et al., using rigorous patient-level splitting of the clinical sMRI dataset, achieved a balanced accuracy rate of just 63% without any class-imbalance mitigation – in direct contradiction to the 99% accuracy rates achieved in benchmarks [92]. Jeyapaul et al. Page 6 of 41 

utilized the ensemble diversity of MobileNetV2, DarkNet, and ResNet alongside feature selection and achieved 98.7%, proving that ensemble diversity is a marginally better approach with little in the way of interpretability [95]. 

> C. Vision Tr ansfor mer s and Attention Ar chitectur es

The Vision Transformer architecture (ViT) is another paradigm change in neuroimaging classification that involves replacement of local convolutional receptive fields with global self-attention architectures capable of modeling long-range spatial dependencies [84]. Alshayeji used ViTs for four-stage AD classification reaching 99.83% with coarse attention map visualization [17]. Khatri et al. proposed a convolution-attention model reaching 94.31% performance on ADNI with a decreased computational complexity [18]. Jomeiri et al. presented RAE-ViT for AD relevant hippocampal and ventricular region localization reaching 94.2% with attention map Dice similarity coefficient of 0.89 compared to hippocampal boundaries – one of the very rare examples of the quantifiable anatomical validation in this literature [19]. Shah et al.'s BiViT reached 96.38% [20], Mahim et al.'s ViT-GRU hybrid reached 99.69% binary accuracy with 10-fold cross-validation on ADNI [21] and Öztürk Söylemez et al.'s NeuroFusion-ViT reached 99.86% with AUC 0.999 on OASIS with ablation-validated contributions [22]. According to Guo et al.'s meta-analysis of 20 clinical studies with 12,897 participants, the pooled AUC value was 0.924 for transformer-based fusion, which proves that three-or-more-modality fusion and intermediate feature-level fusion give optimal diagnostic accuracy [23]. The key difference between transformer literature and CNN literature lies in interpretability. Attention maps in ViT are token-to-token similarity in embedding space and not gradient-weighted feature importance, which, according to Cheng et al.'s 2025 review of XAI, makes explanations derived from transformers hard to validate by anatomical truth [84]. According to Bayahya et al., ViT models had almost zero balanced accuracy on the clinical neuroimaging dataset without domain-specific pretraining [92]. 

> D. Class Imbalance, Synthetic Augmentation, and GAN-Based Methods

Class imbalance has been the least systematically researched problem in AD MRI classification. In the four-class Kaggle benchmark employed in this study, the Moderate Dementia class consists of about 64 training and 12 test images whereas the class of No Impairment includes 3,200 training and 640 test images in a 50:1 ratio which makes accuracy as a performance measure inappropriate for the task. GAN-based solutions appear to be most theoretically sound for the problem. Alamudin et al. applied WGAN-GP to AD MRI four-class problem generating realistic Moderate Dementia images using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID = 31.84), resulting in statistically significant increase in balanced accuracies on four CNN architectures [24]. The current approach uses the same WGAN-GP approach. Survey by Alzubaidi et al. demonstrated that GAN-based augmentation always performs better than SMOTE and geometric augmentation for medical imaging applications [25]. Göceri proved that the augmentation approach needs to be aligned with the imaging modality used, with synthesis-based approaches Page 7 of 41 

providing the greatest variety of MRI images [26]. IBGAN by Ding et al. focused on augmenting the intra-class sparse and boundary images that standard GAN approach ignores [27]. Islam et al. proved that SMOTE balancing and Grad-CAM XAI approaches can be applied simultaneously for AD classification, reaching 99.76% [28], while Wong et al. proved that GAN augmentation can provide an accuracy level above 80% for multi-stage classification with significantly smaller number of training images on OASIS dataset [29]. The problem addressed by this literature is the lack of research that combines WGAN-GP, multi-stage classification, and gradient-attribution explainability. 

> E. Explainable AI Foundations and Gr adient-Based Saliency Methods

Tension between accuracy and model interpretability is responsible for driving a huge amount of studies in XAI. Nazir et al.'s study (with 225 citations) distinguished three types of techniques including gradient based, perturbation based, and concept based approaches and found clinical trust to be the primary factor that prevents adoption of these techniques [30]. Chaddad et al. conducted survey in healthcare field in regard to XAI (with 475 citations) and found SHAP and LIME to be the most widely used technique in a model-agnostic approach while Grad-CAM being the most widely used gradient based technique in imaging related tasks [31]. Bhati et al. provided a classification of three techniques including saliency maps, class activation maps, and perturbation techniques; Grad-CAM was considered to be the most clinically interpretable among radiological imaging tasks [32]. There have been many variants developed within the Grad-CAM family. The original Grad-CAM approach [34] of Selvaraju et al. computes gradients in a global manner, resulting in poor localization. The work of Chattopadhyay et al., called Grad-CAM++, which has received 3,116 citations [35], applies pixel-wise second-order gradients to improve localization. Score-CAM (1,436 citations) of Wang et al. achieves better localization through score computation without any dependency on gradients [36]. Ablation-CAM (512 citations) of Desai and Ramaswamy avoids gradient saturation using channel ablation [37]. SS-CAM of Naidu et al. combines SmoothGrad and Score-CAM for improved localization [38], whereas Smooth Grad-CAM++ (284 citations) of Omeiza et al. combines SmoothGrad and Grad-CAM++ [39]. Grad++-ScoreCAM (IEEE Access 2024) of Soomro et al. combines Grad-CAM++ and Score-CAM sequentially [40]. Winsor-CAM (IEEE TPAMI 2025) of Wall et al. aggregates maps across all layers using percentile Winsorisation, obtaining 46.8% IoU compared to 39.0% of standard Grad-CAM [41]. The two most directly relevant methods are Eigen-CAM and SmoothGrad. Muhammad and Yeasin's Eigen-CAM (IJCNN 2020, 534 citations) replaced gradient-weighted averaging with PCA decomposition of raw feature activations, demonstrating 12% improvement over Grad-CAM in weakly supervised localization [42]. Dugaesescu et al.'s 2025 evaluation confirmed that PCA-based methods produce consistently higher faithfulness and coherence scores than standard Grad-CAM variants [45]. Smilkov et al.'s SmoothGrad (2,601 citations) demonstrated that averaging sensitivity maps over N Gaussian-noise-augmented input copies substantially reduces Page 8 of 41 

gradient shatter [44]. Skliarov et al.'s 2025 comparative evaluation confirmed SmoothGrad achieves the best fidelity scores among six widely-used methods [83]. The critical gap these methods collectively share is that none combines PCA-based eigenvector decomposition with gradient-weight-space noise averaging for neuroimaging classification. EigenGradCAM, as implemented in the pytorch-grad-cam library [43], adds gradient weighting to Eigen-CAM but without noise stabilization. The AugSmooth component of the proposed framework fills this gap by applying Gaussian perturbation to Grad-CAM weight vectors αk before PCA decomposition — not to input pixels — producing smoother principal eigenvectors without distribution shift. 

> F. XAI Applied to Alzheimer 's Disease

An increasing number of works have employed XAI to tackle the problem of AD detection in particular, transitioning from pure accuracy considerations to clinically interpretable assistance. The systematic PRISMA study carried out by Viswan et al. in their meta-review (74 studies) analyzed gradient-based, perturbation-based, and concept-based methods of explaining AD [46]. In the more focused Brain Informatics study (306 citations) by Vimbi et al., it was discovered that although SHAP shows impressive local faithfulness, it struggles with high-dimensional neuroimaging data [47] an issue that is directly addressed by our gradient-based EigenGradCAM model. According to Grammenos et al., hippocampus volume and ADAS-Cog were found to be the most influential SHAP features for MCI-to-AD conversion [48]; according to Alatrany et al., memory, judgment, and communication features were determined among 169,408 NACC medical records [49]; in the hierarchical method proposed by Zarei et al., hippocampal volume, delayed recall memory, and FAQ scores were shown to drive conversion [59]; and Vlontzou et al. obtained an 87.5% balanced accuracy using SHAP together with counterfactual explanation method [60]. This consistency across methodologies with regard to hippocampal atrophy as the main feature allows validating heatmap methods. For imaging-specific XAI, Song et al.'s 3D Grad-CAM on fMRI-based AD classification (96.4%, PLOS ONE 2024) identified the precuneus and hippocampus as primary ROIs one of the most direct neuroanatomical validation studies in this literature [52]. Lozupone et al.'s AXIAL generated 3D attention maps localizing the hippocampus and amygdala but evaluated these maps qualitatively without insertion/deletion metrics [50]. Junior et al. highlighted hippocampal changes as key Grad-CAM discriminators for MCI [54]. Mahmud et al.'s XAI ensemble achieved 96% accuracy with saliency maps and Grad-CAM without quantitative faithfulness scores [55]. Taiyeb Khosroshahi et al.'s 2025 Diagnostics review explicitly identified PCA-enhanced gradient attribution combined with augmented noise smoothing as an unaddressed research direction [56] the exact contribution of the present framework. AbdelAziz et al.'s SECNN-RF (99.89%, Frontiers in AI 2024) used channel-attention analogous to EigenGradCAM's feature weighting with qualitative saliency evaluation [53]. Zubair et al.'s Multi-Layer U-Net combined with EfficientNet-SVM achieved 97.78% three-class accuracy on ADNI with partial quantitative saliency analysis [65]. Aksoy et al.'s XRAI-based system (MobileNet-V3, 99.18%, Diagnostics 2025) was the first to apply XRAI to AD severity Page 9 of 41 

classification [64]. Yi et al., Soladoye et al., and Chakraborty et al. further confirmed that SHAP-based methods provide actionable clinical insights on tabular AD data [57][58][61], while Khater et al. and Castellano et al. extended XAI validation to GWAS genetic data and multimodal PET-MRI fusion respectively [62][63]. 

> G. Multimodal Fusion, Feder ated Lear ning, and Clinical Tr anslation

The integration of multiple imaging modalities and privacy-preserving deployment define the cutting edge of AD AI research in 2024–2025. Papers by Zhang et al. on MRI-PET deep learning fusion (Neuroscience 2025), recognized site data heterogeneity as a leading problem [66]. Christodoulou et al. performed a narrative review of AI in Alzheimer's diagnosis, after FDA approval of Tauvid PET in which transformers-based architecture and explainable AI were major trends [67]. Alsaleh's NeuroFusion-ADNet fused co-registered MRI and PET with bi-directional cross-attention, achieving 99.48% with attention focused on the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and basal ganglia [68]. Shaikh et al.'s 2025 review covered accuracy-interpretability trade-offs, federated learning, and regulatory considerations for clinical translation [69]. On privacy-preserving deployment, Mitrovska et al. demonstrated Federated Averaging with Secure Aggregation achieves performance comparable to centralized training for AD detection [70]. Jahan et al. combined FL with SHAP-based XAI achieving 98.93% while maintaining full data decentralization [71]. In Pan et al., LSTM combined with FedAvg was used on six simulated EHR sites, with a 6% AUC improvement over local models [72]. Rehman et al. have conducted a systematic review that supports the fact that hybrid CNN-transfer learning approaches and multimodal fusion deliver the highest diagnostic accuracy [73]. The real-world implication of this cutting-edge technology for the current research project is straightforward: unimodal 2D axial MRI classification is a mandatory and repeatable milestone, and the interpretability assessment approach developed in this study is the basis for further developments.

> I. Synthesis: The Accuracy–Interpretability Trade-off and Convergent Failure

Perhaps the most important synthesis across this entire body of literature is that high accuracy and clinical interpretability are not inherently opposed but have been treated as separate research objectives. Ennab et al. found that the accuracy advantage of opaque models over interpretable ones decreases substantially when the latter are properly regularized [87]. Frasca et al.'s bibliometric review of 448 studies confirmed that the accuracy-interpretability trade-off remains the defining challenge of medical AI [88]. Singh et al.'s meta-analysis of 67 XAI medical imaging studies documented only a 5–7% AUC performance penalty for interpretable models, substantially smaller than commonly assumed — but also found 86% of studies used only qualitative evaluation [85]. Abbas et al.'s meta-analysis confirmed that clinical usability of XAI systems depends critically on quantitative faithfulness evaluation, which remains largely absent from the literature [86]. The pattern that emerges from all reviewed work is this: high accuracy on the Kaggle benchmark is achievable by many architectures; quantitative heatmap faithfulness on neuroanatomically validated targets has been demonstrated by almost none; and the combination of class-balanced WGAN-GP training with PCA-based gradient attribution and weight-space noise smoothing has not been studied. This triple gap defines the research contribution of the proposed EigenGradCAM with AugSmooth framework. 

> J. Identified Research Gap

The foregoing review of 95 published works across nine thematic dimensions reveals a convergent finding: no published study has combined (a) eigenvector-based PCA decomposition of gradient-weighted feature maps (EigenGradCAM) with (b) Gaussian perturbation averaging of activation weight vectors (AugSmooth) for multi-stage AD staging on 2D axial MRI. Specifically: existing EigenGradCAM applications are limited to general computer vision benchmarks [42][43][45] with no application to neuroimaging; existing AD heatmap evaluations are purely qualitative without insertion/deletion AUC faithfulness metrics [56][85]; and no prior work has validated that WGAN-GP-balanced training and PCA-smoothed gradient attribution jointly improve both classification accuracy and heatmap clinical alignment [24][44]. This EigenGradCAM with AugSmooth approach fills this gap. ## 3. Pr oposed Methodology 

This work proposes an interpretable deep learning approach for Alzheimer's disease classification at multiple stages using 2D axial MRIs, through the combination of EigenGradCAM and Augmented Smoothing (AugSmooth). This framework deals with three basic problems: (1) extreme class imbalance in the Moderate Dementia class; (2) the black-box nature of the CNNs' predictions; (3) the instability of gradient-based methods in the presence of small changes in the input image. 6.1 Over view of the XAI Pipeline The proposed EigenGradCAM with Augmented Smoothing (AugSmooth) framework generates class-discriminative visual explanations for each CNN prediction by computing the first principal eigenvector of gradient-weighted convolutional feature maps, stabilized through Gaussian perturbation of activation weight vectors averaged over N = 50 samples with σ = 0.15 (Section 3.6). Unlike standard Grad-CAM, which produces coarse activation maps susceptible to gradient shatter artefacts, and unlike Eigen-CAM [42], which lacks class discrimination, the proposed method combines class-specificity from Grad-CAM weights [34] with the spatial coherence of PCA decomposition [42] and the noise robustness of SmoothGrad-style averaging [44]. These heatmaps are overlaid on the original MRI with blend factor α = 0.4, thus creating clinically interpretable visualizations indicating the brain areas which are most important for each AD stage classification decision. In this part of the research paper, the generated heatmaps for all four AD stages are analyzed, and the influence of AugSmooth on heatmap stability is assessed. 6.2 Heatmap Visualization per AD Stage Figure 8 shows the EigenGradCAM with AugSmooth heatmaps obtained for five representative test samples of all four AD stages. Heatmaps presented in Figure 8 show the spatially consistent and stage-dependent activation patterns. In case of the No Impairment class, the activation pattern is rather widespread in terms of cortical areas with no concentration in the medial temporal lobe. Such activation pattern reflects the fact that there is no AD-induced structural atrophy in cognitively healthy subjects and proves that the network does not have any artificial activations in clinically irrelevant regions. In the case of the Very Mild Impairment class, the activation starts to concentrate more in the medial temporal lobe, but it still remains diffuse as it should be given the subtle changes in the hippocampus volume at this AD stage. The heatmaps for the mild impairment category indicate highly localized activations in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, which are the key areas in which neurofibrillary tangles and loss of grey matter occur in the case of AD at this disease stage [5][6]. The high localization of activations in relation to the medial temporal structures rather than a scattered pattern of activations proves the benefit of the eigenvector decomposition based on PCA in comparison to standard Grad-CAM. In the case of the class Moderate Impairment, activation reaches its peak concentration in the hippocampal and entorhinal areas, in line with the late atrophy that is typical for this level of impairment [6]. Such activations are in agreement with the ROIs defined by Song et al. [52] through 3D Grad-CAM analysis of AD classification based on fMRI data. 6.3 Effect of AugSmooth on Heatmap Stability In order to analyze the role played by the AugSmooth part of the algorithm, we compare the heatmaps obtained using three different approaches applied to the same Mild Impairment test image standard Grad-CAM, EigenGradCAM without AugSmooth, and EigenGradCAM with AugSmooth. ## 7. Discussion 

7.1 Inter pr etation of Classification Per for mance The framework developed provides test accuracies of 97.2%, BAS of 97.0%, and MCC of 0.944 (Table 7, Section 5.2). This performance is seemingly inferior to baselines' (Table 10 with 99.6%–99.92%). Nevertheless, such a comparison should be considered carefully because all baseline models [7][8][10][28][53] provide only top-line accuracies with the test distribution, where No Impairment covers 50.0% and Moderate Impairment just 0.9% – that is, where a trivial predictor which predicts "No Impairment" for any sample will achieve an accuracy rate of 50.0% but fail to identify other categories entirely. None of the baseline models provides BAS and MCC, which makes it impossible to evaluate their accuracies. BAS of 97.0% from the developed framework ensures high performance throughout all of the four steps. In addition to this, a robust single evaluation measure in the form of an MCC of 0.944 is obtained considering all the values from the confusion matrix. Such a result corresponds well with the idea stated by Ennab et al., according to which the benefit of accuracy from opaque models becomes less pronounced with a correct regularizer applied to interpretable models [87]. 7.2 Significance of WGAN-GP Class Balancing WGAN-GP synthetic augmentation (Section 3.2) plays a foundational role in the performance achieved. Without class balancing, the 50:1 ratio between No Impairment and Moderate Impairment training samples would suppress sensitivity for the Moderate Dementia stage — precisely the class where accurate identification most directly influences intervention timing [1][5]. By synthesizing realistic Moderate Impairment samples and training all four classes to equal representation (2,560 images each, Table 2), the WGAN-GP strategy produces an F1-score of 0.91 for Moderate Impairment (Table 7) — substantially higher than unbalanced training would yield. Alamudin et al. reported a mean balanced accuracy improvement of 4.76% across four CNN architectures with WGAN-GP applied to the same AD MRI class imbalance problem [24]. The use of WGAN-GP rather than SMOTE is motivated by GAN synthesis modelling the full MRI data distribution rather than interpolating between existing samples [25][26]. 7.3 Inter pr etation of Explainability Results The EigenGradCAM with AugSmooth heatmaps (Section 6) reveal a neuroanatomically consistent and stage-dependent activation pattern providing meaningful qualitative evidence of clinical alignment. The progressive concentration from diffuse cortical distribution in No Impairment through tightly focused hippocampal and entorhinal cortical activation in Mild and Moderate Impairment (Table 11, Section 6.4) mirrors the Braak staging trajectory of AD pathology [6]. This is independently corroborated by Song et al.'s identification of the hippocampus and precuneus as primary 3D Grad-CAM ROIs in fMRI-based AD classification [52], and is consistent with the NIA-AA biological framework for AD staging [5]. The qualitative comparison in Table 11 demonstrates that AugSmooth makes a specific and Page 36 of 41 

measurable contribution by suppressing gradient shatter artefacts without altering the input image distribution — a methodological distinction from original SmoothGrad [44]. Taiyeb Khosroshahi et al. explicitly identified PCA-enhanced gradient attribution with augmented noise smoothing as an unaddressed research direction [56] — the present work directly fills this gap. 7.4 Limitations Some limitations should be addressed upfront. First, the model works solely on 2D axial MRI slices at 128x128 pixels (Table 2, Section 3.1), losing the volumetric information available in 3D MRI scans. Bayahya et al. showed that models trained on 2D benchmark data have significantly lower balanced accuracy on 3D clinical datasets with correct patient-level splitting [92]; thus, 97.2% accuracy seems to be a benchmark value and not an estimate of clinical deployment performance. Second, all experiments are performed using one Kaggle dataset [96]. Lack of external validation on either the ADNI or the OASIS dataset hinders generalizability [73][92]. Third, the lack of quantitative faithfulness metrics such as insertion and deletion AUC or Average Drop on the XAI assessment in Section 6. Fourth, the number of instances in the class for the Moderate Impairment test set equals 12 (Table 2), rendering the per-class evaluation metrics for this stage high variance. Fifth, the dataset fails to specify the ratio of patients' gender (Table 2), which makes the study of gender-based bias classification impossible due to females higher AD prevalence [4]. Sixth, no clinical validation by neuroradiologists has been conducted to confirm that heatmap activations correspond to clinically meaningful findings on individual patient scans. 7.5 Futur e Wor k Limitations discussed in Section 7.4 set an obvious research agenda. Firstly, it is necessary to extend to 3D volumes of MRI scans by considering all coronal and sagittal slices, as well as applying 3D versions of EigenGradCAM equivalent to the AXIAL setup described by Lozupone et al. [50]. Clinical generalization should be performed using independent patient splitting from ADNI and OASIS [73]. Quantitative faithfulness verification of XAI using insertion/deletion AUC and pointing game score [83][85] is another objective to be addressed. Multimodal fusion through integration of amyloid PET with structural MRI [63][68] would be an obvious next step to enhance the accuracy of classification and heatmap neuroanatomy. The use of privacy-preserving federated learning at several clinical sites [70][71] would tackle issues related to data heterogeneity and train models for bigger populations. Lastly, marking the borders of the hippocampus by neuroradiologists in test images would make it possible to calculate the Dice similarity coefficient. Page 37 of 41 

## 8. Conclusion 

This paper presented EigenGradCAM with Augmented Smoothing (AugSmooth), an interpretable deep learning framework for four-class Alzheimer's disease staging from 2D axial MRI. The framework addressed three fundamental challenges limiting the clinical adoption of existing AD classification systems: severe class imbalance in the Moderate Dementia category, the black-box nature of CNN classification decisions, and the instability of gradient-based saliency maps under minor input perturbations. WGAN-GP synthetic augmentation resolved the 50:1 class imbalance by producing a fully balanced training distribution of 2,560 images per class. A custom CNN trained on this balanced dataset achieved 97.2% test accuracy, a Balanced Accuracy Score of 97.0%, and a Matthews Correlation Coefficient of 0.944, with AUC values consistently between 0.96 and 0.99 across all four AD stages. These results demonstrate that high and balanced multi-class performance is achievable on the four-class MRI staging benchmark when class imbalance is explicitly addressed during training. EigenGradCAM was an extension to Eigen-CAM by introducing class-discriminative Grad-CAM gradient weighting before performing PCA on convolutional feature maps. On the other hand, AugSmooth was an extension to SmoothGrad where Gaussian perturbations were used on activation weight vectors instead of pixel values of the input images over N=50 images and σ = 0.15, resulting in stable spatially coherent heatmaps without affecting the distribution of the input images. The heatmaps that were produced successfully marked hippocampus and entorhinal cortex as the most activated areas for the classifications of Mild and Moderate Impairments, anatomically consistent with the Braak staging pathogenesis of AD and independently validated through existing XAI studies based on gradients [5][6][52]. Key limitations of this work, including working on 2D axial slice planes only, validation on a single dataset, and the qualitatively-based XAI faithfulness evaluation of the system are defining key directions for future studies. Extension of the approach to 3D volumetric MRIs, validation on other independent datasets from ADNI/OASIS databases, the quantitative evaluation of faithfulness of insertion/deletion AUC, MRIs/PET fusion approach, and application of this technology in federated learning mode can be identified as some of the most promising research directions for bringing this approach closer to its practical applications in clinical practice.
