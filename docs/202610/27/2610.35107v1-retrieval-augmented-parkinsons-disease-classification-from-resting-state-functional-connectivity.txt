# RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED PARKINSON’S DISEASE CLASSIFICATION FROM RESTING-STATE FUNCTIONAL CONNECTIVITY 

# Tara Niknam, Sana Hassan Imam, Daniel Kristanto 

# Psychological Methods and Statistics, Department of Psychology, Carl von Ossietzky Universit¨ at Oldenburg, 26129 Oldenburg, Germany 

ABSTRACT 

Altered resting-state functional connectivity (FC) is a can-didate marker for the diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Publicly available PD datasets, however, are small and frag-mented across sites, scanners and clinical criteria. Transfer learning mitigates data scarcity by reusing a model trained on larger cohorts, but represents their knowledge primarily through a single set of weights shared by all individuals. Motivated by precision neuroimaging, we propose a retrieval-augmented classification (RAC) framework that additionally places each target individual in the context of similar indi-viduals from external cohorts. A graph convolutional autoen-coder (GAE), trained on external PD and control cohorts, encodes these cohorts into a neuro-knowledge base. Each target individual is encoded using the pretrained GAE, and its nearest neighbors are retrieved from the external knowledge base. Their embeddings are combined with the target embed-ding using mean aggregation or attention for classification. A systematic comparison on the TaoWu cohort separated the contributions of encoder pretraining, retrieval, attention and partial fine-tuning. Pretraining improved mean classi-fication accuracy from 60.5% to 66.0%. However, retrieval and attention did not consistently improve performance over the pretrained-only variants. Several retrieval-based config-urations nevertheless showed modestly higher ROC-AUC than the randomly initialized baseline. Varying the num-ber of retrieved neighbors and partially fine-tuning the en-coder provided no consistent additional gains. Retrieval-neighborhood analysis further showed diagnostically mixed neighborhoods. These findings provide initial evidence that population-learned representations can be complemented by individual-specific retrieval in neuroimaging, although larger datasets are needed to determine when such contextual infor-mation improves classification.

1. INTRODUCTION 

Parkinson’s disease is the fastest-growing neurological disor-der [1], with the number of people living with PD projected to reach 25.2 million by 2050 [2]. Diagnosis remains largely based on clinical motor signs [3], motivating the search for objective neuroimaging biomarkers. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) has revealed reproducible alterations of func-tional brain organization in PD [4], and FC has increasingly been used for machine-learning-based classification of indi-viduals with PD and healthy controls (HC) [5]. A major challenge is that PD rs-fMRI cohorts are typi-cally small and distributed across sites, scanners, and clinical populations [6, 7]. Direct pooling is complicated by ac-quisition and cohort differences that can substantially affect FC [8], as well as regulatory and privacy constraints on sharing individual-level neuroimaging data. Models trained on single cohorts therefore remain limited by small sam-ple sizes and uncertain performance estimates [9]. Transfer learning addresses this problem by pretraining models on external datasets before applying them to a target cohort [10]. However, the transferred knowledge is primarily encoded in parameters learned across the source population and may not preserve which previously observed individuals are most informative for a particular target individual. This limitation relates to a central principle of preci-sion neuroimaging: brain organization contains both shared population-level structure and meaningful individual-specific variation [11]. Analogously, transformer models use atten-tion to complement general representations with context-specific information by weighting the relevance of surround-ing representations to a given query [12]. We therefore investigate retrieval-augmented classification, inspired by retrieval-augmented generation [13], as a way to combine both levels of information. A GAE first learns a common latent representation of FC from external PD and HC. For each target individual, RAC then retrieves the most similar external individuals in this representation space and inte-grates their embeddings with the representation of the target individual for classification. External cohorts thus serve not only as a source for population-level pretraining, but also as an individual-specific contextual memory. We evaluate arXiv:2610.35107v1 [q-bio.NC] 26 Oct 2026 whether combining population-learned representations with individual-specific retrieval can provide additional informa-tion for PD classification from resting-state FC. 

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS 2.1. Data 

We used preprocessed resting-state FC matrices of PD and HC cohorts from an open-access collection of brain network datasets [6]. In this collection, raw MRI data were prepro-cessed with a common fMRIPrep pipeline, parcellated with the 100-region Schaefer atlas, and converted into FC matri-ces of Pearson correlations between regional BOLD signals. We used three PD cohorts. The knowledge base comprised 

N = 169 individuals from the Parkinson’s Progression Mark-ers Initiative (PPMI) and Neurocon datasets ( NPD = 139 ,

NHC = 30 ); prodromal and SWEDD individuals of PPMI were excluded. The classification framework was trained and evaluated exclusively on the TaoWu cohort ( NPD = 20 ,

NHC = 20 ), which is not part of the knowledge base. The cohorts differ in acquisition and clinical definition: PPMI is a multi-site study of patients diagnosed within the past two years and not taking PD medication, whereas TaoWu and Neurocon were acquired on single scanners and include treated patients [6]. After pretraining, all 169 knowledge-base individuals were embedded once with dropout disabled, and their 128-dimensional embeddings were stored in an exact Euclidean FAISS in-dex [17] for nearest-neighbor retrieval.

2.6. Model variants 

We evaluated six configurations. M0 was trained from scratch on TaoWu without pre-training or retrieval, whereas M1 used the frozen pretrained encoder without retrieval. M2 added mean retrieval, M3 parameter-free dot-product atten-tion, and M4 learnable scaled dot-product attention. M5 extended M4 by fine-tuning only the final encoder projection while keeping both graph convolutional layers frozen. The external knowledge-base embeddings and FAISS index re-mained fixed. Retrieval-based models were evaluated with 

k ∈ { 5, 10 , 15 }.

2.7. Training and evaluation 

All configurations were evaluated on TaoWu using repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation with five repetitions, yield-ing 25 validation-fold evaluations per configuration. Identical individual partitions were used across configurations. Train-able components were reinitialized in each fold; pretrained models reloaded the external encoder, whereas M0 was ini-tialized randomly. Models were trained for up to 100 epochs using Adam (batch size 16, learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 10 −4) and binary cross-entropy loss. For M5, the fine-tuned encoder projection used a learning rate of 10 −5.A learning-rate scheduler reduced the learning rate based on validation loss, and early stopping with patience 15 selected the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Retrieval used only the external knowledge base, which contained no TaoWu individuals. Performance was sum-marized by mean accuracy and ROC-AUC across the 25 validation folds, with fold-level variability reported where applicable. All models were implemented in PyTorch Ge-ometric. 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

As shown in Fig. 2, mean validation accuracy improved from M0 to the pretrained-only model M1, but retrieval did not provide a consistent additional improvement across RAC configurations or values of k. In contrast, ROC-AUC was modestly higher for several models using the pretrained rep-resentation and retrieval than for the randomly initialized baseline, although the distributions across cross-validation folds overlapped substantially. Increasing the number of retrieved neighbors from k = 5 to k = 15 produced no con-sistent improvement in either metric. These results suggest that population-level pretraining and retrieval may influence the ranking of PD and HC individuals, although retrieval did not yield a consistent additional gain in threshold-dependent accuracy. The diagnostic composition of the retrieved neighbor-hoods is summarized in Table 1. PD target individuals retrieved on average 79 .5 ± 13 .6% PD and 20 .5 ± 13 .6% 

HC neighbors, whereas HC target individuals retrieved 

81 .0 ± 12 .9% PD and 19 .0 ± 12 .9% HC neighbors. Thus, the neighborhoods were diagnostically mixed and showed similar compositions for PD and HC target individuals. This indicates that similarity in the unsupervised FC representation does not simply reproduce diagnostic group membership. At the same time, the predominance of PD individuals in the external knowledge base likely contributes to the observed neighborhood composition. More broadly, this result is con-sistent with the motivation underlying RAC and with the perspective of precision neuroimaging, which emphasizes that meaningful individual variation may not align directly with categorical group boundaries. The retrieval framework operationalizes this idea by identifying the external individu-als most similar to each target individual rather than relying exclusively on population-level representations. 4. CONCLUSION 

This study investigated retrieval-augmented classification as a framework for combining population-level representation learning with individual-specific contextual information in PD functional connectivity. Although retrieval-based RAC configurations did not consistently improve accuracy beyond the pretrained-only model, several configurations showed modestly higher ROC-AUC, while the retrieved neighbor-hoods revealed that FC similarity was not restricted to indi-viduals sharing the same diagnosis. Together, these findings suggest that external cohorts can provide information beyond a single population-trained representation, but that the present retrieval strategy does not yet translate this information into consistently improved classification. The framework was motivated in part by transformer and retrieval-based approaches in large language models, where attention and retrieval benefit from very large training cor-pora and rich representation spaces. The present results pro-vide initial proof-of-concept evidence that a related princi-ple can be implemented in neuroimaging, by combining a population-learned representation with information retrieved specifically for each target individual. However, the substan-tially smaller scale of available neuroimaging datasets likely limits the extent to which such individualized retrieval can be learned reliably. From a precision neuroimaging perspec-tive, the framework offers a computational way to comple-ment population-level learning with individual-specific con-textualization, although the present results indicate that larger and more diverse datasets are needed to determine when such personalization improves clinically relevant prediction. A key next step is therefore to evaluate the framework in substantially larger and more diverse datasets. Larger co-horts would allow individual-specific neighborhoods to be estimated more reliably, provide richer variability for repre-sentation learning, and enable a more robust assessment of whether retrieval contributes information beyond population-level pretraining. They may also help determine whether retrieval captures clinically meaningful heterogeneity beyond binary PD versus HC labels. Future extensions could further enrich the knowledge base with clinical metadata, such as diagnosis, medication status, or other individual-level char-acteristics, allowing retrieval to incorporate not only imaging similarity but also clinically relevant contextual information.
