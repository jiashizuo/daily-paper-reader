# Longitudinal Brain Aging in Covariance Space: Temporally Calibrated Bayesian Inference for Adaptive Covariance Operators 

## Montserrat Fuentes, PhD St. Edward’s University Yi Zhao, PhD Department of Biostatistics and Health Data Science Indiana University 

Abstract 

Functional brain aging is usually described from differences between people of differ-ent ages, even though aging itself unfolds within the same individual. This distinction becomes especially important for functional connectivity, where the scientific object is a high-dimensional covariance system and the direction of reproducible change may itself evolve across adulthood. We introduce a novel Bayesian longitudinal covariance-operator framework that treats each visit covariance as a latent positive-definite state informed directly by repeated resting-state fMRI runs. The proposed approach combines several innovations: run-level information is calibrated for temporal dependence and replicate variability, within-person aging is separated from between-person age differences, and an adaptive tangent representation learns the low-dimensional structure of systematic 

1arXiv:2610.19353v1 [stat.ME] 14 Oct 2026 longitudinal change without selecting directions from pooled covariance variation. The longitudinal operator is allowed to change smoothly in magnitude and orientation with age, and exact Fr´ echet derivatives translate the fitted trajectory back to the covariance scale. This construction makes it possible to study questions that are not addressed by standard cross-sectional analyses or fixed-projection longitudinal approaches: how the covariance organization of the same brain changes with age, when that reorgani-zation is most pronounced, whether it follows the population age gradient, and which functional systems account for agreement or divergence. In the Dallas Lifespan Brain Study, within-person covariance aging differs markedly from the cross-sectional age gradient through early and middle adulthood, with the discrepancy weakening later in life. Reorganization is strongest around midlife, the systematic longitudinal signal is concentrated in only a few coordinated directions, and most of its off-diagonal energy is expressed between functional systems. The results show that population-level signatures of brain aging do not necessarily reflect the trajectory followed by an individual brain and demonstrate the value of modeling longitudinal covariance change directly as a biological process. 

# 1 Introduction 

Healthy brain aging is increasingly understood as a reorganization of interacting neural systems rather than a uniform loss of connectivity. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has made this organization observable through spontaneous correlations among distributed brain regions. Across adulthood, changes have been documented in long-range connectivity, network integration, modular structure, and the balance between within-system and between-system coupling (Damoiseaux et al., 2008; Betzel et al., 2014; Sala-Llonch et al., 2014; Geerligs et al., 2015). More recent studies continue to show that aging is associated with changes in the organization and differentiation of large-scale functional systems rather than a simple global reduction in connectivity (Setton et al., 2023; Martin 2et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Calder et al., 2026). This broader view is consistent with neurocognitive theories in which adaptation and compensatory reorganization accompany decline (D. C. Park and P. Reuter-Lorenz, 2009; P. A. Reuter-Lorenz and D. C. Park, 2014). The relevant scientific object is therefore not a collection of isolated connections, but the organization of dependence across the brain as a whole. The Dallas Lifespan Brain Study (DLBS) has played an important role in shaping this perspective. Cross-sectional analyses showed that younger adults exhibit stronger functional-system segregation, whereas increasing age is associated with less differentiated large-scale organization (Chan, D. C. Park, et al., 2014; Chan, Alhazmi, et al., 2017). These findings, together with more recent evidence of age-related reorganization across functional systems (Setton et al., 2023; Martin et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Calder et al., 2026), make the distinction between population age differences and individual aging increasingly important. A pattern that distinguishes older from younger adults is not necessarily the path followed by the same brain as it ages. Population age contrasts combine longitudinal change with stable differences among people and with cohort structure. In a high-dimensional covariance system, this distinction can involve more than a difference in effect size: the direction of covariance change within a person may differ from the direction that separates people of different ages. A cross-sectional signature may therefore characterize how older and younger individuals differ without describing how an individual brain changes over time. Longitudinal rs-fMRI studies provide evidence that functional organization changes within individuals, including changes in connectivity, segregation, modularity, and network configuration (Ng et al., 2016; Oschmann and Gawryluk, 2020; Malagurski et al., 2020). Yet most longitudinal analyses reduce each scan to selected edges, predefined network summaries, graph measures, or a small number of connectivity features. Those summaries are valuable for focused questions, but they do not describe the evolution of the covariance state itself. They cannot determine whether the dominant covariance direction of within-person aging is stable across adulthood, whether the rate of whole-system reorganization changes with age, 3or whether the longitudinal trajectory agrees geometrically with the population age gradient. Statistical methods for covariance outcomes provide important foundations for addressing these questions. Matrix-logarithmic and geometric approaches preserve positive-definite structure (Chiu et al., 1996; Yuan et al., 2012), while covariance regression and Bayesian factor models allow covariates to alter dependence structure (Hoff and Niu, 2012; Fox and Dunson, 2015). Covariate Assisted Principal regression introduced a way to learn covariance directions associated with predictors rather than selecting them from pooled variation alone (Zhao, B. Wang, et al., 2021), and its longitudinal extension accommodates repeated covariance outcomes through a learned projection (Zhao, Caffo, et al., 2024). Recent developments have broadened this literature further. Bayesian CAP provides posterior inference for covariance-associated directions (H. G. Park, 2025), while multilevel covariance regression introduces hierarchical structure for repeated and clustered covariance outcomes (Orindi et al., 2023). MCAP allows covariance-associated projection directions to vary across groups while sharing information across them, with a cross-sectional lifespan application that identifies population subnetworks that differ across age (Murphy Green et al., 2026). Latent-space formulations provide another strategy for representing high-dimensional covariance structure parsimoniously (S. Wang et al., 2026). These methods provide a foundation for studying covariance variation, but our focus is the continuous trajectory of the covariance within individuals. Unlike approaches based on a fixed covariance-associated projection, we allow the direction of longitudinal change to evolve with age. The geometric and neural-field modes studied by Robinson and Pang (Robinson et al., 2016; Pang et al., 2023) arise from anatomical or dynamical operators. Our covariance modes instead describe dependence among BOLD signals and are estimated from repeated measurements. The visit-specific covariance is inferred from finite BOLD time series rather than observed directly. Filtering, temporal dependence, and censoring affect the information available from each run. Repeated runs also vary around the underlying visit covariance. Our model carries 4this uncertainty into inference on longitudinal change rather than treating an estimated covariance matrix as error-free. High dimensionality creates a related modeling challenge. With 100 cortical regions, a visit covariance contains 5,050 distinct entries. Regularization is unavoidable, but the source of the low-dimensional structure matters. Directions that dominate pooled covariance variation may reflect stable individual heterogeneity, overall amplitude, or nuisance variation rather than reproducible aging. We therefore place adaptive dimension reduction on the systematic within-person aging component while allowing baseline covariance and individual heterogeneity to remain substantially richer. The learned subspace is tied to the longitudinal scientific question rather than inherited from an unsupervised decomposition of total variation. A Bayesian hierarchy links repeated runs to a latent positive-definite covariance state at each visit. It then learns the longitudinal trajectory and the uncertainty in its direction, magnitude, and effective dimension. Age is decomposed into within-person change around each participant’s mean observed age and between-person location in the population age distribution. Separate operators represent these two sources of age variation, so their similarity is learned rather than assumed. The within-person operator varies smoothly with age inside an adaptive tangent span, allowing the covariance-aging direction to reorient across adulthood. Exact Fr´ echet derivatives then translate the tangent-space model to covariance scale, where the scientific questions are naturally expressed. The fitted trajectory describes the rate and direction of within-person covariance change and its agreement with the cross-sectional age gradient. Network-resolved summaries show which systems contribute to this agreement or divergence without requiring separate tests for thousands of edges. The DLBS provides a particularly informative setting because the study has helped establish cross-sectional functional segregation as a hallmark of healthy aging and now contains repeated observations spanning much of adulthood. The longitudinal data allow that influential population-level picture to be reconsidered as a within-person biological 5process. Agreement between the longitudinal covariance trajectory and the cross-sectional gradient would support the interpretation that population age signatures trace individual aging. Divergence would show that the two describe different aspects of lifespan variation and would identify when and where that distinction is strongest. The remainder of the paper follows the analysis from imaging data to biological inter-pretation. Section 2 introduces the DLBS observations and defines the scientific objective. Section 3 describes how repeated runs inform a longitudinal covariance model that separates changes within individuals from differences across individuals. Section 4 explains how the fitted trajectory is summarized in biologically interpretable measures. Section 5 presents the Bayesian approach and the positive-definiteness property. Section 6 reports the DLBS findings, and Section 7 considers their implications. Mathematical details, the proof, and simulation results are provided in the Supplementary Materials. 

# 2 Data and scientific target 

The Dallas Lifespan Brain Study was designed to characterize neural and cognitive change across adulthood. The full study enrolled 464 adults aged 21–89 years at the initial assessment. Follow-up waves were conducted approximately every 3.5–5 years, with 338 participants returning for a second epoch and 224 for a third (D. C. Park, Hennessee, et al., 2025). The broad baseline age distribution is important for the present analysis because repeated measurements begin at many points in adulthood rather than being concentrated only in late life. Our methodological analysis uses 50 participants selected to provide broad lifespan coverage while retaining three repeated visits. The resulting data contain 150 participant-visits and 252 resting-state runs. Across all observed visits, ages range from 22 to 96 years. The cohort contains 28 women and 22 men. Most participants contribute five runs across the three visits, while two contribute six. The resting-state acquisition used a repetition time 6(TR) of 2 s throughout the study. Epoch 1 included a 154-volume resting-state run, whereas epochs 2 and 3 included two runs per visit; the run duration was increased from 154 to 180 volumes partway through epoch 2 (D. C. Park, Hennessee, et al., 2025). Thus, the difference between 154 and 180 original volumes reflects a change in run duration rather than temporal sampling. After preprocessing and framewise-displacement censoring, retained run length ranges from 89 to 180 volumes, with a mean of 156. All BOLD images were preprocessed with fMRIPrep (Esteban et al., 2019) and analyzed in the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template space. Regional signals were extracted with the Schaefer 2018 cortical atlas (Schaefer et al., 2018) using 100 parcels grouped into seven canonical networks. The atlas was obtained directly in the same nonlinear template space and at 2-mm resolution, avoiding cross-template interpolation of the label image. Regional series were linearly detrended and band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.10 Hz. Nuisance regression included the six rigid-body motion parameters together with their temporal derivatives, squares, and squared derivatives, plus white-matter and cerebrospinal-fluid signals. Volumes with framewise displacement above 0.5 mm were censored. Runs remained separate throughout the analysis rather than being concatenated within visit. The primary representation uses percent signal change (PSC), rather than standardizing each parcel to unit variance. This choice is important for the present scientific target because unit-variance standardization removes regional amplitude information by construction and produces a correlation-like representation. PSC preserves relative BOLD fluctuation amplitude, allowing the covariance model to distinguish changes in marginal variability from changes in dependence structure. A z-standardized representation is retained as a prespecified sensitivity analysis to assess whether the main conclusions depend on amplitude information. Quality-control diagnostics indicated that residual run-level motion burden remained associated with features of the estimated covariance matrices after preprocessing and censoring. We therefore include run-level motion as a nuisance covariate in the longitudinal covariance model to reduce the possibility that residual motion-related variation is attributed to age. 7This model-based adjustment is complementary to preprocessing and does not reintroduce motion into the BOLD time series. Robustness was further assessed using stricter censoring and exclusion of high-motion observations. Figure 1 connects the imaging representation to the statistical object estimated by the model. Panel A shows the Schaefer-100 cortical parcellation organized into seven functional networks. Each visit therefore contributes repeated multivariate BOLD observations on the same 100-region cortical system. The goal is not to estimate pairwise covariance changes independently, but to characterize how the covariance system changes as a coordinated whole. Biologically, the covariance-aging operator describes this coordinated pattern of change. At a given age, it represents how the fitted covariance structure changes with an incremental increase in age. Individual entries describe changes in covariance between particular regions, but the operator retains those changes as one multivariate object. An edge-by-edge analysis treats those changes as separate effects, whereas the operator-valued analysis characterizes their joint direction, magnitude, and effective dimensionality. The accompanying posterior summaries quantify how the organization of longitudinal covariance change evolves across adulthood. The figure also illustrates why the distinction between cross-sectional and longitudinal aging is made at the operator level. Aging may weaken covariance within one network while strengthening coupling between others, or reorganize dominant spatial modes without producing a uniform change in connectivity. # 3 Longitudinal covariance model 

We seek to describe how the covariance of brain activity changes as an individual grows older. This requires two distinctions. First, an estimated covariance from a BOLD run is an uncertain measurement, not the underlying covariance state. Second, differences between people of different ages do not necessarily describe changes within the same person. Our approach addresses both questions in one model. We first calibrate the information in each run, then model longitudinal and between-person age effects separately. A central contribution is that the directions of longitudinal covariance change are learned from the repeated visits rather than selected from the dominant patterns of overall covariance variation.

## 3.3 Learning the structure of aging 

Although a covariance matrix has many entries, reproducible aging may involve only a small number of coordinated directions. We allow a flexible set of candidate aging directions and place cumulative ordered-shrinkage priors on their coefficients. Later directions receive progressively stronger shrinkage, but none is discarded in advance. The posterior therefore learns the effective complexity of the aging signal while retaining broader participant and visit variation. This differs from selecting principal components based on pooled covariance, which can emphasize between-person differences rather than longitudinal aging. The low-dimensional representation also makes fitting practical. Matrix exponentials are evaluated exactly within the combined span of the systematic and nuisance components. Technical details on run information and the low-dimensional exponential identity are provided in the Supplementary Materials. The important point is that this reduction changes neither the covariance model nor the meaning of the aging operators. Section 4 now turns those 13 operators into quantities that describe the magnitude and organization of brain aging. 

# 4 Quantifying longitudinal covariance aging 

The model estimates covariance change through symmetric operators, but those operators are expressed relative to a reference covariance. To interpret aging biologically, we translate the fitted trajectory back to the covariance scale. Whole-brain measures tell us whether the two age effects agree, but not where their differences arise. We therefore use the seven-network Schaefer partition (Schaefer et al., 2018) to group covariance changes by functional system. For each network pair, we estimate the share of longitudinal covariance-change energy and assess the alignment of longitudinal and between-person changes within that block. This distinguishes changes within networks from changes between networks and identifies the systems contributing most to global disagreement. The network summaries are calculated from the same posterior covariance derivatives used for the whole-brain analysis. They are therefore descriptive decompositions of one fitted model, rather than separate network models or thousands of connection-level tests. The derivative and network summaries are defined using the fitted covariance trajectory. 

# 5 Bayesian inference and theoretical properties 

Bayesian inference is particularly useful here because the covariance state at each visit is uncertain and the direction of longitudinal aging is learned rather than specified in advance. We combine the calibrated run-level quasi-likelihood with priors on the age effects and nuisance components. Smooth age-basis coefficients allow the longitudinal operator to evolve gradually, while ordered shrinkage favors a small number of reproducible aging directions. Separate centered priors on participant and visit effects accommodate variation that is not part of the systematic aging trajectory. The result is a generalized Bayesian posterior over covariance trajectories and their age-dependent operators. Posterior draws are propagated through the covariance-scale calculations in Section 4 to obtain estimates and credible intervals for alignment, rate, effective rank, and network summaries. Because the updating criterion is a calibrated quasi-likelihood rather than an exact sampling likelihood, these are generalized Bayesian credible intervals. Their frequentist coverage is assessed separately in the simulation study. This distinction is 15 important: calibration of run-level covariance information is intended to improve uncertainty quantification, but it does not by itself guarantee nominal coverage for nonlinear summaries. Our main structural result is that the model always produces valid covariance matrices. This follows directly from the matrix exponential representation.

The DLBS application returns to the biological question that motivated the model: whether the covariance pattern associated with age across people is the same pattern expressed as people themselves age. The analysis included 50 participants observed at three visits, for 150 visits and 252 resting-state runs spanning ages 22–96 years. All validated run-level time-series matrices contained the same 100 Schaefer cortical parcels. Runs retained 89–180 volumes after censoring and nuisance processing. The analysis used percent signal change rather than z-standardization so that covariance amplitude remained part of the target. The run-level sample covariance matrices were themselves structured, but their dominant pooled directions were not used to define the longitudinal aging span. An overview of the age-specific posterior covariance-aging patterns is shown in Figure 1B, with the individual inferential summaries examined below. Repeated runs were important for separating uncertainty in an observed scan from genuine visit-to-visit covariance change. Temporal autocorrelation reduced the nominal covariance information substantially, and the additional repeated-run calibration gave bϕ = 3 .5805. Under this correction, the mean effective covariance information was 17.45 per run. Earlier posterior covariance checks under the same statistical hierarchy gave a pooled standardized residual standard deviation close to one and approximately 94% coverage by nominal 95% covariance-moment intervals, supporting the scale of the calibrated quasi-likelihood before the biological functionals were interpreted.

## 6.2 The rate of covariance reorganization is greatest near midlife 

The global reorganization rate RW (a) showed a smooth, nonmonotone trajectory (Figure 3). Its posterior mean increased from 0.023 at age 30 to approximately 0.027 at ages 50–60 and then declined to 0.022 by age 90. The result does not support a picture in which whole-brain covariance simply changes progressively faster with advancing age. Instead, the estimated rate is greatest around midlife and attenuates thereafter. Because RW (a) is a norm, its positive credible interval is not interpreted as a formal test against an atom at zero; the scientific information is in the age-varying magnitude and its uncertainty.

26 7 Discussion 

The central finding is that the covariance organization associated with age across people is not, especially in early and middle adulthood, the same as the covariance trajectory followed within individuals. This distinction is more than a technical separation of two regression effects. It changes the biological interpretation of lifespan connectivity. Population age signatures can be informative markers of age while still failing to describe the direction in which an individual’s functional organization changes over time. By estimating the two directions separately, the analysis makes their agreement or divergence an empirical question rather than an assumption. The DLBS results show that this divergence is structured across the lifespan. The within-person and between-person covariance directions are negatively aligned through approximately age 60, after which the discrepancy weakens. At the same time, the magnitude of longitudinal covariance reorganization is greatest around midlife rather than increasing monotonically into old age. These findings suggest that functional brain aging is not well summarized by a single slope or by a fixed network signature. The trajectory changes in both magnitude and orientation, with the most pronounced reorganization occurring at different stages of adulthood rather than simply at the oldest ages. The low effective dimension of the systematic longitudinal signal adds an important complementary result. Only a few coordinated directions are needed to describe reproducible covariance aging, even though covariance variation among individual scans is much richer. This concentration does not imply a fixed low-dimensional aging pattern. Nearby ages have highly similar operators, but similarity decreases gradually over wider age separations, indicating smooth reorientation across adulthood. The biological picture is therefore one of a parsimonious but evolving process: aging is organized, yet the organization through which it is expressed is not static. Network localization shows why the whole-operator perspective matters. Roughly three quarters of off-diagonal longitudinal aging energy is carried by covariance between functional 27 systems. The disagreement between longitudinal and cross-sectional aging is especially visual-centered in early and middle adulthood, involving relationships of the visual system with default, control, dorsal-attention, and limbic systems. Much of the global discrepancy attenuates later in life, but visual–limbic covariance remains strongly discordant even at age 90. A global summary alone would miss this persistence. Conversely, an edgewise analysis would fragment a coordinated system-level pattern into many separate tests. The operator decomposition preserves the global biological question while showing where the disagreement is expressed. The model connects these findings to uncertainty in the finite BOLD runs while allowing the directions of covariance aging to be learned from longitudinal observations. The results also refine how cross-sectional studies of brain aging should be interpreted. Cross-sectional signatures remain valuable for describing population differences and for identifying age-associated organization. The present analysis does not invalidate those findings. Instead, it shows that their biological meaning should not automatically be extended to within-person change. The distinction is most consequential when the scientific aim is mechanistic—to understand how functional organization changes as a person ages—or when cross-sectional patterns are used to motivate trajectories, intervention windows, or markers of progression. The proposed framework also has applications beyond functional neuroimaging. Across biomedical research, repeated measurements often capture complex relationships among bio-logical signals that evolve over time. Similar challenges arise in electrophysiology, physiological monitoring, wearable sensor data, and molecular studies, where the goal is to understand how patterns of dependence change within individuals. In these settings, covariance must be estimated from limited observations, and changes observed within an individual may differ from associations found across a population. By accounting for these differences, our approach offers a way to study how biological systems reorganize over time while preserving the underlying dependence structure. 28 Acknowledgments
