A B S T R A C T
Surface-based cortical analysis is useful in many applications as it is a natural way to model the highly
folded structure of the cerebral cortex, allowing quantities such as curvature and thickness to be easily
calculated. Existing deep learning (DL) methods have focused on 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1-weighted (T1w)
scans, thus replicating classical pipelines. Here we present a neural network that is trained exclusively
on synthetic scans with random contrast and resolution. Consequently, it can be applied to almost
any kind of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan. In addition, our model provides vertex-wise
uncertainty estimates of the predicted surface as well as a spherical registration to fsaverage space.
Our model performs on par with other state-of-the-art DL methods on 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1w scans and
outperforms the only other method capable of estimating cortical surfaces from low resolution, non-
T1w scans. The predicted uncertainty, moreover, correlates with overall scan quality on low resolution
scans and highlights areas of poor surface reconstruction on 1×1×1 mm3 T1w scans, suggesting that it
can be used for quality control. Finally, the predicted spherical registrations prove comparable to other
DL and classical registration methods. These qualities, when combined with the fast inference time,
will provide the ability to analyze large, existing databases of clinical MRI scans, with the potential
for finding disease-specific patterns that would otherwise be infeasible in a research setting.
1. Introduction
Surface-based analysis has become a central framework
for studying the structure and function of the human cortex.
Reconstruction of the white matter (WM) and gray matter
(GM) (pial) surfaces provides an anatomically informed
coordinate system for representing and analyzing functional
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data, and enables mor-
phometric quantification of the cortical sheet. Seemingly
simple morphometric quantities, such as the thickness, cur-
vature, or surface area, have provided profound insights into
cortical development in health and disease. For example, one
representative result is the link between cortical thinning
and dementia, which is strong enough that cortical thick-
ness estimates can be used to predict conversion from mild
cognitive impairment to Alzheimer’s disease with a 76%
accuracy [42]. Localized changes in thickness, curvature
and surface area have also been linked to autism spectrum
disorder [38, 12], schizophrenia [48] and temporal lobe
epilepsy [32]. The cortical geometry is also linked to the un-
derlying microstructural properties, including cytoarchitec-
ture, myeloarchitecture and intracortical lamination [17, 41].
Despite the usefulness of morphometric measures, cur-
rent surface reconstruction tools are primarily tailored to

high-quality, research-grade MRI scans with T1-weighted

cally have thick slices and do not include a high-resolution,
isotropic T1w scan, or ex vivo acquisitions, which typically
deviate significantly from standard T1w and clinical scans
in terms of resolution, contrast, and spatial coverage. Ex-
tending cortical analysis to these types of datasets would,
on the one hand, enable large-scale studies of how cortical
measures relate to disease symptoms using already existing
clinical data, and, on the other hand, allow zooming into the
connection between cortical geometry and microstructure
using high-resolution ex vivo data. In this paper, we aim to
widen the scope of surface-based cortical analysis by intro-
ducing a model that can reconstruct surfaces from diverse
MRI data and provides automated uncertainty estimates to
highlight potential reconstruction errors.
Surface reconstruction of high-quality MRI scans.
Classical surface reconstruction approaches such as FreeSurfer [16],
BrainSuite [46], Caret [50], BrainVoyager [22], CAT [8],
and Civet [36] rely on iterative optimization and geometry
processing algorithms with carefully tuned hyperparame-
ters to extract surfaces from an input MRI scan. These
methods work well and are thoroughly tested but have
long run times due to their iterative nature and the need
for topology correction. A representative example of the

classical surface extraction process is FreeSurfer’s widely
used recon-all pipeline, which consists of several steps:
first, a segmentation of the WM is created and the boundary
is tessellated to create an initial white matter surface, next
the surface is corrected for topological errors, e.g., bridges
or holes, to ensure the surface is continuously deformable
to a sphere [15], then the surface is locally deformed to
match the intensity gradient of WM-GM-border visible in
the input MRI scan, and finally the optimized WM surface
is deformed outwards to fit the GM surface while monitoring
and fixing self-intersections [9].
Recently, deep learning (DL)-based models have emerged
as an attractive alternative to the classical approaches,
and are usually trained on their output. One of the main
benefits of moving from an optimization-based approach to a
learning-based approach is that the reconstruction time can
be reduced from hours to seconds. The current DL-based
methods can be roughly divided into two main categories:
implicit and explicit approaches.
Implicit methods estimate a function that implicitly de-
fines a surface. This function can be evaluated on the image
grid and may reflect belief about a tissue class, e.g., WM, in
a certain voxel. This is the approach used in FastSurfer [27],
where the speed-up is obtained by predicting a volumetric
segmentation using a convolutional neural network (CNN).
The segmentation is subsequently processed using steps
similar to those in the classical approaches to obtain the sur-
face representations. Instead of predicting a segmentation,
it is also possible to predict a real-valued signed distance
function (SDF) whose zero level-set defines a surface (e.g.,
the WM-GM interface) implicitly [24]. This can be extended
to arbitrary grid points, e.g., using hyper-columns [7]. Im-
portantly, all implicit methods require a subsequent surface
extraction step (e.g., using marching cubes) [35]. The result-
ing surface, although accurately placed, is potentially subject
to the same topological errors as the classical approaches and
post-processing is typically necessary.
Explicit methods estimate a surface directly by learning
to deform a template surface representation to fit the input
image [55, 5, 29, 45]. They typically consist of two parts:
a CNN that extracts a set of feature maps from the input
images, and a graph convolutional neural network (GCN)
that estimates deformation vectors by sampling the feature
maps at relevant locations. One of the main benefits of the
explicit methods, as opposed to the implicit ones, is that the
predicted surface is guaranteed to share its topology with the
template surface. Consequently, if the template is topolog-
ically correct, so are the predicted surfaces, although geo-
metrical errors such as self-intersecting faces (SIF) within
and between surfaces can still occur. Additionally, explicit
methods require that the template surface is co-registered to
the input image in a preprocessing step.
Extension to clinical data. Broadening the scope of sur-
face reconstruction tools from 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1w scans to
clinical data is challenging because the acquisition param-
eters are not standardized in clinical examinations, which
results in MRI scans with varying resolution and contrast.
In other medical image analysis tasks, such as segmenta-
tion [4], registration [28], and super-resolution [31], the
capacity for CNNs to generalize has been successfully ex-
panded by using a domain randomization approach where
the training data is synthesized from label images and no
real MRI scans are needed [25]. When the model is trained
using synthetic data, the CNNs generalize well to unseen
data without the need for retraining. This approach is used
in [24] where the authors present recon-all-clinical (RAC),
which is a variation of FreeSurfer’s recon-all pipeline aimed
at processing scans of arbitrary contrast and resolution.
RAC predicts an SDF for each cortical surface. The surface
representation is then extracted from the SDF using the same
classical surface fitting steps as in recon-all. Because of the
surface extraction steps, the average runtime of RAC is 3 to 4
hours on a standard desktop [24]. As far as we are aware, RAC
is the only tool that can currently do surface reconstructions
from clinical MRI data.
A critical challenge when applying surface extraction
tools to clinical data, or to data of varying quality in general,
is that it can be difficult to assess accuracy of the estimated
surfaces. The MRI data quality can vary both within a
scan, e.g., when the scan has high in-plane resolution but
large slice thickness, and across scans, e.g., due to motion
artifacts. Consequently, having a direct measure of surface
placement uncertainty would be extremely valuable, espe-
cially when processing large datasets where manual quality
control becomes unfeasible. In [26], the authors extend RAC
with uncertainty estimation based on the difference between
the SDF estimated by the CNN and the final SDF computed
from the extracted surface. Thus, the estimate does not
directly reflect surface placement uncertainty due to the data
(aleatoric uncertainty), but mostly highlights areas where
extensive geometric processing was needed when extracting
the surface from the initial SDF. This approximation scheme
is necessary to reduce computation, as directly estimating
the surface placement uncertainty would require running the
surface extraction step, which takes approximately 2 hours,
multiple times from different SDF samples.
Contribution. Here, we present a standalone tool for cor-
tical surface reconstruction and spherical registration with
integrated uncertainty estimation, which runs in less than 1 s
on a GPU, and works on heterogeneous input scans ranging
from low resolution clinical scans to high resolution research
scans. This work extends our preliminary results presented at
the International Workshop on Machine Learning in Medical
Imaging [40] in several aspects:
• We present extensive comparisons against other state-
of-the-art deep learning surface reconstruction tools
on 15 different datasets consisting of high-resolution,
research-quality 1×1×1 mm3 T1w scans, and against
RAC on low resolution clinical datasets.
• We extend the model to predict an estimate of surface
placement uncertainty, and we demonstrate that this

2. Methods
Our goal is to estimate cortical surfaces (WM and GM)
from any MRI scan of the brain. Specifically, given an input
image and a template surface we seek a model that deforms
the vertices of the template surface so as to fit the input
image. To simplify the theoretical description, we make
a couple of assumptions. First, the input image should be
oriented such that the linear part of its affine transformation
(from voxel to world space) is a 3 × 3 identity matrix (i.e.,
right-anterior-superior (RAS) orientation, 1×1×1 mm3 vox-
els). Second, the template surface, which exists in Montreal
Neurological Institute (MNI) space, must be aligned with the
input image beforehand. In practice, these two preprocessing
steps are automatically handled by our method at inference
time.

2.2.3. Implementation
Following [29] (with minor modifications), we model
𝑓in eq. (1) using a CNN with a UNet architecture [44].
The UNet has five levels (encoder channels 16, 32, 64, 128,
256; decoder channels 128, 64, 32, 16) between which we
do max-pooling with a kernel size of 2 in the encoder and
upsampling with a factor of 2 in the decoder. At each level,
3 × 3 × 3 convolutions are applied followed by instance
normalization and parametric rectified linear unit (PReLU)
activation. We use the feature maps from all levels of the
decoder.
To estimate the WM surface, we perform integration of
the quantities of interest (vertex position, vertex position
uncertainty, and spherical coordinates) using a multiscale
approach where we iteratively update and upsample these
quantities. The initial template has 62 vertices (scale 𝑛= 1)
and the final surface prediction has 245,762 vertices (scale
𝑛= 7). To upsample from scale 𝑛to 𝑛+ 1, we insert a new
vertex at the midpoint of each edge, thus splitting each face
into four new faces. Because of this multiscale approach, we
have not only a single function 𝑔in eq. (3) but one for each
scale, 𝑔𝑛, all of which are modeled with a GCN with a UNet-
like architecture which has a maximum depth of four levels
(encoder channels 96, 96, 96, 96, max-pooling; decoder
channels 96, 96, 96, max-unpooling). For example, when
𝑛= 1 (coarsest scale) there is only one level ({1}) and when
𝑛= 7 (finest scale) there are four levels ({7, 6, 5, 4, 5, 6, 7}).
The graph convolutions are implemented on the edges to im-
mediate neighbors [53] followed by instance normalization
and PReLU activation. Finally, a graph convolution layer is
used to estimate the deformation vectors. To summarize, we

the uncertainties have achieved sensible values, we use these

overview of the loss weights and scheduling can be found

The training is done on an Nvidia RTX 6000 GPU
(48 GB RAM) using the AdamW optimizer (𝜆= 0.01; 𝛽1 =

ther to 2.5×10−5 after 60,000 gradient steps. We use a batch
size of 1 and train until the standard chamfer distance on
the validation set plateaus which was approximately 80,000
gradient steps (≈24 hours). The model is implemented in
PyTorch 2 [2] and uses the PyTorch-Ignite framework [21]
for training. To speed up model convergence, we start by
training a model from scratch on T1w scans. The image
feature extractor (𝑓in eq. (1)) of the model using synthetic
data is then pretrained using a mean squared error loss of
the feature maps (comparing the features from the to-be-
trained network on a synthetic scan and the features from the
pretrained network on a corresponding T1w image). This is
fast (≈6 hours) and, in our experience, makes training more
stable, especially when training on low-resolution, synthetic
data. Once trained, the inference time is approximately 0.6
seconds on a GPU.
At inference time, we need to register the template mesh
(which exists in MNI space) to the input MRI scan. There-
fore, inspired by the DeepSurfer package [29], we train a
small network that performs an affine registration of a brain
MRI scan to MNI standard space. This network is trained
on synthetic data with resolution augmentation and can be
run before the main surface prediction network to align the
template to the input scan.

2.3. Experiments
2.3.1. Datasets
We train our model on multiple datasets (see table 3)
all of which include high-resolution (near 1 mm isotropic)
T1w scans. All scans were resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3
and processed with FreeSurfer 7.4.1 to obtain the WM
and GM surfaces and a brain segmentation. To generate
synthetic MRI scans (see section 2.2.1), we simplify the
volume segmentation by merging structures with similar
intensity profiles (e.g., amygdala, cortex, hippocampus), and
model the extracerebral tissues using 𝑘-means clustering.
The cortical surfaces, which are the training targets, were
resampled to the template surface, which the model learns to
deform. For training, we coregister the template to the input
scan by calculating an affine transformation that minimizes
the squared distance between the template and the surface
of that particular subject. Since all surfaces were resampled
to the template, we solve for the 4 × 4 matrix which, when
applied to the template points, minimizes the difference
between the template and the target points (augmented with a
column of ones) in a least squares sense. The training dataset
is split in train, test, and validation subsets with fractions
0.8, 0.1, and 0.1. All datasets were subjected to manual
quality control which led to some subjects being excluded
(see table 3).
To quantify the performance between models trained
on synthetic and real data, we train three different models.
The first model, denoted T1w-1mm, is trained on 1 × 1 ×
1 mm3 T1w scans; the second model, denoted synth-1mm,
is trained on 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 synthetic scans; and the third
model, denoted synth-random, is trained on synthetic scans
of random resolution.

2.4.2. Surface Uncertainty
To evaluate the uncertainty estimates from our model,
we correlate them with the surface reconstruction errors
that we observe when comparing with the surfaces from
FreeSurfer’s recon-all pipeline. Note that although our
model provides an uncertainty estimate along each axis of
Euclidean space (𝑥, 𝑦, and 𝑧) (see eq. (8)), here we always
use the 𝑙2 norm over these values, and this is what is referred
to by “uncertainty” in the following.
To estimate surface reconstruction errors of models ap-
plied to scans with a resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, we
use the distance from a vertex on the predicted surface
to the closest point on the reference surface. To estimate
surface placement errors of models applied to scans of low
resolution (specifically, the 1 × 1 × 5 mm3 fluid-attenuated
inversion recovery (FLAIR) scans from ADNI GO-2), we
map both predicted and target surfaces to the sphere, register
the target surface to the predicted surface, resample the latter
to the former, and finally compute distances between the
corresponding vertices2. The reason for doing this is that
when the errors are large, the closest point on the reference
surface may not be meaningful in the sense that this is not
the point where that vertex was “supposed” to be (e.g., in
the wrong sulcus). This is particularly problematic when
correlating with uncertainty and is illustrated in fig. 3.
To investigate if the uncertainty estimate can inform us
about the general quality of a surface reconstruction for
a given subject, we compare mean values of uncertainty
and error (over the whole cortex) between subjects. To
investigate if the uncertainty estimate is useful for localizing
areas of poor surface accuracy within a subject, we correlate
uncertainty estimates with errors on a per-vertex level. How-
ever, since we are interested in localizing patches of poor
reconstruction (e.g., a gyral crown) rather than individual

In table 4, we quantify surface accuracy and quality of
our method, DeepSurfer, and V2C-Flow. Unsurprisingly, we
see that the model trained specifically on 1×1×1 mm3 T1w
scans performs better than the models trained on synthetic
scans when applied to this kind of data. In terms of mesh
quality, our models produce surfaces with triangles of more
equal size and shape compared to DeepSurfer and V2C-

We also show results of applying our synth-random
model to 1×1×5 mm3 FLAIR from the ADNI GO-2 dataset.
As expected, the surface accuracy decreases compared to
predicting on 1×1×1 mm3 T1w scans, although the error is
still below the image resolution (∼0.70 mm) and lower than
that of RAC (∼0.94 mm). The mesh quality metrics, however,
are comparable to those obtained on 1×1×1 mm3 T1w data.
Table 4 suggests that overall generalization performance
of our model on unseen 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1w scans is
similar to that of the test set. However, it may still be
interesting to explore performance on individual datasets
to investigate whether the model is biased towards certain
image characteristics, demographics, or scanning hardware.
Hence, in the supplementary material we show ASSD per
dataset for the test and generalization sets. Performance is
stable across datasets albeit slightly lower for synth-1mm
and synth-random models on 2015 International Symposium
in Biomedical Imaging (ISBI2015) which is a small dataset
containing lesions where the FreeSurfer-based ground truth
is not particularly accurate (see section 4 for more details).
Lastly, we show the results of applying our models as
well as RAC to two ex vivo samples. Performance is lower than
on in vivo data but our models still outperform RAC on almost
all metrics.

4. Discussion
We have presented a DL model that predicts (1) cortical
surfaces, (2) an estimate of the uncertainty associated with
location of each vertex on the surfaces, and (3) coordinates
on the fsaverage sphere corresponding to each vertex. We

scans, one on 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 synthetic scans, and one on

4.1. Surface Accuracy and Quality
In terms of surface reconstruction accuracy, our T1w-
1mm model performs similarly to (or better than) other DL
methods and the errors are similar across test and general-
ization set suggesting that it generalizes well to unseen data.
The synth-random model performs better than RAC and we
note that it is significantly faster taking only about 1 s on a
GPU whereas RAC has a runtime of a few hours (admittedly,
RAC returns everything that recon-all does, however, the
runtime is dominated by surface postprocessing).

We note that the lack of ground truth surfaces against
which to quantify the error warrants a certain level of caution
when interpreting these metrics. In particular, the common
strategy of using FreeSurfer as reference, which can only
be considered a silver standard in this regard, may some-
times be problematic. This becomes increasingly relevant
as DL models get more accurate and sometimes outperform
FreeSurfer. When visually inspecting the results, we have
seen many examples where the estimate of a particular DL
model is (locally) better than FreeSurfer (e.g., around a
WM hypointensities). We illustrate this in fig. 11 and it is
our impression that this is a general feature of our models
compared to FreeSurfer, namely that they tend to be more
robust to abnormalities in the scans, perhaps because they
have learned priors over shapes of sulci and gyri. On the
other hand, it is also possible to find areas where FreeSurfer
performs best and quantifying these findings is not trivial.
Thus, whether an ASSD of 0.13 mm is actually better than
an ASSD of 0.15 mm may not be easy to say. This ambiguity
is not a major concern when quantifying the performance on
low resolution scans since a surface estimated from a 1×1×
5 mm3 FLAIR scan will almost surely be less accurate than
a FreeSurfer prediction on an accompanying 1 × 1 × 1 mm3
T1w scan.
From table 4, it is evident that the models trained on
synthetic data generally perform worse when applied to
T1w scans than a model trained on this particular contrast.
Consequently, the comparison to FreeSurfer-based ground-
truth is less of an issue for the synthetically trained models.
To explore why we see this performance gap, we have tried
to explicitly model partial volume effects during data syn-
thesis (using signed distances between a voxel and cortical
surfaces; see [40] for a more detailed description). However,
this did not make a difference compared to the approach used
here, where we simply rely on image smoothing to create
partial volume effects. Another reason for this performance
gap could be differences in general image statistics (e.g.,
unrealistic noise model), but this is beyond the scope of this
work. However, it should be noted that the performance is
still well below the resolution of the scans.
In addition to high anatomical accuracy, we see from
table 4 that our methods produce high quality meshes; both
in terms of triangle shape and size as well as low number
of intersecting triangles. We visualize the surface meshes
for the different models for a randomly selected sulcus
in the supplementary material. This may be an attractive
feature in many scenarios. For example, interpolating layers
between WM and GM surfaces will be straightforward if
the boundary surfaces are smooth. Likewise, high quality
meshes may lend themselves more easily to subsequent mesh
processing (e.g., remeshing) and improve numerical stability
if used in for example finite element modeling.
In table 4, we also show the results from applying our
models to two ex vivo cases, namely data from the BigBrain
project and the MRI scan from Edlow et al. [13]. The
reference surfaces were obtained from [41] and using the
method from [57], respectively. Performance is decreased
compared to the in vivo data, however, it is still acceptable
and demonstrates the flexibility of the models, and the
approach in general, despite ex vivo scans not being the
focus of the current work. We speculate that by tweaking
the augmentation scheme slightly towards ex vivo cases, the
models should be able to handle these cases much better.
For example, our current models were trained exclusively
on full-brain scans, but single hemisphere augmentation is
straightforward.
4.2. Surface Uncertainty
We evaluated the value of the uncertainty estimates
produced by our models by correlating them with observed
surface placement errors. We found that (1) uncertainty
averaged over the entire cortex is a good predictor of overall
surface accuracy when applied to low resolution scans,
whereas (2) vertex-wise uncertainty estimates are able to
highlight areas of potentially problematic surface recon-
struction within a subject (especially for the GM surface)
on high resolution scans.
Evaluating the predicted uncertainties of the models is
difficult since we do not have a target for this quantity which
is why we do it indirectly by correlating uncertainty and
observed reconstruction error. However, as discussed above,
FreeSurfer surfaces can only be considered a silver standard
in this regard making it difficult to determine if
1. low uncertainty but high error is actually a false neg-
ative (where our model fails to recognize a potential
error) or if our model is actually correct whereas
FreeSurfer is not (thus justifying a low uncertainty),
and
2. high uncertainty but low error is actually a false posi-
tive because both models are correct (although if both
models happen to be correct despite high level of
noise in the image this might still be okay) or both

An additional feature of the uncertainty estimates of
our models is that they are generally biased towards high
uncertainty in certain regions (e.g., insula, temporal pole
area, orbitofrontal area, occipital area) as is clear from fig. 8.
The reason for this probably depends on the region. For
example, the contrast in T1w scans is often not good in
insula, orbitofrontal and temporal areas and so recon-all
may be less reliable here (thus increasing arbitrary variation
in the training data), whereas the occipital area is simply
difficult because it consists of many small gyri and sulci. On
the other hand, from fig. 11 it is also clear that the uncertainty
estimates are sensitive to local features in the image. To
illustrate this, fig. 12 demonstrates this in a controlled setting
where we show that increasing the amount of simulated
noise in a small area of an image increases the (local)
uncertainty estimate of the model. Indeed, from looking
at uncertainty overlaid on the surfaces for many subjects,
it is our general impression that the uncertainty estimate
is almost always high in areas that have poor contrast or
are somehow abnormal (e.g., pathology). In addition, when
severe reconstruction errors occur, we have seen that this is
often highlighted by the uncertainty estimate.
Lastly, we acknowledge the importance for models to
provide well-calibrated uncertainty estimates. This is an
active area of research [58] and while we have not performed
a formal calibration of our uncertainty estimates, mainly be-
cause we have no ground truth data against which to perform
such calibration, we believe that our results demonstrate
the usefulness of our uncertainty estimates, e.g., for quality
control or downstream applications.
4.3. Surface Registration
Our methods generally provide accurate spherical regis-
trations that are similar to those from CAT12 and JOSA. To
evaluate the quality of our predicted spherical registration,
we used the standard FreeSurfer pipeline as reference for cal-
culating vertex-wise accuracy. Although manual inspection
of a subset of the registrations suggested that it performs well
in the vast majority of cases, and thus is suitable as reference,
it is worth noting that this is not a gold standard.
From fig. 10 (left), we see a few more cases with low
accuracy for CAT12 and our method compared to JOSA.
These outliers mostly reflect disagreement on where to lo-
cate the pre- and postcentral gyri. Interestingly, CAT12 and
our approach generally agree, although manual inspection
suggested that FreeSurfer is correct in these cases. Given
that JOSA uses the same rigid transformation for the initial
registration as the standard pipeline from FreeSurfer, it is not
surprising that we do not find major disagreements between
these methods. On the other hand, the average error of JOSA
is higher than CAT12 and our predictions.
Additionally, we observed greater variability when the
registration methods were applied to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1w
scans compared to 1 × 1 × 5 mm3 FLAIR scans. This
is consistent across all methods and is likely because the
surfaces predicted from the former scans generally capture
more details than the latter, hence increasing the possibility
of the methods settling in different local minima.
To our knowledge, the only other DL method that does
simultaneous cortical surface reconstruction and spherical
registration was presented by [43]. However, since we were
unable to find parameters for this model, we have not been
able to do a proper comparison. Additionally, since the
registration is tied to the predicted surface, it is not pos-
sible to apply either method to the surface prediction of
the other. In [43], the authors find average Dice scores
of approximately 0.92 which is impressive, although not
directly comparable to our metric of parcellation accuracy.
However, since resampling a surface onto the fsaverage
template tends to degrade smoothness and triangle quality
(e.g., shear triangles; see supplementary material for an ex-
ample), we speculate that the mesh quality may be negatively
affected when driving the training exclusively with a vertex
correspondence loss.
Finally, our approach and that of [43] are different from
most other DL methods that are trained only to perform

4.4. Limitations
There are a number of limitations relevant to this work
that are worth highlighting. Regarding the models them-
selves, we note the following:
• The prediction accuracy and surface quality from our
models are on average high, but there are no guaran-
tees or upper bounds on the errors, and the models
may fail if applied to data that are outside of the
training data distribution. For example, although we
have found that our models are quite robust to white
matter hypo- and hyperintensities, the accuracy of the
surfaces in the presence of (major) cortical abnormal-
ities or artifacts remains to be evaluated.
• Our models expect an input image with 1 × 1 ×
1 mm3 voxels as inputs. If the input scan has a lower
resolution, it will be upsampled, however, if it has
a higher resolution it will need to be downsampled.
Consequently, the networks will not benefit from sub-
millimeter resolution.
• In general, the data synthesis pipeline used for training
the “synth” models does not generate realistic MRI
scans. Rather, we rely on generating a sufficiently
broad distribution of data that will encompass the
cases encountered in reality. This approach has been
successfully used in many other applications (e.g.,
[4, 24, 25]). However, we observed that in some cases,
surfaces may be consistently over- or underestimated
by a fraction of a voxel. We have not been able to
pin down the exact reason(s), but it is evident that
the noise model used here (Gaussian noise) does not
reflect the true noise characteristics in MRI scans.
Another set of limitations relate not to the models themselves
but to their evaluation because of the lack of a ground truth
data:
• As mentioned above, surface estimates from FreeSurfer
cannot be considered ground truth and therefore dif-
ferences in ASSD below a certain threshold may
be meaningless. At the very least, one should be
cautious about putting too much emphasis on small
differences between models. We excluded subjects
where FreeSurfer predictions were very bad. How-
ever, caution has to be exercised with this approach as
we may be excluding scans in a biased way (e.g., cases
that are somehow at the tail of the distribution) thus
limiting the generalization performance of the trained
model. However, this may not be a major concern
as we found the generalization performance of our
models to be on par with the performance on the test
set.
• We do believe that the predicted uncertainty estimates
can be useful for example for quality control purposes.
However, as we have tried to stress, it can be difficult
to quantify its exact accuracy and value (this ties back
to the previous point).
• Regarding the registration to fsaverage, FreeSurfer is
also not a ground truth although in our experience it
tends to perform well.
Finally, to ensure stability during training on synthetic data,
we chose to pretrain the models to match the features from
the T1w model. One limitation of this approach is that the
final number of feature channels from the UNet must match.
An alternative strategy is to stabilize the training using a
matched vertex loss in the initial training phase [29] although
this is generally slower.

5. Conclusions
Here we have presented three DL models, which, given
an input brain scan, return (1) cortical surfaces, (2) uncer-
tainty estimates, and (3) spherical registration to fsaverage
associated with said cortical surfaces. We have trained a
model specifically on 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 T1w scans but also a
model that can handle scans of any contrast and resolution
(e.g., clinical scans). Our approach is very fast and, if avail-
able on the control PC of a scanner, could allow immediate
surface reconstruction with uncertainty estimates as well as
mapping of cortical atlases on a subject during a scanning
session to help identify areas of potential interest.
We provide a command line interface which takes an in-
put scan and produces the above output without the need for
manual preprocessing or registration. The code is available
at https://github.com/simnibs/brainnet.

The effect of resampling a surface onto the fsaverage
template
In fig. 5, we illustrate the negative effect of resampling a white matter surface
(from FreeSurfer) to fsaverage space. This clearly shows how sheared triangles
are introduced. In the main text, we speculate that training exclusively with
matched point loss on such surfaces (i.e., fig. 5b) might have a negative effect
on overall mesh quality.
