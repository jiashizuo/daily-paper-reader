ABSTRACT 

Incomplete field-of-view (FOV) diffusion MRI (dMRI) acquisitions compromise whole-brain white matter analysis by excluding brain regions from tractography and connectivity studies. While deep generative models for dMRI FOV extension have been proposed, their generalizability remains limited by the acquisition-dependent nature of dMRI, where signal intensity varies with b-value, gradient direction, and scanner protocol, and the inherent 4D complexity of the data, making it difficult to train models that transfer across sites or acquisition schemes without retraining. We propose a flow-matching generative model that extends the dMRI FOV in both the superior and inferior directions by predicting apparent diffusion coefficients (ADC) in six directions, conditioned on the two nearest acquired slices and the full-FOV T1w image to enforce both local diffusion continuity and anatomical consistency. The ADC representation decouples the model from acquisition-specific b-values and gradient directions, providing a middle-ground across protocols. The model was trained on 3,103 subjects (5,105 sessions) across five different datasets spanning diverse acquisition schemes (b = 500 and 1000 s/mm²; 21–192 gradient directions), four single-shell (b = 1000) and one multi-shell (b = 500/1000). Evaluated on 299 subjects across five independent datasets spanning b = 750–1500 s/mm² and 32–90 gradient directions, with single- and multi-shell schemes unseen during training, the proposed method achieves significantly improved bundle Dice score on downstream tractography ( 𝑝 < 0.01 ). To facilitate reproducibility and adoption, we publicly release the trained model weights together with a containerized inference pipeline that performs automatic FOV extension from truncated dMRI and co-registered T1-weighted images [zenodo link will be provided for final submission].

1. INTRODUCTION 

Alterations in white matter have been reported across a wide range of neurological and psychiatric conditions, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) 1, traumatic brain injury 2,3 , delirium 4, bipolar disorder 5, and age-related cognitive decline 6. In particular, degradations in white matter integrity have been implicated early during AD and are shown to be associated with progressive cognitive impairment 7. Although macrostructural alterations, such as regional atrophy and brain volume loss, have been extensively characterized 8, growing evidence suggests that subtle microstructural abnormalities emerge during healthy aging and the earliest stages of neurodegeneration 9. Characterizing these microstructural alterations and their trajectories across the lifespan 10 is therefore essential for improving our understanding of brain aging, disease progression, and the mechanisms underlying cognitive decline. Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) enables non-invasive characterization of white matter microstructure by sensitizing the MR signal to the microscopic displacement of water molecules inside tissue 11 . The diffusion weighting is achieved by applying paired diffusion-sensitizing gradients, causing the measured signal attenuation to depend on the self-diffusion of water molecules 12 . The degree of diffusion weighting is determined by the b-value, which depends on the strength, duration, and timing of the applied gradients 13 . In white matter, water diffusion is anisotropic due to the restrictive effects of axonal membranes and myelin, resulting in signal attenuation that depends on the orientation of the applied diffusion gradient relative to the underlying fiber architecture 14 . This orientational information estimated at each voxel can then be propagated throughout the brain using tractography to reconstruct white matter pathways enabling automated segmentation and analysis of anatomically defined fiber bundles with publicly available methods such as TractSeg 17 , BundleParc 18 and BrainstemSeg 19 .However, adequate angular and radial sampling requires acquiring multiple diffusion-weighted volumes, so dMRI acquisition times exceed those of a structural T1-weighted (T1w) scans 20 . To remain feasible, diffusion protocols generally adopt coarser spatial resolution and a more restricted field-of-view (FOV) compared to the accompanying T1w scans. The reduced coverage leaves little margin for error in slab placement, and variability in positioning, head size, or subject motion truncates the FOV at the superior or inferior extent of the brain, most often affecting the vertex and cerebellum 21 .Truncation removes the diffusion information required to reconstruct any streamline passing through the affected region, causing bundles that traverse those areas to terminate prematurely or be missed entirely during segmentation (Figure 1). Bundle-wise measures are then computed over incomplete pathways, introducing systematic bias and, in longitudinal or multi-site studies, variability unrelated to the biology of interest. Recovering the missing diffusion signal is essential for preserving tractography and unbiased bundle-wise microstructural analyses, motivating learning-based approaches for FOV extension. Deep generative models provide a promising route to recovering the missing diffusion signal. Unlike conventional anatomical images, however, diffusion MRI presents a unique representational challenge because acquisition protocols vary substantially across studies. Since the signal is jointly defined by its radial sampling (b-values) and angular sampling (gradient directions), measurements from different protocols differ in dimensionality, in the directions sampled, and in absolute scale. This protocol dependence has motivated acquisition-invariant representations of the diffusion signal. Golkov et al 22 . mapped raw q-space measurements to microstructural parameters, but the mapping remains tied to the sampling scheme it was trained on. Spherical harmonic expansions provide a continuous, fixed-length alternative and have been applied to cross-scanner harmonization 23 and FOD estimation 24 , though high-order expansions require dense angular sampling to be well-conditioned. Nath et al. 25 used a SHORE basis to obtain multi-shell b-value generalizability. Yao et al. 26 convert the measured signal to apparent diffusion coefficients and resample onto the vertices of an icosahedral tessellation by inverse-distance interpolation, showing that a single model can then operate across datasets with differing gradient schemes. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that suitable signal representations can substantially reduce dependence on acquisition-specific sampling schemes. Despite these advances in protocol-invariant signal representations, existing learning-based FOV extension methods for dMRI remain largely acquisition-specific. Chen et al. 27 parcellate tractography under incomplete FOV by augmenting training with synthetically truncated tractograms, allowing streamlines to be correctly labeled despite missing regions. The missing signal is never recovered, so the approach must be re-engineered for each downstream analysis and cannot restore microstructural measures in the affected territory. Gao et al. 21 synthesize missing diffusion-weighted volumes with a conditional pix2pix framework, mapping each volume independently using a co-registered T1w image as anatomical reference and Li et al. 28 extend this with a conditional variational U-Net that additionally conditions on the gradient table. Both are trained at a single diffusion weighting and require retraining for acquisitions with different radial sampling. As a result, existing approaches cannot be readily applied to the heterogeneous acquisition protocols commonly encountered in multi-site studies and clinical practice. In this work we propose a conditional flow matching model for dMRI FOV extension that is designed to generalize across acquisition protocols. We introduce a protocol-independent six-channel ADC representation obtained by fitting the diffusion tensor to the full acquisition and evaluating it along six canonical icosahedral directions, mapping any acquisition, at any b-value or gradient scheme, onto a common target of fixed dimensionality that is invertible back to the tensor. We then formulate FOV extension as conditional rectified flow, conditioning on the two nearest acquired ADC slices to enforce diffusion continuity and on the co-registered full-FOV T1w slice to supply the anatomy of the target region and extend autoregressively in either the superior or inferior direction without an explicit direction encoding. 

2. METHODS 

2.1 Data and preprocessing 

To evaluate whether FOV extension generalizes across diverse diffusion MRI acquisition protocols, we curated a training dataset spanning a broad range of angular and radial resolutions. The training set consists of 3,103 subjects across 5,105 4imaging sessions from five studies: Health and Aging Brain Study- Health Disparities (HABSHD) 29 , Indiana, KBASE 30 ,ROS/MAP/MARS 31 , and ABCD 32 . Ten percent of the training data was held out for model selection, with the split performed at the subject level rather than the session level to prevent data leakage across repeated imaging sessions. Generalization was evaluated on five studies entirely excluded from training, each with a distinct cohort and acquisition: HCP(healthy young adults, high angular and radial sampling), FloridaADRC and WRAP(aging and Alzheimer's disease), VUIIS-MS(multiple sclerosis; IRB 141337), and BRAIN2 (delirium and survivorship in critically ill patients; IRB 192003).These datasets encompass acquisition protocols with angular and radial resolutions that differ from those represented in the training set (Table 1). There was no subject overlap between the training and test sets, ensuring that performance reflects the model's ability to generalize to previously unseen subjects acquired under unfamiliar imaging protocols. Furthermore, the test set was curated to include only a single imaging session per subject. All diffusion MRI (dMRI) scans were preprocessed using the PreQual 36 preprocessing pipeline which performs denoising and accounts for susceptibility and eddy current artifacts and also accounts for motion. Following preprocessing, all scans underwent visual quality assurance according to the protocol described by Kim et al. 37,38 to ensure data quality prior to subsequent analyses.

2.6 Experimental setup 

We design our experiments with the goal to test the FOV extension in terms of ability to recover diffusion information that preserves downstream tractography enabled by the diffusion tensor model through white matter bundle segmentation. In addition to that, we evaluate generalizability by utilizing a separate set of dMRI datasets from acquisition protocols and studies that were entirely excluded from training. In practice, FOV truncation typically occurs in the diffusion acquisition, while the anatomical T1-weighted image remains available with full brain coverage. Therefore, during evaluation, the full-FOV T1-weighted image is provided to the model, while a portion of the diffusion volume is masked and subsequently reconstructed. Because our method performs FOV extension autoregressively, where each generated slice becomes part of the conditioning context for subsequent predictions, errors may accumulate as the extension distance increases. To characterize this behavior, we progressively increase the missing regions in both superior and inferior directions. Specifically, we remove (10, 20, 30, 40) mm of diffusion FOV and independently bundle segmentation at each extension distance. White matter bundle segmentation evaluation is performed using TractSeg 17 , which performs bundle segmentation from fiber orientation information. Because TractSeg operates on fiber orientation peaks rather than directly on diffusion tensors, we derive peaks from the reconstructed tensor by scaling the principal eigenvector ( 𝑣 ,) with FA. We 8compare bundle segmentations obtained from the complete ground-truth diffusion acquisition and from the FOV-extended reconstructions. Segmentation agreement is quantified using Dice similarity coefficients between the predicted and reference bundles. To determine whether FOV extension significantly affects tractography performance, we compare Dice scores obtained without FOV extension against those obtained after reconstruction using a paired t-test. 

3. RESULTS 

We report segmentation performance for the FOV extension task on seven representative TractSeg bundles spanning three anatomical regimes: two bundles confined to the inferior brain (inferior cerebellar peduncle, ICP; middle cerebellar peduncle, MCP), three traversing the full superior–inferior extent (corticospinal tract, CST; fronto-pontine tract, FPT; parieto-occipital pontine tract, POPT), and two confined to the superior brain (superior longitudinal fascicle I and II, SLF I and SLF II). For bundles localized to one end of the brain, truncation and extension were evaluated only in the corresponding direction, whereas full-extent bundles were evaluated under both superior and inferior truncation. Dice was computed against the segmentation obtained from the untruncated acquisition. FOV extension significantly improved Dice over the cutoff control for every bundle and every truncation extent evaluated after correcting for multiple comparisons ( 𝑝 < 0.01 ), and the magnitude of the benefit grows with truncation severity (Figure 4). At 10 mm, little anatomy is removed and the control already achieves median Dice above 0.9 for all bundles, so the gains, while significant, are small. By 20 mm, truncation visibly degrades the control with ICP and MCP median dice falling to 0.72 and 0.78 respectively, while extension holds every bundle at or above 0.9. At 30 mm the control deteriorates substantially, with extension recovering a median above 0.8 for every evaluated bundle. At the most severe truncation (40 mm), several bundles are frequently not segmented at all in the control: ICP has a median Dice near zero, and MCP and SLF I fall below 0.2. Even at this truncation level, the proposed method recovers usable segmentations, with median Dice above 0.8 for all bundles except ICP and MCP, which lie deepest within the inferior brainstem and are therefore furthest from the retained image context. Figure 5 shows the same trend qualitatively with bundles that are progressively clipped at the truncation plane under FOV cutoff are restored to close agreement with the ground truth after extension, with residual disagreement concentrated at the far edge of the synthesized region.

4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

In this work, we proposed and characterized a generalizable dMRI model for FOV extension. Rather than learning directly from diffusion-weighted signals whose dimensionality changes with the number of gradient directions, gradient orientations, and diffusion weightings, we first fit the diffusion tensor and project it onto six fixed icosahedral directions. This maps every acquisition onto the same six-channel ADC representation, allowing a single conditional flow model to operate across diverse protocols without retraining. We evaluated this protocol-independent representation on five independent test cohorts acquired under protocols entirely absent from training and found that the recovered FOV consistently preserved the diffusion information needed for downstream white matter analysis. Since the motivation for FOV extension is to enable downstream analyses rather than image completion itself, we focused our evaluation on tensor-derived microstructure and tractography instead of conventional image similarity metrics. Across increasing truncation levels, the reconstructed diffusion tensors remained sufficiently faithful to recover major white matter bundles, even when the truncated acquisition alone frequently failed. As expected for an autoregressive model, performance gradually degrades as the missing FOV increases because each synthesized slice becomes the context for the next. This effect is most apparent for bundles extending deep into the missing region and for superior FOV extension, where white matter anatomy becomes increasingly dispersed and variable. Nevertheless, even under the most severe truncations, the model reliably recovered the broad morphology of the underlying white matter pathways. The proposed framework also inherits the limitations of the diffusion tensor model used to construct the protocol-independent representation. Within this regime, the tensor provides a compact six-parameter representation that can be estimated from as few as six non-collinear diffusion measurements, making it broadly applicable across clinical and research datasets. Outside this regime, however, the representation becomes inadequate. Acquisitions collected exclusively at high b-values emphasize non-Gaussian diffusion that cannot be captured by a second-order tensor, while regions containing complex fiber configurations, such as crossing, fanning, or branching fibers, cannot be faithfully represented by a single dominant orientation. Consequently, the synthesized FOV is limited by the fidelity of the underlying tensor model rather than the conditional flow framework itself. Extending the same framework to fixed-dimensional representations derived from higher-order models, such as spherical harmonics or fiber orientation distributions (FODs), would remove these constraints while preserving protocol independence in future work. Additionally, future work should also validate the extension at the level of downstream analysis: whether microstructural measures recovered in the extended field of view preserve the expected associations with disease status and cognitive outcomes.
