# ABSTRACT 

Pretrained representations are increasingly used to share and reuse information from medical images without exposing the original scans, but it is unclear how much patient-specific information these representations retain. We investigate this question for brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) by evaluating five frozen pretrained encoders spanning different architectures, pretraining objec-tives, and training domains on two longitudinal T1-weighted datasets, OASIS-2 and MIRIAD. Under an attacker model with access to released embeddings, the corresponding public encoder, and auxiliary MRI scans, we evaluate patient link-age and sensitive-attribute inference. Across the evaluated representations, we observe substantial subject-specific information: rank-1 patient linkage reaches up to 99% on MIRIAD and exceeds 90% on OASIS-2 for the highest-risk repre-sentation, despite the absence of an identification objective during encoder train-ing. We further show that the released embeddings retain information about a demographic attribute, with recorded-sex prediction reaching balanced accuracy of 90.9% on OASIS-2 and 95.5% on MIRIAD. Overall, our results demonstrate that replacing raw brain MRI with learned embeddings does not, by itself, elimi-nate patient-linkage or attribute-inference risk, and is therefore insufficient on its own to establish anonymity. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an imaging modality that generates detailed images of the internal structures of the body, including the brain’s anatomy. Given this level of anatomical detail, brain MRI is a routine tool in the diagnosis and monitoring of neurological and psychiatric condi-tions such as Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, and brain tumors (Frisoni et al., 2010; Ahmed et al., 2021; Martucci et al., 2023). Many AI models have been trained to detect and classify these conditions from brain MRI scans (Korolev et al., 2017; Mohsen et al., 2018). At the same time, studies have shown that brain morphology can serve as a biometric signature, a “brain fingerprint”, which enables subject re-identification through comparison with reference data (Wachinger et al., 2014; Takao et al., 2015; Valizadeh et al., 2018). Therefore, brain MRI has a dual nature, at once a diagnostic tool and a carrier of identifying biometric information. In recent years, foundation models, large neural networks trained on broad and diverse datasets, have emerged. Many of these models use self-supervised learning to learn transferable representations that can be adapted to a range of downstream tasks (Bommasani et al., 2022). This approach is especially useful for medical imaging, where labeled data can be scarce and costly to obtain. Instead of training a model from scratch, a pretrained model can be adapted to the specific task at hand with limited task-specific labeled data (Liu et al., 2025; Tak et al., 2026). For brain MRI specifically, a growing body of work has explored foundation models for representation learning and downstream analysis (Barbano et al., 2026; Ghamizi et al., 2025; Mazher et al., 2025). Throughout this paper, we refer to the vector representation produced by a pretrained encoder as an embedding .1arXiv:2610.20494v1 [cs.CV] 14 Oct 2026 This question is also relevant to how anonymization is assessed in practice. Recent guidelines from the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) identify three criteria for evaluating whether data can be considered anonymous: No Record Isolation, No Linkage, and No Inference (European Data Pro-tection Board, 2026). Our study examines two corresponding technical risks for released brain MRI representations: linkage across records belonging to the same individual and inference of withheld patient attributes. As pretrained encoders are increasingly used to produce reusable representations of brain anatomy for downstream tasks, a central privacy question arises: when a learned representation is released instead of the raw brain MRI, can it still enable patient linkage or reveal additional patient-specific information? Across the evaluated models and datasets, we observe substantial variation in privacy risk, with rank-1 patient matching exceeding 90% for some representations and remaining well above chance across several model–dataset combinations. We address this question through four contributions: • Define explicit threat models for patient linkage, re-identification, and sensitive-attribute inference from released brain MRI representations, and systematically evaluate five frozen pretrained encoders across two longitudinal MRI datasets. • Quantify identity-related information through a score-based linkability audit and an ex-plicit rank-1 matching attack (exploiting the proximity of embeddings derived from the same patient), showing that several representations preserve substantial subject-specific in-formation despite the absence of an identification objective during encoder training. • Evaluate sensitive-attribute inference from released brain MRI representations, showing that the embeddings can reveal the subject’s recorded sex in addition to supporting patient linkage. • Demonstrate that releasing embeddings instead of raw brain MRI does not, by itself, elimi-nate linkage or attribute-inference risk and therefore is insufficient, on its own, to establish anonymity. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Privacy risks in brain MRI : Historically, safeguarding patient privacy in medical imaging, as re-quired by privacy regulations like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) European Par-liament and Council of the European Union (2016), Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA) Government of Canada (2000), and California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) California State Legislature (2026), has involved removing direct identifiers from medi-cal data before secondary use or disclosure. In medical imaging, this typically includes stripping metadata such as names, birth dates, and medical record numbers from image headers. However, medical images themselves still contain detailed clinical and anatomical information, and patient-specific traces may remain even after obvious identifiers are removed (RSNA). Brain MRIs, for instance, capture detailed facial anatomy, which makes 3D facial reconstruction and therefore identification via face-recognition deep learning achievable. (Boutet et al., 2023) demon-strated that faces reconstructed in 3D from brain MRI scans could be matched against public photos using deep learning, effectively exposing a person’s identity. To guard against this, techniques like defacing are commonly used (Gulban et al., 2020; Mitew et al., 2024). Yet defacing does not fully eliminate the risk either: deep learning can still reconstruct a plausible face from a defaced scan (Jeong et al., 2020; Abramian & Eklund, 2019). The problem is not limited to the face. Research has shown that the shape of the brain itself can serve as an identifying signature, a “brain fingerprint”. Wachinger et al. (2014) developed Brain-Print, a technique that uses brain shape to identify individuals with near-perfect accuracy. Valizadeh et al. (2018) found that brain features remain distinctive and stable even across years. DeepBrain-Print (Puglisi et al., 2023) explicitly trained a contrastive representation for longitudinal brain MRI re-identification, showing that learned representations can also encode stable subject-specific in-formation across scans. More recently, Bajar et al. (2026) reinforced this finding by testing four defacing methods on brain scans; even after defacing, subjects were still re-identified 66–73% of the time using brain-feature-based similarity attacks. Taken together, these findings show that brain 2MRI can reveal identity in more than one way through brain features even after defacing methods have been applied. 

Medical foundation models : Brain MRI foundation models can be broadly grouped according to their pretraining objective. Task-agnostic models are pretrained without a specific downstream task in mind; they rely on self-supervised objectives to learn general-purpose representations that can later be fine-tuned for various tasks (Barbano et al., 2026; Kaczmarek et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Caron et al., 2021). Task-specific foundation models, in contrast, are pretrained around a particular downstream objec-tive, most often segmentation (Wang et al., 2024; Cox et al., 2024). Their encoders are therefore shaped by this task rather than by the goal of producing general-purpose representations. We evalu-ate representative models from these different paradigms; their architectures and pretraining objec-tives are described in Section 3.1. 

Privacy risks for medical foundation models : Nebbia et al. (2025) showed that representations extracted from frozen medical foundation models can support patient retrieval in fundus photogra-phy, OCT, and chest X-ray, and can also expose demographic attributes. However, it does not define a threat model. Also, subsequent work questioned whether some of these results reflect properties specific to foundation models or simpler acquisition and image-level similarity, highlighting the im-portance of carefully specifying the attacker and evaluation protocol (Engelmann et al., 2026). More broadly, learned representations are known to expose sensitive information beyond their intended downstream objective (Song & Shmatikov, 2019), and recent work has emphasized the importance of explicit attacker knowledge and disclosure assumptions when evaluating clinical foundation mod-els (Tonekaboni et al., 2026). 

Summary: Our work connects two previously separate observations: brain anatomy is individually distinctive, acting as a biometric trait, and reusable learned representations can leak information beyond their intended purpose. Focusing on longitudinal brain MRI, we evaluate whether frozen pretrained encoders enable both patient linkage and sensitive-attribute inference, explicitly separat-ing linkability from re-identification threat models. By quantifying how much these representations expose patients to re-identification, we highlight the need for stronger safeguards and greater aware-ness as such models are released as open-source resources and made widely accessible. 

# 3 THREAT MODEL AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

This section describes the experimental setting used to evaluate privacy risks in pretrained brain MRI representations. We first introduce the evaluated encoders and longitudinal datasets, then formalize the attacker capabilities and privacy threats, and finally describe the embedding extraction, linkage, attribute-inference, and evaluation protocols. 3.1 MODELS AND DATASETS 

Models. We evaluate five pretrained encoders that differ in pretraining domain, input dimensional-ity, architecture, and objective (Table 1): AnatCL (Barbano et al., 2026), 3D-Neuro-SimCLR (Kacz-marek et al., 2025), Brain-MRI-SSL (Huang et al., 2025), DINO-ViT (Caron et al., 2021), and SAM-Med3D (Wang et al., 2024). The first three were pretrained specifically on brain MRI, whereas DINO-ViT was pretrained on natural images and SAM-Med3D on heterogeneous medical images. AnatCL, 3D-Neuro-SimCLR, and SAM-Med3D process full 3D volumes, while Brain-MRI-SSL and DINO-ViT operate on 2D images. Most models are trained with self-supervised objectives to learn general-purpose representations, whereas SAM-Med3D is trained specifically for segmen-tation in a supervised way. The models also use different loss objectives for their training. For instance, AnatCL is trained with weakly contrastive loss that combines two terms: an anatomical loss that brings closer subjects with similar anatomical descriptors, and a y-Aware loss that does the same based on age. 3D-Neuro-SimCLR and Brain-MRI-SSL both use contrastive learning. 3D-Neuro-SimCLR uses SimCLR’s NT-Xent loss, which pulls together two views of the same scan and pushes apart different scans in a batch. Brain-MRI-SSL uses MoCov3’s symmetrized InfoNCE loss: each augmented view, encoded by a gradient-updated encoder, is contrasted against the other view produced by a momentum-updated encoder, with the other samples of the batch serving as negatives. In contrast, DINO-ViT relies on self-distillation. It minimizes the cross-entropy between a student 3network and a momentum teacher, without using negatives or labels. Finally, SAM-Med3D uses a supervised segmentation loss (Dice and cross-entropy). This diversity allows us to assess whether privacy risks persist across substantially different pretraining domains, architectures, and objectives.

Datasets. We use two T1-weighted brain MRI datasets that were not included in the training data of any of our tested models: OASIS-2 (Marcus et al., 2010) and MIRIAD (Malone et al., 2013), summarized in Table 2. Both are longitudinal datasets including Alzheimer’s patients and healthy controls, but they differ substantially in scale and acquisition design. OASIS-2 includes more sub-jects (150) but fewer scans per subject (2–5 sessions), whereas MIRIAD includes fewer subjects (69) but with more repeated scans per subject (up to 12 scans over 9 visits, 708 scans in total). OASIS-2 scans are provided in a preprocessed form (motion-corrected, atlas-registered, skull-stripped, and intensity-corrected). Importantly, these are longitudinal acquisitions rather than near-duplicate ac-quisitions of the same scan: MIRIAD follow-ups are separated by approximately two weeks to two years, while OASIS-2 follow-ups extend over multiple years. The attack therefore evaluates whether subject-specific information persists across distinct acquisitions over time.

The lower risk of SAM-Med3D may be related to its training objective: it is the only model trained in a supervised manner for organ segmentation, so its embeddings are optimized for a different purpose. All the other models are self-supervised and not trained with a specific downstream task in mind. DINO-ViT and SAM-Med3D are the only two models not specific to brain MRI, and DINO-ViT is not even trained on medical images. Sur-prisingly, it still poses a considerable re-identification risk, higher on OASIS-2 than brain-specific models such as Brain-MRI-SSL and 3D-Neuro-SimCLR. Preprocessing may also contribute to these differences. Among the 3D models, AnatCL uses the most detailed pipeline: after nonlinear registration, it operates on gray matter maps, which de-scribe the amount of gray matter at each location of the brain and therefore capture individual struc-tural variations. 3D-Neuro-SimCLR uses skull-stripped and linearly registered volumes, and SAM-Med3D applies no brain-specific preprocessing. Overall, these results show that pretrained brain MRI encoders are not privacy-safe: their embeddings can be used to re-identify patients, even though none of these models was trained for this purpose. Ap-pendix A.2 reports the results when the classifier is trained on one dataset and tested on the other. 4.3 IMPLICATIONS OF PRETRAINING OBJECTIVES FOR PRIVACY 

The evaluated encoders differ substantially in how their representations are learned, yet none is explicitly optimized to match longitudinal acquisitions from the same patient. This contrasts with biometric representation learning, where identity consistency is part of the objective. For example, Fallahi et al. (2026) train EEG encoders using supervised metric learning that brings recordings from the same subject across sessions closer while separating different subjects. In our setting, patient identity is never provided as supervision. The contrastive models also differ in their training mechanism. Both 3D-Neuro-SimCLR and Brain-MRI-SSL treat augmented views of the same scan as positives and other scans as negatives, but 3D-Neuro-SimCLR optimizes an NT-Xent objective with a single shared encoder (Kaczmarek et al., 2025), whereas Brain-MRI-SSL follows MoCov3, in which keys are produced by a momentum en-coder (Huang et al., 2025). These objectives are instance-based rather than identity-aware and do 8not explicitly encourage different acquisitions of the same subject to be close. If such acquisitions occur among the negatives, the objective may even discourage their similarity, depending on the pretraining sampling strategy. AnatCL instead guides contrastive learning using anatomical descrip-tors and age (Barbano et al., 2026); DINO-ViT uses self-distillation across augmented views (Caron et al., 2021); and SAM-Med3D is optimized for supervised segmentation (Wang et al., 2024). Despite these different learning signals, all evaluated representations retain subject-specific infor-mation supporting longitudinal linkage, although the magnitude varies across models and datasets. This suggests that such leakage is not specific to a particular contrastive formulation or the use of explicit negatives, but can arise as a by-product of preserving stable anatomical, structural, or instance-specific characteristics. AnatCL provides a direct example: although not designed for iden-tity matching, its anatomical descriptors alone already support strong re-identification. However, because the encoders also differ in architecture, pretraining data, and preprocessing, differences in linkability cannot be attributed to the training objective alone. These findings have implications for both privacy-oriented representation learning and AI gover-nance. An identity-agnostic objective should not be assumed to produce an identity-invariant rep-resentation, motivating privacy-aware training or post-hoc sanitization of released embeddings. For high-risk AI systems, Article 9 of the EU AI Act requires an iterative process for identifying and evaluating reasonably foreseeable risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights and adopting appro-priate risk-management measures (European Union, 2024). Privacy audits such as the linkage and attribute-inference evaluations considered here can provide technical evidence of residual risks be-fore deployment or release, complementing the data-protection assessment of linkage and inference risks discussed earlier (European Data Protection Board, 2026), without by themselves establishing regulatory compliance or anonymity. 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We audited whether released representations from frozen pretrained encoders retain patient-specific information in longitudinal brain MRI. Across five encoders and two T1-weighted datasets, we find that several representations support strong patient linkage and re-identification under the evaluated threat model, despite the absence of an identification objective during encoder training. We also show that released embeddings retain information predictive of the subject’s recorded sex, extend-ing the privacy risk beyond identity linkage alone. These findings indicate that releasing learned representations instead of raw brain MRI does not, by itself, eliminate linkage or attribute-inference risk and is therefore insufficient on its own to establish anonymity. Our study is limited to two longitudinal T1-weighted datasets, five pretrained encoders, and one inferred attribute. Moreover, the evaluated models differ simultaneously in architecture, pretraining domain, and objective, so our comparisons should not be interpreted as isolating the causal effect of any single design choice. Future work should move from measuring privacy risk to reducing it through privacy-aware training objectives and post-hoc sanitization methods that suppress identity-bearing information in released embeddings. In Section 4.2, we showed that the evaluated embeddings retain information predictive of recorded sex when the classifier is trained and tested within the same dataset. Ta-ble 6 reports results in both directions, where A →B denotes training on dataset A and testing on dataset B. Balanced accuracy decreases for every encoder under cross-dataset evaluation, with most models approaching chance level ( 0.5) in at least one direction.
