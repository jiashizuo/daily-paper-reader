A B S T R A C T 

Accurate localization of neonatal hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) lesions on apparent diffu-sion coefficient (ADC) images is clinically important but is limited by scarce expert annotations and substantial variation in lesion appearance. We propose OpenMAP-HIE, a pathology-informed score-based diffusion model for unsupervised HIE lesion localization without voxel-wise lesion supervision. A denoising diffusion model learns the healthy neonatal ADC distribution, while a ControlNet-based conditional branch preserves patient-specific anatomy. At inference, anomaly evidence is derived directly from differences between conditional and unconditioned noise predictions at independently evaluated diffusion noise levels, avoiding dependence on a final pseudo-healthy reconstruction or sequential reverse-diffusion chain. Based on the characteristic ADC reduction associated with acute HIE, only discrepancies consistent with the expected pathological direction are retained and aggregated across noise levels using a geometric mean. Spatial mean filtering further suppresses isolated responses. The framework was evaluated on the BONBID-HIE dataset. The ADC-only method achieved a Dice score of 0.283 ± 0 .244 , average precision of 0.505 , Hausdorff distance of 

25 .45 ± 15 .63 mm, mean average surface distance of 9.40 ± 8 .49 mm, and normalized surface distance of 0.355 ± 0 .236 , providing the best overlap and surface-distance performance among the evaluated unsupervised methods and Dice performance comparable to supervised ADC-only models. Optional population-derived ADC 𝑍 -map refinement further increased Dice to 0.602 ± 0 .269 . These findings support pathology-informed score discrepancies as a useful signal for neonatal HIE lesion localization, while highlighting remaining challenges in detecting small and low-contrast lesions. 

## 1. Introduction 

Hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is a clinical syndrome of neurological disorder caused by impaired cerebral oxygen delivery and blood flow around the time of birth. It affects approximately 1–5 per 1,000 term-born neonates (Long and Brandon, 2007). Therapeutic hypother-mia reduces the risk of death and disability in infants with moderate-to-severe HIE (Silveira and Procianoy, 2015). However, mortality and adverse long-term outcomes, in-cluding neurodevelopmental delay and cognitive or motor impairment, still affect approximately 30% –50% of affected infants (Bao et al., 2025). Accurate identification and local-ization of HIE-related brain injury are therefore important for quantitatively characterizing lesion severity and for understanding disease progression (Vermeulen et al., 2008; Martinez-Biarge et al., 2011; Chang et al., 2020; Bobba et al., 2023). Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has been applied for detecting acute HIE-related injury, because abnormalities may appear earlier and more observable than signal changes on conventional T1- or T2-weighted MRI (Triulzi et al.,

Cytotoxic edema restricts water diffusion, typically producing increased signal intensity on DWI and decreased apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (Onda et al., 2024). ADC maps thus provide quan-titative information for identifying HIE-related injury and assessing its severity and prognosis (Bobba et al., 2023; Bao et al., 2025). Automated localization remains challenging because HIE lesions are often small, multifocal, diffuse, and hetero-geneous in appearance. In the BONBID-HIE cohort, more than half of affected neonates had lesions occupying less than 1% of the brain volume (Bao et al., 2025), resulting in severe foreground–background imbalance and poorly de-fined lesion boundaries. Scanner field strength, acquisition protocol, developmental anatomy, and postnatal imaging time further complicate the distinction between pathol-ogy and normal neonatal variability. These characteristics also make manual delineation difficult. Quantitative lesion analysis typically requires experienced pediatric neurora-diologists, often using multi-reader consensus (Bao et al., 2025), but lesion boundaries may remain ambiguous and dependent on reader judgment. Consequently, expert voxel-level annotation is labor intensive and difficult to scale. However, the scarcity of publicly available neonatal HIE data (Bao et al., 2025), along with the substantial expertise and effort required for annotation, restricts the scalability and external generalizability of these methods. Unsupervised anomaly detection provides an alternative by learning a normative representation from healthy images and identifying deviations in a potentially pathological subject without directly optimizing on lesion labels (Schlegl et al., 2019; Fernando et al., 2021). A common strategy is to reconstruct a pseudo-healthy counterpart of the observed image and interpret the reconstruction residual as abnormal-ity (Schlegl et al., 2019; Wolleb et al., 2022; Wyatt et al., 2022). However, reconstruction-based anomaly detection must balance pathology removal against preservation of healthy subject-specific anatomy. Excessive corruption or insuffi-cient conditioning may alter normal structures and generate false-positive residuals, whereas strong subject condition-ing may reproduce pathological signals and reduce lesion contrast. This trade-off is particularly problematic for HIE because diffusion abnormalities may be subtle, spatially dif-fuse, and poorly separated from normal neonatal variability (Forbes et al., 2000). Diffusion models have recently emerged as expressive generative priors for unsupervised medical anomaly detec-tion (Wolleb et al., 2022; Wyatt et al., 2022; Frotscher et al., 2026). Their multi noise level denoising process provides an opportunity to identify deviations from learned normal anatomy other than the residual of a single final reconstruc-tion. However, reliably distinguishing subtle pathological signals from subject-specific anatomy, acquisition-related variability, and stochastic denoising errors remains challeng-ing. In this work, we propose OpenMAP-HIE, an anatomy-conditioned, pathology-informed score-based diffusion frame-work for unsupervised neonatal HIE lesion localization (Fig. 1). A diffusion model learns a healthy neonatal ADC prior, and a ControlNet branch incorporates the observed image as a spatial condition to capture patient-specific anatomy. We derive anomaly evidence from discrepancies between the healthy-prior and subject-conditioned score estimates, implemented through their noise-prediction dif-ferences (Fig. 2). These discrepancies characterize how subject-specific conditioning modifies the local denoising response of the learned healthy prior. Both branches eval-uate the same noisy input at each noise level, with inputs generated directly from the observed image rather than propagated through a sequential reverse-diffusion chain. This formulation avoids dependence on a final pseudo-healthy reconstruction and the propagation of errors between reverse-diffusion steps. To improve pathological specificity, we incorporate a directional prior based on the characteristic ADC reduction associated with acute HIE (Forbes et al., 2000; Bobba et al., 2023; Bao et al., 2025). ## 2. Related Works 

2.1. Supervised Anomaly Detection 

Supervised medical image segmentation has advanced through encoder–decoder architectures such as U-Net (Ron-neberger et al., 2015) and UNet++ (Zhou et al., 2019; Rao et al., 2025). Recent HIE segmentation systems have extended to three-dimensional U-Net variants, SegResNet, Swin-UNETR (Hatamizadeh et al., 2021; Almansour et al., 2025), ensembles, and loss functions designed for highly imbalanced lesions (Bao et al., 2025). These approaches learn lesion appearance directly from voxel-wise annota-tions. In contrast, our framework learns a healthy-image prior and derives lesion evidence without using lesion masks as optimization targets. 

2.2. Unsupervised Anomaly Detection 

Reconstruction-based anomaly detection learns normal image appearance and identifies abnormalities through dis-crepancies between an input and its reconstructed coun-terpart. Autoencoders and variational autoencoders learn constrained representations of healthy images (Baur et al., 2021), while f-AnoGAN combines a GAN-based normal-image model with an encoder for efficient latent inference (Schlegl et al., 2019). Denoising autoencoders instead learn 

> Page 3 of 22

to recover healthy images from artificially corrupted in-puts, where appropriately designed coarse noise can improve anomaly detection while preserving normal anatomical de-tail (Kascenas et al., 2022). IterMask 2 combines iterative spatial mask refinement with high-frequency image informa-tion to improve reconstruction of normal tissue and reduce false-positive responses (Liang et al., 2024). These methods depend on reconstruction errors being larger in pathological regions than in healthy regions. How-ever, normal anatomy may be reconstructed imperfectly, while sufficiently expressive models may partially repro-duce pathological structures. Consequently, reconstruction fidelity alone does not guarantee pathological specificity. 

2.3. Diffusion Model and Score Method 

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) learn a data distribution by progressively perturbing images with noise and training a neural network to predict the noise of removal for the reverse process (Ho et al., 2020). Under the standard Gaussian perturbation formulation, noise predic-tion provides a parameterization of the score, the gradient of the perturbed data log density with respect to the noisy input (Song et al., 2021). This connection enables anomaly assessment through both reconstructed images and noise-level dependent noise predictions. Further, compared with GANs, diffusion models generally offer stable training and high-fidelity outputs (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021), making them suitable for learning healthy anatomical priors. Early diffusion-based methods primarily used pseudo-healthy reconstruction. AnoDDPM uses structured Simplex noise to obscure abnormalities at lower diffusion noise levels while retaining more image context (Wyatt et al., 2022). Wolleb et al. (2022) combined deterministic Denoising Dif-fusion Implicit Model (DDIM) inversion (Song et al., 2020) with classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021) to generate counterfactual healthy images. Patched diffusion models restrict corruption to local image regions and use surrounding anatomy as contextual information for denois-ing process, with pseudo-healthy reconstruction based on ensembles of predictions through sliding-window procedure (Behrendt et al., 2024). Subsequent methods have attempted to restrict restora-tion to suspected pathological regions to avoid unnecessary modification of healthy anatomy. AutoDDPM first gener-ates an anomaly-likelihood map and then uses masking, stitching, and joint-noise resampling to inpaint candidate abnormalities while harmonizing the reconstructed content with the original image (Bercea et al., 2023). Temporal Harmonization for Optimal Restoration (THOR) instead constructs intermediate anomaly maps during reverse dif-fusion and uses them as implicit guidance to repeatedly restore presumed healthy regions from the original input (Bercea et al., 2024). These methods are conceptually related to diffusion-based inpainting approaches such as RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022), in which known image regions are repeatedly reintroduced during sampling to constrain the generated result. Masking and local restoration have also been explored in latent diffusion frameworks, with introducing localized latent-space corruptions during training as proxies for anoma-lous regions. MAD-AD applies forward diffusion only to randomly selected patches of healthy latent representations during training and jointly learns to reconstruct the clean latent representation and predict the region of corruption. At inference, its mask-prediction head identifies candidate anomalous patches, which are exclusively corrected during reverse diffusion while the remaining regions are preserved (Beizaee et al., 2025a). DeCo-Diff reformulates latent dif-fusion to learn a direction of deviation from normality. It progressively corrects anomalous latent patches while leav-ing normal patches unchanged and combines reconstruction discrepancies measured in the latent and image spaces to produce the final anomaly map (Beizaee et al., 2025b). Rather than producing a final pseudo-healthy recon-struction or iteratively refining a candidate inpainting mask, Aggregated Normative Diffusion (ANDi) derives anomaly evidence directly from deviations within the diffusion pro-cess (Frotscher et al., 2026). At each noise level, ANDi compares the analytical reverse-posterior mean conditioned on the observed image with the reverse mean predicted by the diffusion model, both evaluated at the same noisy input. The resulting voxel-wise squared differences are aggregated across noise levels using a geometric mean. The voxel-wise squared deviations are aggregated across multiple sampled noise levels using a geometric mean. Consequently, ANDi does not require an additional inpainting stage or calculate its anomaly map only from a final reconstruction residual. Extending this reconstruction-free direction, InvAD instead uses DDIM inversion (Song et al., 2020) to map image rep-resentations toward the noise prior and evaluates deviations through Gaussian likelihood scoring in the resulting final latent space (Sakai et al., 2026). Our approach is most closely related to ANDi in its aggregation of discrepancies across noise levels. The key distinction is the comparison itself: we contrast subject-conditioned and learned healthy-prior score estimates, im-plemented through their noise-prediction differences, rather than comparing a model prediction with an analytical poste-rior target. We further filter the discrepancy signals using directional information from an HIE-specific pathological prior. 

2.4. HIE Pathology Directional Prior 

Most reconstruction-based medical anomaly detection methods define abnormity from the difference between an observed image and a pseudo-healthy reconstruction, com-monly using absolute or squared intensity residuals (Schlegl et al., 2019; Wolleb et al., 2022; Wyatt et al., 2022). More recent diffusion-based approaches characterize abnormality through deviations from learned normal denoising dynam-ics (Bercea et al., 2024; Frotscher et al., 2026; Beizaee et al., 2025a) or gaussian prior likelihood (Sakai et al., 2026). These disease-agnostic formulations are applicable to diverse anomaly types, but generally treat positive and 

> Page 4 of 22

negative intensity deviations symmetrically. Consequently, a large anomaly score does not have strong pathological interpretability and cannot properly distinguish the target pathology from the anatomical variance and imaging noise. This limitation is particularly relevant to neonatal HIE. During the acute diffusion-restriction phase, cytotoxic edema typically produces lower ADC values in injured tissue than in healthy tissue (Forbes et al., 2000; Bobba et al., 2023; Onda et al., 2024; Bao et al., 2025). ADC thresh-olds and population-derived statistical maps have there-fore been investigated for identifying and quantifying HIE-related injury (Bao et al., 2025). However, the diffusion-based anomaly detection methods considered above do not explicitly incorporate the expected direction of the ADC abnormality into their multi noise-level discrepancy estima-tion. Our method incorporates this pathological information by retaining only positive differences between subject-conditioned and healthy-prior unconditional noise predic-tions, corresponding to higher healthy-reference posterior than subject-conditioned reverse-mean estimates. Unlike a conventional unsigned reconstruction residual, this formu-lation suppresses denoising discrepancies that are large in magnitude but inconsistent with the characteristic ADC reduction of acute HIE.

3.7. Dataset and Preprocessing 

We used the BONBID-HIE dataset (Bao et al., 2025) for model development and evaluation. The dataset was divided into 85 training subjects, 4 validation subjects, and 44 test subjects, as following its predefined partitions, without per-forming an additional subject-level split and with no subject overlap among the three partitions. All 133 subjects in the selected release were included, with no subject-level ex-clusions. Each subject included an three-dimensional ADC 

> Page 8 of 22

map and its corresponding population-normalized 𝑍 -map. To account for variations in voxel spacing and spatial mis-alignment across scans, all volumes were affine registered to the JHU-neonate-SS atlas template (Oishi et al., 2011) using ANTsPy (Tustison et al., 2021). At the end, all scans were resampled to a common matrix size 256 × 256 × 128 and voxel size 0.58 × 0 .58 × 0 .74 𝑚𝑚 3, with nearest neighbor interpolation, as following Rao et al. (2025). The intensity of each volume went through its independent 99th percentile normalization. The diffusion model was trained using two-dimensional axial ADC slices. To construct a training set representing non-lesion brain tissue, we examined the corresponding lesion mask for each slice from the training subjects and retained only slices containing no annotated lesion voxels. The lesion masks were used exclusively for training-slice selection and were not provided to the model as inputs or optimization targets. The 𝑍 -maps were not used to train the diffusion model and were introduced only during the post-processing stage described in Section 3.6. We have 6042 slices of non-lesion axial brain slices for training and 4838 slices of lesion axial brain slices from the training subjects for the hyperparameter selection for the post-processing stage. The predefined validation partition was used to monitor loss and model performance during training to reduce over-fitting and to select the mean-filter kernel size. Evaluation was performed over complete 3D volumes from the 44 test subjects.

In the second stage, the pretrained U-Net served as the healthy generative prior, and the ControlNet branch was trained for an additional 𝐾 2 = 200 epochs to incorporate subject-specific spatial conditioning. To enable both null-conditioned and subject-conditioned noise predictions, the conditioning image was randomly dropped with probability

During training, the input images were normalized, and random horizontal flipping was applied with a probability of 0.5.During inference, following the intermediate-noise strat-egy described in (Frotscher et al., 2026), we accumulated the conditional-unconditional discrepancy over diffusion timesteps  = {75 , 76 , … , 200} . This interval was se-lected to balance corruption of the pathological signal with preservation of subject-specific anatomy. At very low noise levels, the lesion appearance remains largely preserved in 

𝐱 𝑡 , providing insufficient perturbation for the healthy prior to reveal a clear discrepancy. Conversely, at substantially higher noise levels, excessive corruption removes subject-specific anatomical information and may introduce recon-struction differences unrelated to pathology. The selected intermediate timesteps weaken the lesion-related signal while retaining sufficient anatomical information for spa-tially meaningful comparison between the conditional and unconditional noise predictions.

4.6. Ablation Analysis of Discrepancy Formulations 

Table 6 compares independent noise-level evaluation with reverse-chain accumulation and a formulation that replaces the unconditional prediction with the sampled forward noise, as described in Sec. 3.11. The pathology-informed directional constraint was applied to all configu-rations. Without mean filtering, independent evaluation outper-formed reverse-chain accumulation (Eq. (43)) in Dice ( 0.232 

versus 0.070 ), AP ( 0.374 versus 0.031 ), and all surface met-rics. This advantage persisted after mean filtering: reverse-chain formulation’s Dice remained at 0.070 and AP in-creased only to 0.036 , while HD and MASD worsened to 

49 .59 and 23 .36 mm, respectively, and NSD decreased to 

0.114 . The reduced performance of chain-dependent accu-mulation suggests that maintaining a continuous reverse tra-jectory is not advantageous for discrepancy-based anomaly localization. Because each state is generated from the pre-ceding reverse estimate, stochastic perturbations and recon-struction errors introduced at earlier steps propagate through the remainder of the trajectory. Moreover, as the reverse pro-cess progressively modifies the image, subtle pathological information present in the original subject image may be attenuated, causing later discrepancy estimates to become less directly associated with the observed abnormality. ## 5. Discussion 

5.1. Overall Detection Performance 

The proposed ADC-only framework demonstrated strong overall performance for unsupervised HIE lesion localiza-tion. However, the improvement was not uniform across all evaluation criteria and this behavior reflects the different properties measured by these metrics. AP evaluates the ranking of continuous anomaly scores across lesion and background voxels without selecting a single operating threshold, whereas Dice and the surface metrics evaluate the geometry of the resulting binary segmentation. The compar-atively strong Dice and surface performance of our method therefore suggests improved spatial localization, while its lower AP indicates that the continuous anomaly scores remain imperfectly calibrated and that some background voxels still receive scores overlapping those of lesion voxels. Improving cross-subject score calibration and suppressing non-pathological background responses remain important directions for further investigation. The comparison with supervised ADC-only models also demonstrates the potential of learning from healthy-image structure rather than directly optimizing voxel-wise lesion-mask prediction. Competitive performance in selected spa-tial metrics suggests that a learned healthy prior, combined with subject conditioning and pathological directionality, can provide informative localization cues without voxel-wise lesion supervision in the training objective. This is particularly relevant when available annotations do not ad-equately represent the diversity of lesion appearances. The findings support healthy-prior modeling as a complementary route to quantitative HIE lesion assessment. The lesion-burden analysis indicates that the primary limitation of the proposed method is the detection of subjects with small lesion burdens. Larger HIE abnormalities affect more voxels and are more likely to produce spatially coher-ent, persistent discrepancies across multiple diffusion noise levels. In contrast, small or low-contrast lesions generate weaker and more localized responses that can be difficult to distinguish from normal anatomical variability, registra-tion differences, and acquisition noise. Mean filtering par-tially mitigated this limitation by suppressing isolated high-response voxels and reinforcing discrepancies supported by neighboring voxels, resulting in the largest improvements for the small- and medium- burden groups. For subjects with large lesions, however, the anomaly signal was already spatially coherent, leaving less noise for the filter to remove. Additional smoothing could also blur lesion boundaries or extend responses into adjacent healthy tissue, explaining the minimal Dice improvement and the reduction in NSD observed in this group. The relatively large inter-subject standard deviations fur-ther indicate heterogeneous performance across the test co-hort. Such variability may reflect differences in lesion bur-den, anatomical distribution, scan timing, and acquisition characteristics. Future studies should therefore evaluate per-formance after stratification by lesion burden, injury pattern, scanner field strength, and postnatal age at MRI, in addi-tion to validating the method in independent multi-center cohorts. 

5.2. Intermediate Denoising Discrepancy versus Reconstruction Difference 

Most diffusion-based anomaly-detection methods iden-tify abnormalities by comparing an observed image with 

> Page 18 of 22

its final pseudo-healthy reconstruction. Although intuitive, this formulation compresses the entire reverse diffusion tra-jectory into a single terminal image. Subtle pathological information expressed during intermediate denoising steps may therefore be attenuated or lost before the final recon-struction is obtained. Moreover, the reconstruction differ-ence reflects not only lesion restoration but also stochastic sampling variation, incomplete lesion removal, and unin-tended modification of subject-specific healthy anatomy. The latter can generate false-positive responses even when the reconstructed image appears globally plausible. Our CFG reconstruction experiments illustrate these limitations. Reducing the guidance scale to 𝑤 = 0 .8 en-couraged stronger restoration toward the learned healthy dis-tribution and increased AP relative to the DDPM baseline, but substantially degraded Dice and the surface-distance metrics. This suggests that weaker subject conditioning increased sensitivity to image differences while also intro-ducing spatial changes unrelated to the true lesion. Con-versely, increasing the guidance scale to 𝑤 = 7 .5 preserved and strengthened subject-specific information but did not produce consistently accurate anomaly localization. Our proposed method instead measures the conditional– unconditional difference directly in the intermediate noise-prediction space. Since both predictions are evaluated from the same noisy image 𝐱 𝑡 , the comparison controls for the common forward corruption and isolates the change in the denoising response induced by the subject-specific con-dition. The unconditional branch represents the response favored by the learned healthy prior, and the conditional branch retains information from the observed subject. There-fore, their difference provides a more direct measure of how the observed anatomy modifies the healthy denoising field than a comparison between two final reconstructed images. The substantially higher Dice obtained using multi noise-level discrepancy accumulation than using either CFG-based reconstruction supports the utility of extracting anomaly ev-idence before it is compressed into the final reconstruction. Evaluating each noise level independently provides an additional advantage over following a single reverse chain. In the chain-dependent formulation, each state inherits stochastic variation and reconstruction error from all pre-ceding transitions, and progressive restoration may attenuate subtle pathological signals before they contribute to later discrepancy estimates. In contrast, independently sampling 

𝐱 𝑡 from the observed image connects every comparison to the same subject and prevents errors at one noise level from propagating to another. Accumulation across the se-lected intermediate noise levels emphasizes discrepancies that persist under different corruption strengths rather than relying on a single potentially unstable observation. Inter-mediate noise levels are particularly useful because they par-tially suppress the observed lesion while retaining sufficient subject-specific anatomical information. At substantially higher noise levels, the anatomical condition may become weak, whereas near-clean inputs may provide insufficient separation between the two branch responses. 

5.3. Pathology-Informed Directionality and Interpretability 

A central feature of the proposed framework is that it does not treat all conditional–unconditional discrepancies as equally suggestive of pathology. Acute HIE is commonly associated with cytotoxic edema, restricted diffusion, and reduced ADC values (Forbes et al., 2000; Bobba et al., 2023; Onda et al., 2024). We therefore retain discrepancy components for which the healthy-prior response predicts a higher ADC value than the patient-conditioned response. This constraint provides a clinically motivated direction to the anomaly score and suppresses deviations that are inconsistent with the expected low-ADC appearance of acute HIE. In contrast to an unsigned reconstruction residual, the resulting score can consequently be interpreted as evidence that the observed tissue deviates from the learned healthy distribution in a direction consistent with water diffusion restriction. The quantitative analysis on the distribution of discrep-ancy supports this assumption. At the selected noise levels, the expected positive discrepancy occurred more frequently in annotated lesion voxels than in background voxels. Lesion voxels also exhibited a substantially larger positive discrep-ancy. Consistent with these observations, incorporating the pathology-informed sign constraint improved lesion local-ization relative to the corresponding unsigned formulation. These findings demonstrate that the selected direction is sta-tistically associated with annotated HIE abnormalities and is more specific than using unsigned discrepancy magnitude alone. An additional advantage of the proposed formulation is that the pathological prior is incorporated directly into the intermediate diffusion discrepancy, rather than being applied only to a completed reconstruction-derived anomaly map. Most conventional reconstruction-based methods de-fine direction-agnostic anomaly evidence using an absolute or squared residual between the observed image and its pseudo-healthy reconstruction. Although a low-ADC direc-tional constraint can be applied to the signed reconstruction residual, such output-level filtering is performed only after the reconstruction process has mixed pathological deviations with reconstruction blur, anatomical mismatch, and stochas-tic generation errors. It can remove some directionally in-consistent responses, but it cannot recover pathological ev-idence that was attenuated during reconstruction or deter-mine whether the final residual was supported consistently across the diffusion trajectory. The comparative experiments support this distinction. Applying the same low-ADC directional rule as post-processing to conventional anomaly-detection outputs improved their localization performance, indicating that the pathological prior is useful even at the output level. However, these gains were smaller than those obtained when the directional constraint was integrated into the multi noise-level discrep-ancy formulation. This difference suggests that biological knowledge is more effective when intermediate denoising 

> Page 19 of 22

responses contribute to the anomaly score, rather than being used only to reject candidates after reconstruction. False-positive responses in regions with low ADC or in-terhemispheric intensity asymmetry are consistent with the intended behavior of the directional constraint, but also high-light its limited pathological specificity. The constraint se-lects voxels with deviations in the expected direction of ADC reduction, but directional agreement alone is insufficient to attribute these deviations to HIE-related injury. Incor-porating regional anatomical context and spatially adaptive filtering may help distinguish lesion-associated responses from non-pathological variability. 

5.4. Utility and Limitations of Population 𝑍 -Score Refinement 

The population 𝑍 -map analysis demonstrates that voxel-wise normative information can substantially improve lesion localization when an appropriate reference population and accurate spatial correspondence are available. However, this improvement introduces requirements that may limit gener-alizability in routine clinical settings. Constructing a mean-ingful ADC 𝑍 -map requires spatially resolved estimates of the healthy population mean and standard deviation. These reference statistics may vary with postmenstrual age, scanner vendor, magnetic field strength, diffusion acquisi-tion protocol, reconstruction procedure, and preprocessing pipeline. This issue is particularly relevant in neonatal imag-ing because brain structure and diffusion properties change rapidly during early development. Voxel-wise 𝑍 -maps also depend on accurate alignment between the observed scan and the normative template. Errors in nonlinear registration may generate artificial devia-tions near tissue boundaries, ventricles, and rapidly develop-ing anatomical structures. Consequently, a 𝑍 -map derived from one cohort may not transfer reliably to a population with different demographics, acquisition characteristics, or spatial normalization quality. Site-specific recalibration or construction of a new normative reference may therefore be required before deployment. For these reasons, we consider the 𝑍 -map refinement an optional population-reference assisted extension rather than the principal contribution of the proposed framework. The primary ADC-only model does not require population-level voxel-wise means and standard deviations or the precise nonlinear normalization required to construct a normative 

𝑍 -map. Although its localization performance is lower than that of the 𝑍 -map assisted configuration, it reduces depen-dence on cohort-specific reference data and may therefore offer greater portability across clinical populations and ac-quisition settings. Future work should evaluate whether nor-mative harmonization, age-conditioned reference models, or registration-robust population representations can retain the localization benefit of 𝑍 -scores while improving cross-cohort generalizability. 

5.5. Limitations and Future Work 

Several limitations should be considered when interpret-ing the present findings. First, although lesion annotations were not used as voxel-wise training targets, they were used to exclude lesion-containing slices when constructing the healthy training subset. The proposed training procedure is therefore annotation-free at the voxel-wise training stage but is not entirely independent of annotations. Moreover, unan-notated slices from an HIE-affected subject may still contain subtle or diffuse abnormalities that were not included in the reference mask, potentially contaminating the learned healthy distribution. Future studies should train the frame-work using an independently acquired cohort of clinically healthy neonates with ADC imaging. This would eliminate the need for lesion mask-based slice selection and permit a more direct evaluation of fully annotation-independent training. Second, the present evaluation was conducted using a single cohort and a fixed pre-processing and registration pipeline. Differences in scanner manufacturer, field strength, acquisition protocol, reconstruction method, patient age, and clinical population may alter both the ADC distribution and the model response. The population-derived 𝑍 -map is par-ticularly sensitive to the population, scanning protocols and the accuracy of nonlinear registration. Multicenter external validation is therefore required to determine whether the learned healthy prior and operating threshold transfer across institutions. The current implementation is based on independently processed 2D axial slices. Although this design reduces computational requirements and increases the number of available training samples, it does not explicitly model through-plane anatomical continuity. This limitation may particularly affect small lesions, lesions with weak contrast in an individual slice, and abnormalities extending obliquely or across multiple axial levels. The slice-wise predictions are subsequently assembled into a 3D volume, but this does not recover contextual information that was unavailable to the model during inference. Extending the framework to 2.5D or fully 3D diffusion architectures may improve spatial consistency and the detection of small or fragmented lesions, although these approaches will require substantially greater memory and training data. The pathology-informed directional prior also assumes that acute HIE injury is associated with reduced ADC rel-ative to healthy tissue. This assumption is clinically moti-vated but is not universally valid across all imaging times and injury patterns. ADC values evolve after the hypoxic– ischemic event and may pseudo-normalize during the sub-acute (Vermeulen et al., 2008). Some injured regions may therefore exhibit weak, absent, or temporally altered ADC reduction despite clinically meaningful tissue damage. Such lesions may not produce a consistently positive conditional– unconditional discrepancy and could be suppressed by the proposed one-sided scoring rule. Future work could incorpo-rate time from injury, gestational age, and treatment timing into the model. The hard positive-part operation constitutes another lim-itation. Setting all opposite-direction discrepancies to zero provides a direct implementation of the reduced ADC prior, 

> Page 20 of 22

but it imposes an abrupt boundary at zero. Some lesion voxels may exhibit weakly negative discrepancies or fluc-tuate around zero across the selected noise levels because of subtle lesion contrast, model uncertainty, or temporal variation in ADC abnormalities. Hard clipping completely removes these responses, potentially reducing sensitivity to small or low-contrast lesions. A future extension could replace hard clipping with a soft directional gate, controlled by a parameter 𝜂 , that assigns reduced rather than zero weight to near-zero or slightly opposite-direction responses. This would preserve the pref-erence for the expected HIE direction while accounting for uncertainty in the estimated discrepancy. Population-derived ADC 𝑍 -maps could also be introduced as a continuous spatial weight at this stage, allowing strongly negative 𝑍 -scores to provide supporting evidence for weak diffusion discrepancies. Finally, the continuous anomaly score is not a calibrated lesion probability. Its magnitude may vary with the sampled noise realization, the selected noise levels, the number of ac-cumulated responses, and the diffusion model’s uncertainty. Although geometric aggregation reduces the influence of isolated large responses, it does not eliminate stochastic variability or guarantee a common score scale across sub-jects and cohorts. Future work should quantify repeatability across random seeds and noise realizations, investigate en-sembling or deterministic sampling, and estimate voxel-wise uncertainty. 

## 6. Conclusion 

We proposed OpenMAP-HIE, a pathology-informed score-based diffusion model for unsupervised localization of neonatal HIE abnormalities on ADC images without using lesion annotations as voxel-wise training targets. Rather than relying on a final pseudo-healthy reconstruction, the framework compares and aggregates patient-conditioned and healthy-prior noise predictions across independently evaluated multi noise levels, retaining discrepancies con-sistent with the expected ADC reduction in acute HIE. The proposed ADC-only method improved lesion over-lap and surface-distance performance over the evaluated unsupervised baselines and achieved Dice performance comparable to supervised ADC-only segmentation models, while population-derived ADC 𝑍 -map refinement provided further improvements.
