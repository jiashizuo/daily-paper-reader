Abstract 

Accurate hippocampal segmentation can be a useful tool for diagnosing and monitoring neurological conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and epilepsy. While numerous automated segmentation methods exist, their clinical adoption remains limited. Reliable uncertainty assessment can enhance trust and facilitate clinical translation. This study evaluates ensembles of five heterogeneous hippocampal segmentation methods — InnerEye, ASHS, FastSurfer, HippoSeg, and FreeSurfer — across two dementia datasets and one epilepsy dataset. The sub-ensemble containing InnerEye, FastSurfer, and HippoSeg emerged as both accurate and efficient, highlighting the feasibility of balancing computational cost and performance. Additionally, ensemble-derived uncertainty quantification with sample variance, mutual information, and predictive entropy is shown to reduce inaccurate segmentations by flagging low-confidence cases, potentially providing a mechanism for automatically escalating ambiguous cases for expert assessment. Lastly, this study explores the downstream clinical utility of ensemble-derived uncertainty, finding a significant correlation with motion artefact severity and a modest contribution to Alzheimer’s disease classification.

## 1. Introduction 

Hippocampal volume changes are associated with several neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s and epilepsy (Jack, 1994; Duan et al., 2020). Accurate volumetric measure-ment can serve as a biomarker for diagnosis and disease monitoring (Schuff et al., 2008; Briellmann et al., 2002). While manual segmentation by an expert is still consid-ered the gold standard, its time-consuming nature and the well-documented intra- and inter-reader variability remain significant limitations (Boccardi and et al., 2015; Geuze et al., 2005). Although a concerted effort has taken place to establish a harmonised protocol for manual hippocampal segmentation (Frisoni and Jack, 2015), multiple different protocols for hippocampal segmentation exist, complicating the notion of a ground truth segmentation (Geuze et al., 2005). Numerous methods for automatic hippocampus seg-mentations have been proposed, ranging from atlas-based approaches (Dill et al., 2015), to deep learning methods (Schroder et al., 2023). Despite the range of available methods, clinical adoption remains limited. Key concerns hindering the clinical translation of these tools include the risk of silent failure, and a lack of robustness to unex-pected distribution shifts. Reliable uncertainty estimation can play a central role in strengthening the trustworthi-ness of these algorithms, e.g. by escalating uncertain cases for human review, and various approaches for assessing predictive uncertainty have been explored in the literature (Abdar and et al., 2020). While Bayesian neural networks offer the most principled framework for posterior approxi-mation, they are often computationally prohibitive (Abdar and et al., 2020). Monte-Carlo Dropout and ensembles have emerged as practical methods for Bayesian approxima-tion (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2016; Gal and Ghahramani, 2015). However, these approaches differ substantially in computational cost. Monte-Carlo sampling increases in-ference time through repeated stochastic forward passes, while ensemble-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) re-quires running multiple models or segmentation pipelines, making inference cost scale with ensemble size. This cre-ates an important trade-off between uncertainty reliabil-ity, scalability, and computational efficiency, particularly when methods are deployed across large imaging cohorts or resource-constrained clinical environments. Beyond provid-ing uncertainty estimation, ensemble methods consistently achieve superior performance over individual models, as repeatedly demonstrated by results from the Brain Tumor Image Segmentation Benchmark (BRATS) (Menze and et al., 2015) and the White Matter Hyperintensity Seg-mentation Challenge (Kuijf and et al., 2019). Ensemble predictions can be aggregated through multiple strategies, with stacking of heterogeneous models proving highly ef-fective and simple averaging providing a stable method for integrating their predictions (Müller et al., 2022). Although deep ensembles often outperform single models, their com-bined outputs are not automatically well-calibrated, even when each constituent network is well-calibrated on its own (Wu and Gales, 2021; Rahaman and Thiery, 2021). Cali-bration deteriorates further under distribution shift (Ovadia et al., 2019), a problem that has been traced in part to in-sufficient diversity among ensemble members (Javanbakhat et al., 2024). To counter this, the present study assesses the disagreement of deliberately heterogeneous segmentation methods and treats that disagreement itself as an uncer-tainty estimate, a strategy previously explored by Kofler et al. (2021). Another growing concern is the computational cost and energy consumption of the segmentation methods. Recent work has highlighted the potential environmental impact of large-scale deep learning models (Strubell et al., 2020; Wu and et al., 2022), emphasizing the need for sustainable solutions. High computational demands not only contribute to a significant carbon footprint, but also limit accessibility for hospitals and research institutions with limited resources. Addressing both the need for reliable uncertainty estimation and computational efficiency will be conducive for enabling broader clinical adoption. Beyond performance accuracy, ensembling methods may prove useful to provide robust uncertainty estimates that can be employed for downstream clinical tasks. One such application relates to quality control and particularly mo-tion artefact detection. There are a number of sources that can lead to motion artefacts in brain magnetic reso-nance imaging (MRI), including patient movement, invol-untary physiological motion (e.g., respiration and cardiac pulsation), swallowing, and flow of fluids such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid (Zaitsev et al., 2015). Motion artefacts can negatively impact image quality and lead to erroneous interpretations, in particular in acquisitions with low signal-to-noise ratio (Havsteen et al., 2017). Accurately detecting and correcting motion-related image degradation has long been a challenge in medical image processing (Zaitsev et al., 2015). While both traditional machine learning and deep-learning-based methods have demonstrated strong perfor-mance (Vakli et al., 2023), their effectiveness often does not generalise well across vendors or acquisition protocols. Segmentation uncertainty, which tends to increase as image quality degrades, may therefore offer a complementary sig-nal for flagging motion-corrupted scans, with previous work showing that ensemble-derived uncertainties are particularly useful for quality control (Ng et al., 2023). A further potential application of uncertainty estima-tion in the context of hippocampal segmentation is disease classification. Previous work has shown that hippocampal

Leveraging epistemic uncertainty, we hypothesise that disagreements between methods designed to accommodate atrophy and methods trained purely on healthy individuals will be stronger in the AD cases with stronger hippocampal atrophy. If confirmed, such disagree-ment could serve as an additional feature for classification. 1.1 Contributions Our contributions are three-fold. First, we provide a com-prehensive benchmark of five heterogeneous hippocampal segmentation methods, evaluating their accuracy, calibra-tion, and carbon footprint across two dementia cohorts and one epilepsy cohort. From this benchmark, we identify a resource-efficient triad — InnerEye, FastSurfer, and Hip-poSeg — that matches the full ensemble’s Dice performance while reducing inference CO 2 emissions by approximately 82%, and show that simple averaging of probabilistic out-puts yields the best integration strategy. Second, we demon-strate that uncertainty estimates derived from the triad’s probabilistic outputs can support automated quality control: aggregated uncertainty successfully flags gross segmenta-tion failures across datasets, and correlates significantly with motion artefact severity, suggesting a mechanism for automatically escalating problematic cases for expert review. Third, we investigate the clinical utility of ensemble-derived uncertainty for disease classification, and show that combin-ing uncertainty metrics with hippocampal volume features improves discrimination between cognitively normal and Alzheimer’s disease groups.

As initial benchmarking, the five individual segmenta-tion methods, all possible triads formed from them, and the full ensemble of five methods were compared on the ADNI dataset. The same ADNI dataset was therefore used both to identify the best-performing triad and to report its ADNI performance. Consequently, any performance advantage of the selected triad over other sub-ensembles on ADNI should be interpreted as an in-sample estimate that may be partially inflated by selection on the same data. The Local Dementia and Local Epilepsy datasets were not used for triad selection and therefore provide the held-out assessment of cross-domain segmentation performance and calibration. For each triad and for the full ensemble, the final prediction mask was obtained via majority voting. Performance was as-sessed in terms of DSC, ECE, and estimated CO 2 emissions. The expected calibration error (ECE) of each sub-ensemble was approximated by interpreting the number of votes from the T = 3 ensemble members as discrete confidence levels. The relevant amended scripts are publicly available on GitHub: https://github.com/zfdiwkm/multi-software-uncertainty. 

2.5.1 Ensemble integration strategy 

Next, we compared different strategies for integrating the predictions of the best-performing triad to assess their im-pact on segmentation accuracy and uncertainty calibration. These ensemble strategies were: (1) simple averaging of the output probabilities from each ensemble member (denoted as ‘Ensemble Averaged’ in the results), (2) weighting each prediction by the model’s confidence (‘Ensemble Weighted’), and (3) assigning each voxel to the prediction of the most confident model in a winner-takes-all approach (‘Ensemble Triage’). Due to non-normality of studied distributions, Friedman tests were performed to assess significant differ-ence in performance. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted with the Holm-Bonferroni method. Resource consumption was estimated in terms of duration and CO 2 expenditure for each method and each subject. 2.6 Uncertainty-based quality control We investigated whether ensemble-derived uncertainty can support automated quality control, both by identifying unreliable segmentation outputs and by flagging low image quality cases.

2.6.2 Motion artefact detection 

We further assessed whether uncertainty correlates with im-age quality in the presence of motion artefacts. Using the MR-ART dataset, uncertainty metrics were computed for each hippocampus and grouped by the image quality score (1 = good, 2 = medium, 3 = bad) assigned by expert neu-roradiologists. To test for a monotonic relationship between quality score and uncertainty, a within-subject permutation test was performed (10,000 permutations), using Spear-man’s rank correlation as the test statistic. Quality score labels were permuted within each subject to preserve the repeated-measures structure. Pairwise comparisons (Score 2 vs 1; Score 3 vs 2) were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the subset of subjects with scans in both categories being compared (n = 37 and n = 43 subjects, respectively). For each subject, uncertainty values were av-eraged across hippocampi within each quality category prior to testing. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Bonferroni method, applied across all six pairwise tests (3 metrics × 2 comparisons). 2.7 Uncertainty for Alzheimer’s disease classification We investigated whether ensemble-derived uncertainty could improve classification of Alzheimer’s disease from hippocam-pal features. From the ADNI baseline stratified cohort, 150 subjects were selected with balanced representation across diagnostic groups (50 were cognitively normal, 50 with mild cognitive impairment, and 50 with Alzheimer’s disease). Hippocampal volumes were extracted from each segmenta-tion method and normalised by intracranial volume (ICV), with ICV estimates provided by HIPPOSEG. Voxel-wise uncertainty metrics (sample variance, mutual information, and predictive entropy) were aggregated as the mean across the hippocampal ROI for each hemisphere, then averaged across hemispheres to yield subject-level features. This scalar aggregation was chosen to keep the feature space small relative to the cohort size and to reduce the risk of overfitting that would be introduced by higher-dimensional spatial uncertainty features. Age and sex were included as covariates. To assess the discriminative power brought by uncertainty estimates we compared models including

## 3. Results 

3.1 Ensemble performance & calibration 

3.1.1 Benchmarking cost and performance 

The ensemble consisting of InnerEye, FastSurfer, and Hip-poSeg produced segmentations at the same level of accuracy as an ensemble consisting of all five softwares, while sub-stantially reducing carbon emissions (from 4.65 kg to 0.85 kg CO 2eq for processing the 30 ADNI patients) and retain-ing a comparably well-calibrated uncertainty measure (Fig. 2). For the remainder of the paper, any mention of ’ Triad ’will therefore refer to this specific ensemble. 

3.1.2 Ensemble integration strategy 

Simple mean averaging of the ensemble members’ proba-bilistic outputs yielded the highest Dice performance across all datasets (Fig. 3, p < 0.05 after Holm-Bonferroni cor-rection). For HD95, no significant difference was observed between the mean ensemble and other ensemble integration strategies on the epilepsy dataset. On the Local Demen-tia dataset, the mean ensemble did not significantly differ from InnerEye, while it significantly outperformed all other methods (Fig. 3, p < 0.05 , Holm-Bonferroni corrected). 

3.1.3 Uncertainty calibration 

The averaged probabilistic output by the Triad members produced the best calibrated confidences, with an ECE of 0.05 across all three datasets (Fig. 4). The second most calibrated segmentation method was HippoSeg, with an ECE slightly higher than 0.05 averaged across all datasets. InnerEye (ECE ≈ 0.08 ) and FastSurfer in particular (ECE 

≈ 0.16 ) were less well-calibrated. 3.2 Uncertainty-based quality control 

3.2.1 Silent failure detection 

Applying dataset-specific uncertainty thresholds demon-strated varying effectiveness across UQ metrics and datasets (Fig. 3). For Dice-based outlier detection (Dice < 0.82), entropy achieved the highest performance with 91.7% recall and 44.2% precision, while variance and mutual information both achieved 83% recall with ∼35% precision. For HD95-based outlier detection (HD95 > 2.5mm), entropy again performed best with 88.9% recall and 40.6% precision, com-pared to variance and mutual information achieving 55.6% recall with 28.9% and 30.6% precision, respectively. 

3.2.2 Motion artifact detection 

A significant monotonic relationship was observed between image quality score and uncertainty across all three metrics (permutation test, p < 0.01 ; Spearman ρ = 0 .30 –0.36 ). Pairwise comparisons showed that uncertainty was signif-icantly higher for clinically unusable scans (score 3) com-pared to medium quality scans (score 2) for all metrics (Wilcoxon signed-rank, p < 0.05 , Holm-adjusted; Fig. 5). However, the difference between good (score 1) and medium (score 2) quality scans was not significant ( p = 0 .18 ). En-tropy showed the strongest effect, with the largest corre-lation coefficient ( ρ = 0 .36 ) and most significant pairwise difference ( p < 0.001 ). 3.3 Uncertainty for Alzheimer’s disease classification Adding uncertainty metrics to hippocampal volume features did modestly improve classification of Alzheimer’s disease. For CN vs AD classification, the volume-only baseline (total intracranial volume (TIV)-normalized bilateral hippocampal volume with age and sex covariates) achieved an AUC of 0.81 ± 0.04. The best-performing model (Full_mean, logistic regression) achieved AUC 0.87 ± 0.03, representing a modest improvement of +0.06 (permutation test, p < 0.001; Fig. 6). In practical terms, using the Full_mean feature set led to one additional subject (out of 49) being correctly identified as AD and four additional subjects (out of 48) being correctly identified as CN (Fig. 7). Adding any individual uncertainty feature to volume (Vol+UQ variants) provided no benefit over volume alone ( ∆AUC ≈ 0, p > 0.05). For CN vs MCI (baseline AUC 0.73) and MCI vs AD (baseline AUC 0.70) classifications, uncertainty features consistently were unable to improve predictive performance (supplementary figures S1 to S4). 

## 4. Discussion 

The current study comprehensively evaluated ensembles of heterogeneous hippocampal segmentation methods, and proposes the triad of InnerEye, FastSurfer, and HippoSeg as the optimal combination for achieving high segmentation performance while minimising resource cost and maintaining good calibration. This comparison should be interpreted as a within-study inference benchmark on a fixed hardware and energy-mix setting, rather than as a full lifecycle carbon assessment or as a comparison with substantially larger models. The approximately 82% reduc-tion reflects the exclusion of the two most computationally expensive tools, ASHS and FreeSurfer, relative to the five-tool ensemble on the ADNI-30 benchmark. Other efficient paradigms, including lightweight or parameter-efficient ar-chitectures such as neural cellular automata, may offer further reductions and could be valuable components of future ensembles. Further, our comparison of ensemble integration strategies showed that simple mean averaging performed best, consistent with prior findings by Müller et al. Future work could investigate whether extracting more complex features from uncertainty maps further increases the added value of uncertainty for classification tasks. 4.4 Uncertainty decomposition On the topic of uncertainty decomposition it has to be noted that the current study does not make strong claims about the type of uncertainty captured by the presented methods. Traditionally, uncertainty is separated in aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, where ensemble-derived uncer-tainty metrics are viewed to focus more on the epistemic uncertainty. Changes in image quality - themselves in-creasing aleatoric uncertainty - can however also present a distribution shift that may then result in higher ensemble disagreement if the resulting discrepancy to the training data varies between the ensemble members. Consequently, the ensemble derived metrics (entropy, variance, and mu-tual information) cannot be easily mapped onto aleatoric or epistemic origin. 4.5 Limitations Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample sizes were relatively small, particularly for the local epilepsy dataset (n=8), which limits the generalis-ability of the findings. Second, with respect to identifying the optimal ensemble members, it should be noted that the triad was selected and evaluated on partly overlapping data (ADNI dataset), which may have introduced selection bias. Third, the use of FreeSurfer v6.0.0 may have introduced a potential confound when comparing FreeSurfer against the other methods, as performance differences may partly reflect version-specific effects rather than the underlying algorithm alone. Fourth, we did not evaluate the broader family of uncertainty-aware segmentation models, such as Probabilis-tic U-Net, PhiSeg, variational inference-based methods, or stochastic segmentation networks. These approaches may capture spatially structured segmentation ambiguity more directly than post hoc ensemble disagreement. Future work should compare this pragmatic ensemble-based approach against specialised probabilistic architectures. Another limi-tation of the present work is that it focuses exclusively on post-hoc methods for uncertainty estimation. The advan-tage of post-hoc methods is that the ensemble members can be replaced easily, should better segmentation models become available, which potentially makes these methods more attractive for clinical translation due to ease of im-plementation as well as future-proofing. However, it is acknowledged that segmentation robustness might bene-fit from incorporating uncertainty in the training process, which future work might want to explore. We further did not compare ensemble disagreement with alternative post-hoc, model-agnostic approaches, such as temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) or conformal prediction. Conformal methods for semantic segmentation can transform model outputs into set-valued predictions with statistical risk or coverage guarantees, providing a principled alternative to ensemble disagreement as a proxy for uncertainty (Mossina et al., 2024). These guarantees require a representative held-out calibration set and rely on exchangeability between calibration and test data. Future work should directly compare these approaches in terms of calibration, failure detection, robustness to distribution shift, and computational cost. While AECE is more suitable for skewed confidence distributions than standard ECE, it still has important limi-tations (Nixon et al., 2019). AECE can remain sensitive to the choice of bin count, which was addressed in the current work by evaluating multiple bin counts. Moreover, collaps-ing model calibration into a single value risks concealing patterns of over- and underconfidence across confidence ranges, as errors can cancel out within individual bins. Lastly, in the classification experiment, voxel-wise uncer-tainty maps were aggregated into a single scalar value per hippocampus, thereby discarding spatial patterns of uncer-tainty. This loss of spatial information may partly explain the mixed findings in the experiment on disease group clas-sification, and future work could investigate whether more complex uncertainty features that incorporate spatial struc-ture carry additional discriminative value for downstream clinical tasks. 4.6 Conclusion In conclusion, the evaluated triad-ensemble offers a prac-tical compromise between segmentation accuracy, uncer-tainty calibration, and computational cost, reducing CO 2

emissions by approximately 82% compared to the full five-method ensemble. Entropy-based uncertainty aggregation provides a useful mechanism for flagging potential segmen-tation failures. For downstream clinical tasks, uncertainty shows promise for detecting motion artefact severity, as well as adding modest improvements in predictive power in Alzheimer’s classification. Future work will investigate more complex uncertainty derived features to potentially further aid classification, as well as incorporating longitudinal data, to investigate the potential of UQ-supported detection of hippocampal atrophy.
