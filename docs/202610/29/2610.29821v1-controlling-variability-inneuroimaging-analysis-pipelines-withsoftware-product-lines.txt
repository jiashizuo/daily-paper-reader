Controlling Variability in Neuroimaging Analysis
Pipelines with Software Product Lines
Gauthier LE BARTZ LYAN1[0000−0003−4711−6636], Paul
TEMPLE1[0000−0002−8276−0593], Boris CLENET1[0009−0006−7727−5100], and

Univ Rennes, Inria, CNRS, IRISA
Abstract. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an acquisition tech-
nique used for medical diagnosis. It is notably used for structural and
functional MRI (or fMRI) image analysis. MRI data is known to be com-
plex and noisy, so pipelines are created to extract and enhance relevant
information in retrieved images. The NARPS study highlighted the sig-
nificant variability that can emerge from different pipelines set up by in-
dependent teams while trying to answer the same research question about
brain functioning. Having different implementations realizing the same
task is a typical playground for software product lines (SPLs), which may
be out of the sight of neuroimage practitioners. Thus, proposing an SPL
out of these different pipelines falls back on a typical reverse-engineering
problem with its usual difficulties. In this document, we advocate for
the conceptual adoption of SPL principles as a promising direction to
improve reproducibility and consistency in neuroimaging research.
Keywords: fMRI, Medical Imaging, Variability, Reproducibility, Soft-
ware Product Line, Software Engineering
1
Introduction
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), a non-invasive medical imaging technique,
has highly improved medical diagnosis as a complement to X-ray and other kinds
of analyses. MRI is used mostly for two types of brain studies: structural analy-
sis and functional analysis. Structural analysis involves combining images from
one or multiple subjects to highlight specific anatomical regions of the brain.
Functional analysis, on the other hand, aggregates brain images over time, in-
troducing a temporal dimension. This allows researchers to identify regions of the
brain that become active (or inactive) in response to specific events. For exam-
ple, functional MRI (fMRI) can reveal what happens in the brain when a human
subject is asked to answer a question or move a part of their body. Despite many
advantages, MRI data present significant challenges in terms of analysis and in-
terpretation. A variety of noise sources can compromise data quality, including
biological variability (such as differences in brain structure between individuals),
subject movement during acquisition, and technical artifacts arising from image
processing, such as interpolation or rescaling.

To extract meaningful signals from complex MRI data and minimize noise,
neuroscientists rely on software suites that assemble different components that
we call pipelines. However, no formal conventions exist to describe these pipelines,
despite initiatives like COBIDAS [14] that are promoting standardized best prac-
tices for analysis. The NARPS study conducted by Botvinik-Nezer et al. [3] in-
vestigated how different research teams process a single fMRI dataset in response
to a set of closed binary research questions (i.e., with possible answers being only
’yes’ or ’no’). The pipelines were unconstrained, letting research teams use their
usual software suites and create the pipeline they want. The NARPS study re-
vealed that even when using the same data and hypotheses, widely used software
tools can produce non-consistent results. This means that, when aggregating the
different results to answer the research questions, observations could lead to half
the research teams answering ’yes’ while the other half would answer ’no’. These
concerns were later reinforced by Bowring et al. [4].
In a similar vein, Germani [8] examined the analytical variability arising
from different processing methods and proposed several improvements to en-
hance the validity of neuroimaging studies. Vila et al. [17] demonstrated that
variations in computing hardware can significantly affect the output of neu-
roimaging pipelines, showing that the same analysis can yield different results
when run on different systems. Chatelain et al. [5] investigated numerical vari-
ability in FMRIPrep [7,6], a widely used data preprocessing framework, and
showed that changes in its configuration can alter the final preprocessed images.
Finally, Sokolowski et al. [16] explored how software versioning impacts brain
structural analysis. They revealed that different versions of the same software
can produce statistically significant discrepancies in the results.

With so many layers of variability as shown in Figure 1, it is essential to un-
derstand what happens in each of them. To achieve this, establishing a mapping
of the impact of each layer is a necessary first step. This requires the ability
to explore every layer using appropriate protocols and tools. In particular, the
layer representing the ability to choose between different software is one of the
most challenging. These software have no codebase in common or do not neces-

sarily share coding conventions, thus reasoning and associating the realization of
different functionalities is difficult. In addition, once the association is done, com-
paring the same functionality in different software requires numerous dedicated
pipelines, extensive runs, and the generation of large volumes of data.
In this paper, we demonstrate that NARPS Open Pipelines, an open source
pipeline suite, exhibits SPL-like characteristics and supports a structured vari-
ability analysis. While we do not formally define the SPL architecture, our ob-
servations suggest shared components, variation points, and modular structure,
key hallmarks of SPLs. We share our experience in trying to replicate pipelines
from NARPS and exhibit the challenges we faced that could be alleviated by
moving to a SPL structure. We also propose discussions about the specificities of
SPL in the current neuro-imaging world. Furthermore, neuro-imaging could be
a playground to further push the exploration of layers of variability as proposed
by Lesoil [11].
The rest of the paper is structured as follows: first, we explain how neuroimag-
ing pipelines are designed and highlight the room for variability management in
Section 2. Then, Section 3 reports our experience in replicating some of the
pipelines from the NARPS study and the challenges we have faced. Section 4
summarizes our experience and draws some future directions and challenges to
adopt a variability-aware strategy in the development of neuroimaging pipelines.

Background
2.1
Neuroimaging software
Nipype [9] is a widely used middleware for neuroimaging workflow management.
It aims at reducing software heterogeneity and encapsulating domain-specific
processing concepts. By providing a standardized interface for integrating di-
verse tools (e.g., FSL, SPM, AFNI), Nipype enables researchers to focus on
data flow and analysis design rather than underlying software architecture or
version-specific dependencies. However, seamlessly switching between different
neuroimaging tools or workflows remains challenging without requiring signif-
icant manual intervention, including rewriting all or part of the pipelines and
modifying the runtime environment [12,13]. This harms the code quality of work-
flows and limits the ability to explore the pipelines’ configuration space. In the
meantime, these pipelines are designed to process similar data and provide an-
swers to research questions defined by neuroimaging practitioners. Hence, our
interest in using software product lines to explore this context.
2.2
Software Product Lines
With the mass customization industry, the software engineering development
process has changed from developing a single piece of software for specific usages
of a particular customer to developing multiple similar software systems that
share a common code base and address multiple customers’ usages at once, the

so-called Software Product Lines (SPL). This way, reusing becomes a central
concern of software development. Pohl et al. [15] argue that reuse as much as
possible brings (1) a reduced development cost, since less code is written; mostly
glue code has to be created which is usually smaller than developing a complete
functionality; (2) a reduced time-to-market since the product is not developed
from scratch; (3) an improved code quality, since with the reusability concern
in mind, newly developed code should be modularized. Thus easing testing,
readability and reducing the complexity and technical debt of future code.
Previous industrial success stories have been reported in the SPLC confer-
ence1. These companies developed multiple products for different needs but
wanted to capitalize on previous work. They were aware that complete parts
of their products were generic and only some other parts would need custom
code for customers’ specific needs. Thus, based on the multiple already devel-
oped products, they tried to reverse engineer what would be the structure of
their software product lines. We think that neuroimaging is in a similar context,
especially with tools like Nypipe that simplify the development of pipelines by
interfacing different tools. However, the different pipelines, which make up the
SPL, would not be owned by a unique entity as it is the case in industrial success
stories mentioned before.
2.3
Potential impacts for neuroimaging research and their pipelines
The growing body of evidence on variability in neuroimaging [3,4] shows that
differences in software, versions, and environments can lead to divergent results.
Yet, as the pipelines and the tools are built differently, performing comparisons
seems difficult. Ablation studies seem impossible as each tool is intricated with
another, the definitions of functions are specific to each tool, etc. However, some
strategies remain, and from a high-level view, some functionalities or successions
of functions can be identified (e.g., noise reduction or blur), which suggests that
software product lines could be interesting to further analyze this variety of
pipelines.
Hereafter, we report on our experience replicating neuroimaging pipelines
and highlight some challenges that we foresaw. We would like to remind that
this work is exploratory. It is based on a single dataset and a limited set of
pipelines SPM122 associated with MATLAB. It does not claim to generalize
across all neuroimaging tools or software stacks. Our findings are preliminary
and intended to stimulate further research into the application of SPL principles
in neuroimaging pipeline design.

As of late 2025, only a limited number of pipelines have been successfully
reproduced. This progress is hindered by the significant challenges of retro-
engineering pipelines based on sparse, often informal documentation, primarily
recorded in spreadsheet files with limited technical details. The lack of clarity
in descriptions, combined with differences in software versions and implemen-
tation choices, has led to substantial reinterpretation, development overhead,
and testing effort. These challenges emphasize the difficulties of translating ad
hoc, team-specific workflows into reproducible, maintainable, and transparent
software systems.
Using an ad hoc Dockerfile generator5 inspired from Neurodocker’s core
Dockerfile6, we successfully ran three pipelines (named after their anonymized
teams) from NARPS Open Pipelines7, 2T6S, U26C and V55J, all based on the
same software stack that uses SPM12 and MATLAB.
We specifically investigated aspects of deep variability in neuroimaging analy-
sis, drawing on recent work in reproducibility [11,1,2]. Focusing on preprocessing,
image manipulation, and statistical map generation, we conducted controlled ex-
periments by performing mutually exclusive swaps of key software components,
including MATLAB, SPM12 release version, and underlying hardware platforms
(Intel and AMD x86 CPUs, and Apple ARM architectures). To ensure repro-
ducibility and isolate environmental influences, all tests were executed within
dedicated, isolated Docker containers.8. Roughly, our replications take raw im-
ages as inputs, apply the signal processing steps described in the pipelines, and
superimpose the resulting visualization images to detect differences.
The retrieved results exhibit minor numerical discrepancies in voxel values,
ranging from 6e−5 to 1e−2. Figure 2 is a representative example of the mag-
nitude and importance of discrepancies that were observed. These differences
are small and seem negligible; they are below typical thresholds used in fMRI
statistical analysis (e.g., p < 0.05). This suggests a degree of robustness in the
application of core operators across different MATLAB versions, and further in-
dicates that variations in hardware platforms, such as transitions between x86
and ARM architectures, do not introduce substantial deviations in computed
results. However, their impact on statistical inference should be evaluated by
domain experts, particularly when studying sensitive brain regions, as we are
non-experts in this domain.

However, despite their apparent insignificance in the context of final con-

that these differences should be evaluated by domain experts, who can assess
whether such magnitudes are clinically or statistically meaningful. More impor-
tantly, highlighting these variations raises awareness among researchers about
the sensitivity of (f)MRI processing to environmental factors such as software
version, platform architecture, and computational environment. This emphasizes
the critical need for standardized, well-documented, and reproducible processing
pipelines that explicitly control and report such environmental variables, ensur-
ing both transparency and scientific rigor in neuroimaging analysis. Finally, our
empirical analysis of pipeline variability reveals three key SPL characteristics: (1)
shared core modules across pipelines; (2) variation points where environmental
factors can be configured; and (3) modular structure enabled by containerization.
3.1
Towards Software Product Lines for (f)MRI analysis
With our current experiment, we have examined only a limited subset of the neu-
roimaging software ecosystem. SPM (and its MATLAB-based environment) are
by no means the only adopted tools within the community. Therefore, general-
izing our findings requires a comprehensive evaluation across a broader range of
existing neuroimaging software packages. However, as highlighted in Section 3,
reproducing and validating processing pipelines across diverse tools is highly
resource-intensive. Furthermore, while middleware such as Nipype offers partial
integration between tools, it often lacks complete or standardized interfaces for
each platform. As a result, dedicated pipelines would need to be developed for
every major software suite and testing purposes. Such a comprehensive effort
remains infeasible with current resources. Figure 3a illustrates the vast number
of distinct pipelines that would be required to test only a representative sam-
ple of existing neuroimaging tools, demonstrating the practical limitations of a
broad-scale validation approach at present.
One conceptual solution to the fragmentation of neuroimaging pipelines is
to introduce a standardized software interface that every neuroimaging tool,
whether developed by commercial vendors, open-source communities, or individ-
ual researchers, should implement. This interface would enable middleware com-

hances reproducibility and portability but also provides a scalable foundation
for managing variability, promoting interoperability, and enabling transparent,
plug-and-play analysis workflows.
3.2
Challenges
The main problem to adopt such an approach is that all stakeholders provid-
ing software within the neuroimaging community need to reconsider how their
tools are designed and made accessible to external users. This implies the need
for a standardized, well-defined interface that specifies the core capabilities a

neuroimaging software component must offer to support consistent and interop-
erable MRI data processing. Such an interface, which would outline mandatory
features, data formats, and functional requirements, could be established through
a collaborative effort involving software developers, researchers, and domain ex-
perts. It might emerge as a community-led initiative, a consortium of providers,
or, initially, as a proposal from a single entity offering a pilot interface. Crucially,
given the diversity and autonomy of current stakeholders, a compelling first step
would be a proof-of-concept demonstrating the feasibility and benefits of such
a standardized interface. A successful, publicly shared proof-of-concept would
serve as a catalyst to build community engagement, foster trust, and establish
a foundation for broader adoption.
4
Conclusion
The analysis of variability in neuroimaging pipelines, particularly as revealed
by the NARPS study, demonstrates that current practices, while functionally
effective, lack structural coherence and reproducibility. Despite the diversity of
tools and workflows, the shared architectural patterns and configurable compo-
nents across pipelines suggest a natural alignment with the principles of Software
Product Line (SPL) engineering.
Our empirical exploration of the NARPS open pipelines project, including
controlled experiments on software versions, hardware platforms, and process-
ing configurations, reveals that while minor numerical differences occur, these
are often within clinically and statistically insignificant bounds and are most
probably absorbed by downstream analysis. This highlights both the robustness
of core processing operations and the critical need for greater transparency and
control over environmental variables.
However, the absence of standardized interfaces and interoperable middle-
ware remains a major barrier to consistent, reproducible, and scalable pipeline
development. To address this, we advocate for the adoption of an SPL-inspired
framework, one that enables a “Code Once, Run Anywhere” (CORA) paradigm
through a shared, well-defined interface for neuroimaging software components.
This would allow researchers to build reusable, configurable pipelines that dy-
namically plug in different backends (e.g., FSL, SPM, AFNI, FreeSurfer) without
requiring extensive rework or manual intervention.
Implementing such a vision requires a collaborative, community-driven ini-
tiative to establish a standardized software interface, supported by a proof-of-
concept demonstrating feasibility and benefits. While technical and organiza-
tional challenges remain, the potential gains in reproducibility, transparency,
and scientific rigor make this a compelling and necessary step forward.
