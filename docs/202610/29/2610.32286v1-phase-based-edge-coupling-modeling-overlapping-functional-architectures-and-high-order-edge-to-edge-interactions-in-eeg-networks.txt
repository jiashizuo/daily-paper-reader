Phase-based Edge Coupling: Modeling 
Overlapping Functional Architectures and High-
Order Edge-to-Edge Interactions in EEG 
Networks 
 
Chengcheng Wu, Zichen Shi, Jiayang Xu, Shanbao Tong, Senior Member, IEEE, Xiaoli Guo, Senior 
Member, IEEE
Abstract—Network neuroscience has traditionally relied 
on node-centric approaches, which are inherently limited in 
capturing the brain’s overlapping functional architectures 
and higher-order edge-to-edge interactions. The emerging 
edge-centric paradigm addresses this gap by shifting the 
analytical unit from nodes to edges. However, while edge-
centric models have been established for DTI and fMRI, 
comparable EEG methodologies have yet to be developed. In 
this study, we proposed a Phase-based Edge-centric 
Coupling (PEC) algorithm for EEG network modelling, where 
edge time series are derived from instantaneous phase 
differences of EEG signals and edge-to-edge coupling is 
quantified via normalized inner product. Simulation tests 
demonstrated the robustness of PEC algorithm, achieving 
high accuracy (<10% relative error) in coupling estimation 
and discriminative capacity (AUROC > 0.7) against spurious 
coupling. When provided sufficient data length (≥16 cycles), 
PEC outperformed traditional Phase Node-centric Coupling 
(PNC) in both individual identifiability and cognitive-state 
identifiability, enabling superior characterization of stable 
individual neural signatures and dynamic task-dependent 
functional configurations. Furthermore, unlike PNC’s hard-
partitioned modularity, PEC captured highly overlapping yet 
functionally specialized modular structures and connector 
hubs, aligning with task-engaged cognitive processes. 
Applied to dyadic interactions, PEC identified distinct cross-
brain modules encoding symmetric and asymmetric neural 
coordination. These findings establish PEC as a valid and 
powerful tool for EEG network modelling, providing new 
insights 
into 
the 
brain’s 
complex, 
multifunctional 
organization at both intra-brain and inter-brain networks. 
Index Terms— Edge-centric coupling, EEG, Phase 
synchronization, Differential identifiability, Overlapping 
communities

I. INTRODUCTION 
HE brain is a quintessential complex system, whose 
remarkable computational power arises from intricate 
interactions among billions of neural elements. 
Network neuroscience has provided a powerful mathematical 
language to describe this complexity, modeling the brain as a 
graph where nodes represent neural units and edges denote 
their statistical or physical dependencies [1]. This framework 
has been instrumental in uncovering fundamental principles 
of brain organization, such as modular segregation and small-
world efficiency, which balance specialized local processing 
with integrated global communication [2, 3]. However, the 
dominant analytical paradigm in this field, the node-centric 
connectivity analysis, confines analytical units to vertices and 
thus inherently limits our ability to fully capture the brain’s 
dynamic and multifaceted functional architecture. 
One of the fundamental limitations of the node-centric 
paradigm is its rigid modularity modeling. Standard node-
centric community detection algorithms assign each node to a 
single, 
non-overlapping 
module, 
producing 
a 
“hard-
partitioned” representation of brain organization [4]. However, 
a growing body of evidence demonstrated that the brain’s 
functional modules are highly overlapping [5-7]. Many cortical 
regions, such as the precuneus and anterior insula, are not 
dedicated to a single cognitive process but serve as flexible 
hubs involved in multiple functional systems—from the default 
mode network to the frontoparietal control network—
depending the tasks [6]. These flexible hubs, also known as 
connector hubs, facilitate inter-community connections to 
promote information integration across different functional 
modules [5]. This multifunctionality is not a system bug but an 
evolutionary adaptation, enabling efficient allocation of finite

community membership, node-centric methods systematically 
obscure this critical feature of brain organization, leading to 
simplified and potentially misleading functional mappings. 
Although overlapping community detection algorithms, such as 
Clique Percolation Method (CPM) [8], the Stochastic 
Variational Inference for the Mixed-membership Stochastic 
Block Model (SVINET) [9], the Affiliation Graph Model 
(AGMFIT) [10], have been proposed, their efficacy remains 
fundamentally constrained by the node-centric framework, 
making them difficult to achieve balance between excessive 
community overlap (blurring partition distinctiveness) and high 
proportions of unclassified vertices (undermining coverage), 
resulting in unstable partition results [11]. 
A more fundamental limitation is that the node-centric 
paradigm is intrinsically incapable of modeling higher-order 
edge-to-edge interactions, which are crucial for understanding 
the complex functions of the brain. Traditional node-centric 
network metrics (e.g., degree, clustering coefficient) essentially 
describe the topological properties of vertices, while 
overlooking connections as fundamental units of information 
processing. However, emerging evidence suggested that brain 
functional organization is not solely encoded in region-to-
region connectivity, but fundamentally relies on the 
coordinated activity of connection ensembles serving integrated 
computations [12]. The functional characteristics of vertices in 
brain networks are largely determined by their connectivity 
relationships, which inherently contain rich structural and 
dynamic information [12]. By aggregating edges to form multi-
level collective structures such as motifs, fingerprints, and edge 
communities, cross-system collaborative patterns can be 
revealed [12-14]. Furthermore, analysis of edge time series 
indicated that transient patterns of functional connectivity in the 
brain are driven by synchronous fluctuations of grouped edges 
rather than simple summation of vertex activities [12]. 
Therefore, in-depth exploration of brain function requires not 
only a vertex-centric perspective but also an edge-oriented 
approach.  
Meanwhile, edge connectivity analysis is not only applicable 
to intra-brain networks but also holds significant relevance for 
inter-brain interaction research. In traditional node-centric 
paradigm, we can only examine the connectivity relationships 
between vertices belonging to different brains. However, inter-
brain network research indicated that brain functional 
organization is reflected not only in inter-regional connectivity 
patterns but also in the synergy, coupling, and higher-order 
network structures formed through cross-brain connections [15, 
16]. Furthermore, edge connectivity analysis enables the 
examination of functional relationships between “intra-brain 
edges” and “inter-brain edges”, providing novel analytical 
approaches for hyper-network research spanning single-brain 
and multi-brain systems.  
To model overlapping functional architectures and high-
order edge-to-edge interactions, an edge-centric framework has 
been introduced to complex network theory [17]. Unlike node-
centric approaches, edge-centric connectivity analysis treats 
"edges" (i.e., connections between vertices) as fundamental 
units and functional entities with intrinsic temporal dynamics. 
By directly quantifying the coupling between edges, for 
instance, 
through 
their 
time-varying 
co-fluctuations, 
researchers can construct an “edge network” to analyze network 
organizational structures and functional characteristics. 
Crucially, this framework inherently reveals overlapping 
modular structures, i.e., clustering edges into non-overlapping 
“link communities” and projecting them onto constituent nodes 
automatically generates overlapping node communities, 
thereby reflecting the brain’s multifunctional architecture [17]. 
Furthermore, this perspective provides a universal toolkit for 
probing higher-order network dynamics, as it directly addresses 
the question of how connection ensembles collectively support 
cognitive processes through their coordinated activities. 
This new framework has significantly advanced our 
understanding of the human connectome. In structural imaging, 
edge-centric analysis of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data has 
revealed that white matter pathways constitute a highly 
interwoven, overlapping scaffold underpinning regional 
multifunctionality [18]. In functional magnetic resonance 
imaging (fMRI), an edge‑centric functional connectivity (eFC) 
algorithm has been developed to capture co‑fluctuations 
between brain‑region pairs by tracking the dynamics of 
connection ensembles, demonstrating superior subject‑specific 
fingerprinting and overlapping modular modeling compared to 
traditional nodal approaches [4, 19]. These methods have 
undergone sustained advancement through both model 
refinement and clinical application. Novelli et al. systematically 
elucidated the theoretical foundations of edge-centric 
functional connectivity from a mathematical modeling 
perspective, and proposed a unified framework for formalizing 
the generation, measurement, and statistical inference of edge 
time series, thereby providing crucial support for the 
standardization and reproducibility of edge-centric methods 
[20]. Jones et al. integrated edge time series with generalized 
linear models (GLMs), establishing an "Edge-Based GLM" 
analytical framework that enables real-time tracking of higher-
order cognitive states at (sub)-second time scales, significantly 
enhancing the analytical capacity of functional connectivity for 
fine-grained cognitive processes [21]. Zamani Esfahlani et al. 
applied edge-centric dynamic network analysis to clinical 
neuroscience, revealing that children with Autism Spectrum 
Disorder exhibit abnormal dynamic stability in specific edge 
communities during social cognitive tasks [22]. 
However, as a novel analytic framework, the current 
approaches of the edge-centric analysis remain predominantly 
confined to DTI and fMRI modalities. Electroencephalography 
(EEG), with its millisecond temporal resolution, offers the ideal 
modality for capturing the rapid, real-time co-fluctuations 
between edges. The absence of an EEG-based edge-centric 
approach thus constitutes a critical methodological gap in this 
area.  
In this study, we developed a Phase-based Edge-centric 
Coupling (PEC) algorithm—a novel pipeline for EEG edge 
connectivity analysis, with comprehensive validation and tests.

III. SIMULATIONS 
A. EEG Dataset for Simulation 
To validate the accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of the 
PEC algorithm in detecting phase synchronization between 
edge pairs, we designed two simulation tests based on real EEG 
signals. The dataset for simulation (Dataset 1) includes 1 min 
eye-open resting-state EEG data from 45 healthy adults (6 
males and 39 females, average age = 45.83 ± 14.35 years) 
recorded via a 32-channel wireless EEG system (NeuSen.W32, 
Neuracle Technology Co., Ltd., China) at a sampling rate of 
1000 Hz. Electrode montage followed the international 10-20 
system, with FCz as reference, AFz as ground, and electrode 
impedances maintained below 10 kΩ. The dataset was collected 
in our previous study [26] approved by the Ethics Committee 
of Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the School of 
Medicine, Hangzhou Normal University (20221111001).

MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the EEGLAB toolbox 
[27]. First, EEG signals were band-pass filtered at 1-30 Hz 
using a FIR filter, followed by a 50 Hz notch filter to remove 
the power line interference. Then, noisy electrodes and highly

IV. DIFFERENTIAL IDENTIFIABILITY 
A. EEG Dataset for Differential Identifiability 
To validate the advantage of PEC in brain characterization 
over traditional PNC, we compared their performances in 
individual identifiability and cognitive-state identifiability. The 
dataset used for identifiability assessment (Dataset 2) includes 
dual EEG data from 51 mother-child dyads engaged in a 
collaborative tangram puzzle task. The dual EEG signals were 
recorded using two 32-channel NeuSen wireless EEG systems 
(sampling rate: 1000 Hz, reference: FCz, ground: AFz, 
impedance <10 kΩ). Specifically, maternal EEG data were 
selected for identifiability assessment, employing the same 
preprocessing pipeline as previously described. The dataset was 
collected in our previous studies [32, 33] approved by the 
Review Board of Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children's

B. Individual Identifiability 
The differential identifiability metric (denoted as 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙) was 
originally introduced by Amico and Goñi (2018) to compare the 
effectiveness 
of 
different 
functional 
connectivity 
representations 
in 
capturing 
individual-level 
neural 
characteristics [34]. This metric has since gained wide 
acceptance and is now considered a benchmark standard for 
evaluating the identifiability capacity of functional connectivity 
methods [19]. In this study, this metric was not only applied to 
quantify individual identifiability, but also extended to evaluate 
cognitive-state identifiability, providing a comprehensive 
assessment of capacity to represent stable individual 
characteristics and sensitivity to dynamic cognitive state 
transitions. 
For individual identifiability, we included 41 mothers (female, 
average age = 39.27 ± 7.69 years) with ≥9 artifact-free EEG 
epochs (2-s duration) during maternal puzzle-moving trials, and 
computed both PEC and PNC matrices for each trial. 
Specifically, the alpha band, which is crucial for adults in 
cognitive functioning, anticipatory and sustained attention 
[35],was then chosen for brain network construction. Then, we 
calculated pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients across all 
possible matrix pairs, categorizing them into: (1) within-subject 
pairs (from same participant) and (2) between-subject pairs 
(from different participants). 
The within-subject identifiability score (𝐼௦௘௟௙) was defined as 
the mean Pearson correlation coefficient across all within-
subject matrix pairs, where higher values reflect greater intra-
individual stability in connectivity patterns. Conversely, the 
between-subject identifiability score (𝐼௢௧௛௘
) was defined as 
the mean Pearson correlation coefficient across all between-
subject matrix pairs, with lower values indicate reduced 
similarity between individuals. 
The individual differential identifiability 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ was then 
calculated as: 
𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ = (𝐼௦௘௟௙−𝐼௢௧௛௘) × 100.
(13) 
A high 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ value indicates robust individual-specific 
neural fingerprinting, as evidenced by high within-subject 
similarity along with low between-subject similarity. 
To establish the statistical significance of  𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ 
differences between PNC and PEC, we performed bootstrap 
resampling (with replacement) to generate 1,000 subsamples, 
each containing 12 randomly selected participants (30% of 41). 
Then we computed 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ for both algorithms per subsample, 
and conducted paired t-tests.  
Before the analysis of differential identifiability, spectral 
analysis was performed to confirm the role of alpha band in the 
collaborative tangram puzzle task. The result showed that 
depressed alpha power during maternal acting periods 
compared to their observing periods (Fig. 4a), justified as the 
primary frequency band for differential identifiability analysis. 
At the group level, PEC outperformed PNC in individual 
identifiability, achieving a higher differential identifiability 
score overall ( 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ= 14.97 for PEC vs. 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ=
13.68 for PNC, n = 41, signal length = 2000 ms, Fig. 4b). This 
superiority was statistically validated via paired t-tests on 
bootstrap-resampled subsamples (Cohen’s d = 0.65, p < 0.001, 
signal length = 2000 ms, Fig. 4c). 
To investigate the impact of varying signal lengths on the 
individual identifiability of the two algorithms, we segmented 
the EEG signals into time windows of varying lengths, ranging 
from 200 ms to 8000 ms with 200 ms intervals. EEG signals 
were concatenated when native trial durations were insufficient 
to generate segments beyond 2000 ms. For each window length, 
we applied the described bootstrap resampling method (30% 
sampling rate with replacement and 1000 repetitions) to 
calculate the distribution of 𝐼ௗ௜௙௙ି௜௡ௗ values for both the PEC 
and PNC algorithms, and performed paired t-tests to assess their 
statistical significance. We found that PEC outperformed PNC 
significantly for signal lengths ≥ 1600 ms (Cohen’s d > 0.34, p 
< 0.001, Fig. 4d), with its superiority amplifying as signal 
length increased. Conversely, this advantage of PEC was absent 
at short signal lengths, and even showed a significantly lower 
individual identifiability than PNC at a signal length ≤ 1200 ms 
(Cohen’s d > 0.24, p < 0.001, Fig. 4d).

set of orthogonal bases—principal components (PCs)—ranked

V. INTRA-BRAIN NETWORK COMMUNITY DETECTION 
A. Leiden Community Detection Algorithm 
To evaluate the efficacy of PEC in identifying overlapping 
communities, we performed intra-brain network community 
detection analysis using the Leiden algorithm [39]. Among 
various community detection algorithms, those based on 
modularity maximization have gained wide adoption in large-
scale brain network analysis due to their computational 
efficiency and performance in handling weighted networks [40]. 
Especially, the Leiden algorithm provides an advanced variant 
that address the limitation of poorly connected communities in 
the standard Louvain method [39], ensuring well-connected and 
structurally cohesive communities.  
The EEG epochs (2-s duration, alpha band) during maternal 
puzzle-moving trials in Dataset 2 were used for community 
detection. To ensure robust network estimation, only 41 
mothers with ≥9 artifact-free epochs were included. Before 
community detection, we computed both PNC and PEC 
matrices for all the trials, and then these individual trial-level 
matrices were averaged to generate group-level PNC and PEC 
matrices. To eliminate spurious connections arising from noise 
or volume conduction effects while preserving the topologically 
significant backbone of the network, we applied proportional 
thresholding [41] by retaining the strongest 20% of connections 
in the matrices. This threshold was selected within the 
established optimal range (10%-30%) for balancing biological 
plausibility and topological stability of brain networks [42].  
The sparsified connectivity matrices were then subjected to 
community detection using the Leiden algorithm. For the PNC 
matrix, this process directly produces vertex communities; 
whereas for the PEC matrix, the algorithm first identifies edge 
communities, which are subsequently mapped to vertex 
communities by assigning each vertex to all communities 
containing its connected edges to gather the overlapping 
structure [4].  
Despite the inherent limitation of EEG’s low spatial 
resolution, the sensor-level functional communities inferred 
from EEG networks were compared against the Yeo 7-network 
cortical parcellation scheme [43], a template derived from 
resting-state fMRI data of 1,000 healthy adults, to establish 
their neuroanatomical validity. This reference framework 
divides the cerebral cortex into seven canonical functional 
networks: (1) Visual Network, (2) Somatomotor Network, (3) 
Dorsal Attention Network, (4) Ventral Attention Network, (5) 
Limbic Network, (6) Frontoparietal Control Network, and (7) 
Default Mode Network. The Yeo parcellation has demonstrated 
exceptional robustness across diverse populations and 
experimental conditions, recognized as a gold standard for 
functional brain organization [44].

VI. INTER-BRAIN NETWORK COMMUNITY DETECTION 
A. Bipartite Network Community Detection Using the 
BRIM Algorithm 
To examine edge-level mother-child neural coupling, we 
constructed a dyadic PEC matrix for each trial by computing 
PEC values between all possible mother-child edge pairs. Then, 
the group-level PEC matrix was obtained by averaging across 
trials and dyads, followed by 20% sparsity thresholding to 
retain robust inter-brain connections. Given the bipartite 
structure of inter-brain edge networks (edges from two brains 
form non-overlapping node sets), we applied the BRIM 
algorithm [52] to the group-level dyadic PEC matrix to identify 
cross-brain modules. This algorithm optimizes bipartite 
modularity 
through 
iterative 
alternating 
optimization, 
partitioning the two distinct node sets (child-edges and mother-
edges) into 
communities 
that 
maximize 
intra-module 
connectivity while minimizing inter-module connectivity.

modules encoding the symmetric and asymmetric interactive 
neurocognitive processes during dyadic interaction. This 
computational framework advances our understanding of how 
mother-child neural systems coordinate to support cooperative 
social engagement. 
VII. ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS 
In this study, we developed a phase-based edge coupling 
algorithm by constructing edge time series from instantaneous 
phase differences of EEG signals and quantifying edge-to-edge 
coupling via normalized inner product, addressing the 
methodological gap in edge-centric connectivity modeling for 
EEG network analysis. Simulation tests confirmed robustness 
of our algorithm, generally achieving <10% relative error in 
edge coupling strength estimation across frequency bands and 
AUROC 
>0.7 
in 
discriminating 
synchronized/non-
synchronized edge pairs, except at the extremely weak coupling 
condition (λ = 0.02). PEC significantly outperformed traditional 
node-centric PNC approaches in individual identifiability and 
cognitive-state identifiability, though these advantages required 
sufficient data length (>16 cycles) due to its edge-centric 
complexity. Unlike PNC’s hard-partitioned modularity, PEC 
showed potential to capture highly overlapping yet functionally 
specialized modular structures. It successfully identified four 
key modules that align with tangram-solving cognitive 
processes, including connector hubs (e.g., somatomotor and 
frontoparietal networks) that bridge functional systems. When 
applied to inter-brain analysis, PEC effectively decomposed 
cross-brain modules encoding the symmetric and asymmetric 
interactive neurocognitive processes during dyadic interaction, 
providing novel insights into the neural substrates of 
cooperative engagement. 
Although PEC algorithm demonstrates multiple advantages 
in brain functional characterization, several limitations must be 
acknowledged. First, edge networks exhibit inherent high-
dimensional 
complexity 
compared 
to 
node-centric 
representations, with edge counts scaling quadratically with the 
number of nodes (O(N2)). This dimensionality explosion 
generates substantial increases in storage requirements, 
computational load, and analytical overhead, posing challenges 
for large-scale neuroimaging studies (e.g., source level) or real-
time applications. Second, PEC’s computational complexity 
necessitates stringent data length requirements, as insufficient 
signal duration leads to truncated edge time series and high- 
variance coupling estimates. This constraint may limit its 
application to brief event-related paradigms where edge-centric 
modeling may fail to capture transient neural dynamics. Third, 
the current PEC exclusively quantifies phase-based coupling, 
constructing edges from instantaneous phase differences of 
nodal signals, and measuring edge-to-edge coupling from 
temporal consistency of their phase relationships, thereby 
inherently overlooking alternative neural synchronization 
mechanisms such as amplitude co-modulation. Future work 
should integrate diverse coupling metrics into the edge-centric 
framework.
