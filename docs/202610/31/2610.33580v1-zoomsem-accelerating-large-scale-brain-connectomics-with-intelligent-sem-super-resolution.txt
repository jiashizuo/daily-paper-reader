with intelligent SEM super-resolution

Abstract. Serial electron microscopy (EM) is essential for large-volume biological imaging and connectomics, yet its throughput remains a
major bottleneck, with cubic-millimeter-scale brain imaging requiring months or years. Existing deep learning–based EM super-resolution
methods are limited to small upscaling factors or rely on synthetic data, offering modest acceleration and limited practical impact. We
introduce ZoomSEM, a deep learning-based super-resolution method to dramatically accelerate SEM imaging. Trained on real low- and
high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM) pairs, ZoomSEM enables reliable recovery of 4×, 8×, and up to 16× higher-resolution
images from fast low-resolution scans while preserving fine neuronal structures critical for connectomic analysis, achieving up to 30-fold
effective acceleration. Extensive experiments across datasets of diverse biological samples demonstrate robust generalization under realistic
low-resolution acquisition. This advance enables high-resolution, large-volume serial EM imaging at unprecedented speed, poised to
transform connectomics and related fields.
Keywords: large-scale brain connectome; serial electron microscopy; deep learning; super-resolution
Introduction
High-resolution serial electron microscopy (EM) has become
a pivotal tool in modern biological research, particularly for
detailed mapping of brain connectomes at synaptic resolu-
tion [1–3]. Scanning electron microscopes (SEMs) routinely
achieve nanometer-level spatial resolution, revealing ultra-
structural details far beyond optical imaging. However, imag-
ing large volumes at this scale is painfully slow. Standard
single-beam SEMs scan sequentially one pixel per tens or
hundreds of nanoseconds. State-of-the-art multi-beam SEMs
(e.g. 61 or 91 beams) can theoretically capture ∼1.2 gigapixel
per second, but mechanical overhead (stage movement, auto-
focusing, etc.) limits effective throughput to only ∼0.2 Gpix/s
[4, 5]. Consequently, imaging a cubic millimeter of brain
tissue at nanometer resolution would typically take many
months or even years on current instruments [6]. Reduc-
ing pixel dwell time increases throughput at the expense of
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), i.e., a speed-SNR trade-off. Such
constraints pose a substantial bottleneck to the application of
serial EM, especially in large-scale brain connectomics.
To alleviate this, computational EM image resolution en-
hancement, often referred to as EM super-resolution, has
emerged as a promising strategy. Recent studies have shown
that deep neural networks [7] trained on paired low/high
resolution EM images can infer unresolved features in coarse
scans [8–14]. Despite notable progress in deep learning-based
approaches, several limitations continue to hinder their appli-
cation in practical EM imaging. First, most existing methods
[8, 10–12, 14] are limited to small upscaling factors and
degrade significantly in performance at higher magnifications.
Although a few studies have explored large-factor super-
resolution [12, 13], they typically rely on synthetic training
data generated by artificially degrading high-resolution EM
images, an approach that poorly reflects real EM degradation
processes. Second, current methods often fail to fully exploit
spatial continuity across serial EM sections. Many adopt
multi-frame techniques [15–18] from video super-resolution
[19–22] that rely on optical flow but overlook the structural
scale variance characteristic of serial EM images. Third,
previous methods primarily focused on algorithmic design
and improving performance on small-scale datasets, without
considering practical applicability to large-scale data.
In this work, we present ZoomSEM, a novel deep-learning
network that significantly enhances SEM imaging efficiency
by computationally restoring high-resolution ultrastructures
from low-resolution SEM images across multiple scales.
ZoomSEM is designed to support 4×, 8×, and 16× enhance-
ments in lateral resolution, allowing correspondingly coarser
scans (i.e., 4×, 8×, and 16× larger pixel size) to be collected
within a much shorter period of time and then computation-
ally refined to nanometer detail. To our knowledge, it is
the first approach explicitly designed for large-factor EM
super-resolution in real-world settings. ZoomSEM employs
a recurrent transformer architecture incorporating an affine-
deformable multi-scale alignment (ADMA) module. This
design leverages the inherent inter-section correlations in
volumetric EM data: the ADMA module aligns axial features
to integrate information from adjacent EM sections, and the
recurrent transformer aggregates these aligned features across
depth to model long-range dependencies. This yields more
faithful reconstructions of the current section, especially im-
proving the continuity and fine detail of elongated neurites
and synapses that may span many sections.
A key factor in ZoomSEM’s superiority is the real-world
dataset we constructed. The resulting resource, RealSEM-
Pair, consists of precisely registered low- and high-resolution
SEM image pairs acquired from brain tissue, and faithfully
captures authentic noise profiles, structural variations, and
imaging artifacts typical of practical SEM acquisitions. This

to learn robust and generalizable representations. Beyond
supporting this study, RealSEM-Pair provides a valuable

Our experiments demonstrate that ZoomSEM faithfully
recovers ultrastructural features from low-resolution scans.
On large-volume brain tissue, the network accurately recon-
structs delicate structures such as dendritic spines, synapses,
synaptic vesicles, mitochondria and other subcellular or-
ganelles (verified against high-resolution ground truth), even
for 16× super-resolution. The network also exhibits strong
cross-sample and cross-species generalization, with minimal
performance degradation in downstream segmentation tasks
compared to direct high-resolution imaging. In a breast-
cancer organoid system, microvilli and other subcellular
structures are accurately restored with minimal network re-
finement. Importantly, this translates into drastic speedup of
high-resolution data acquisition: for example, imaging a vol-
ume at 16×16 coarser sampling and then applying ZoomSEM
yields comparable quality to standard-resolution imaging,
but with roughly 256× less acquisition time (neglecting over-
head). In practical terms, ZoomSEM can reduce imaging
duration by nearly 30-fold, shortening a multi-month imaging
campaign to days. As a result, our method can significantly
enhance the capability of conventional single-beam SEM
systems, typically costing hundreds of thousand US dollars,
enabling their imaging speeds comparable to state-of-the-art
multibeam SEMs priced at several million dollars. This not
only accelerates data acquisition but also greatly lowers finan-
cial barriers, democratizing high-throughput, large-volume
electron microscopy for a far broader community across con-
nectomics and the life sciences. By lifting the resolution
bottleneck, our method enables high-throughput connectome
mapping and other large-volume EM applications that were
previously impractical.
Results
Workflow of using ZoomSEM for SEM super-
resolution
We aim to develop a method that directly predicts high-
resolution EM images from low-resolution inputs using a
trained network, thereby reducing both imaging cost and
acquisition time (Fig. 1a). This requires both representative
training data and an architecture capable of exploiting the 3D
spatial structure of serial EM volumes. Existing datasets are
limited to small upscaling factors or rely on synthetic degra-
dation that poorly reflects real EM acquisition. We therefore
constructed RealSEM-Pair, a 3D serial SEM dataset from
mouse entorhinal cortex containing pixel-level aligned vol-
ume pairs at four resolutions (4 nm, 16 nm, 32 nm, and 64 nm),
registered via a stepwise strategy. Even across a 16-fold reso-
lution gap, our stepwise registration strategy achieves precise
pixel-level alignment. Compared to prior datasets, RealSEM-
Pair provides up to 16-fold resolution spans from exclusively
real acquisitions, with full axial registration and substantially
larger scale. Trained on this dataset, ZoomSEM employs a
recurrent transformer architecture with bidirectional propaga-
tion across serial sections, and introduces the ADMA module
to handle the structural scale variances characteristic of axial
adjacent EM sections (Fig. 1b).
As the input resolution decreases, the acceleration gains
increase markedly: 16 nm imaging combined with ZoomSEM
achieves a 10.0× speedup, 32 nm imaging combined with
ZoomSEM achieves a 21.0× speedup, and 64 nm imaging
combined with ZoomSEM achieves a 29.2× speedup (Fig. 1c).
These speed improvements account for both acquisition time
and model inference time, measured using 8 A100 GPUs
to ensure fair and reasonable computational resources. Im-
portantly, imaging speed remains the primary bottleneck in
large-scale SEM workflows. A substantial portion of the total
imaging time is resolution-independent, including delays
from stage movement, coil and field switching, scintillator
and photodetector signal conversion, and data buffering or
software processing.
ZoomSEM excels in SEM super-resolution
across various upscaling factors
We comprehensively evaluate ZoomSEM on three super-
resolution tasks: from 16 nm to 4 nm (4×), from 32 nm to
4 nm (8×), and from 64 nm to 4 nm (16×). Three strong
baselines are included for comparison: CFAT (single image
super-resolution method) [23], IART (video super-resolution
method) [22], and EMDiffuse (EM super-resolution method)
[14]. We use PSNR, SSIM [24], and FSIM [25] for quantita-
tive assessments. LPIPS [26] is excluded due to its emphasis
on perceptual texture similarity over structural fidelity, which
misaligns with the objective of accurately replicating true
high-resolution SEM images of cellular structures. The test
set comprises four volumes, each containing 177 sections at
1536×1536 pixels, totalling 708 high-resolution images. As
shown in Fig. 2a, d, and g, ZoomSEM consistently outper-
forms all baselines in all fidelity metrics.
We further assess qualitative outcomes by comparing input
and output images (Fig. 2b, e, and h). For super-resolution
from 16 nm to 4 nm (4×), ZoomSEM closely matches the
high-resolution ground truth (GT) and is the only method that
accurately reconstructs vesicle locations (Fig. 2b). For super-
resolution from 32 nm to 4 nm (8×), ZoomSEM maintains
high fidelity and resolves fine structures other methods fail to
capture (Fig. 2e). At the extreme 16× magnification (from
64 nm to 4 nm), ZoomSEM recovers most cellular features,
despite minor errors, whereas other approaches largely fail
(Fig. 2h). Although EMDiffuse produces outputs stylistically
closer to the GT, it introduces numerous incorrect structures.
In contrast, ZoomSEM accurately reconstructs cellular ultra-
structure, rendering it more suitable for real-world, large-scale
EM super-resolution tasks. We also visualize the pixel-wise
intensity error in Fig. 2c, f, i, showing that ZoomSEM con-
sistently produces pixel values closer to the GT than existing
methods across all three super-resolution settings.

Advancing SEM super-resolution with real-
world training dataset and superior network
architecture
To validate the necessity of real-world training data, we
compared models trained on synthetic degradation pipelines—
bicubic downsampling (BD) and the high-order degradation
pipeline (HOD) [27]—against those trained directly on real
image pairs.
Models trained on synthetic data perform
notably worse under real super-resolution scenarios (Fig. 3a-
c), confirming that real paired data are essential for effective
EM super-resolution.
To demonstrate that the proposed ZoomSEM network
itself offers inherent advantages, we trained all baseline
methods on the RealSEM-Pair dataset using PSNR, SSIM
[24], and FSIM [25] as quantitative metrics, and compared
results with CFAT [23], IART [22], and EMDiffuse [14]. As
illustrated in Fig. 3d-f, ZoomSEM markedly outperforms
these baselines in terms of numerical fidelity. We further
perform qualitative comparisons of input and output images
(Fig. 3g-h). For the 32 nm to 4 nm (8×) task, CFAT+ and
IART+ (cf. Fig. 2e and Fig. 3g) visibly improve upon CFAT
and IART, owing to training on our RealSEM-Pair dataset;
nevertheless, ZoomSEM demonstrates superior structural
fidelity. In the more challenging 64 nm to 4 nm (16×) task,
although most methods fail to reconstruct the underlying
architecture, ZoomSEM successfully recovers the global
cellular morphology (Fig. 3h). Notably, while IART also
employs a recurrent transformer architecture, its alignment
module does not fully leverage the spatial correlations in EM
images. By contrast, our ADMA module incorporates both
affine transformations and sub-pixel-level alignment across
EM sections, leading to results that significantly surpass those
of IART+. EMDiffuse is already trained on RealSEM-Pair;
consequently, no additional dataset refinements are applied
in this comparison.
Accurate downstream segmentation enabled by
ZoomSEM
To validate the accuracy of ZoomSEM reconstructions in
downstream segmentation, we utilized both the Segment Any-
thing Model (SAM) [28], a large deep-learning-based segmen-
tation model, and the conventional Otsu thresholding method
[29], to automatically segment the EM super-resolution out-
puts of all methods. Specifically, we employed VOI [30] to
assess SAM-based segmentation results and defined the pixel-
level accuracy (ACC) metric to measure consistency with a
binarized GT for Otsu-based segmentation. All segmentation
procedures were fully automated with no manual refinements.
Since the segmentation labels were pseudo-labels generated
automatically from the GT, a small degree of error and in-
consistencies in mask selection might arise. Nonetheless, the
segmentation metrics remained reliable for evaluating overall
consistency with the GT. Two key conclusions emerge from
these experiments (Fig. 4). First, ZoomSEM demonstrates
significantly higher semantic consistency with the GT across

Robust cross-sample and cross-species general-
ization of ZoomSEM
We then investigate the cross-sample and cross-species gener-
alization capabilities of ZoomSEM. To evaluate cross-sample
generalization under large-scale dataset, we used a 32 nm
low-resolution SEM dataset acquired from the mouse stria-
tum. We selected a central region of interest with a field of
view of 5120×5120 pixels at 32 nm resolution and acquired
900 serial sections. Two sections with poor imaging quality
were excluded. After axial registration, patch extraction, and
brightness normalization, inference was performed on the
remaining 898 slices using a ZoomSEM network trained
on the RealSEM-Pair dataset (from mouse entorhinal cor-
tex), without any fine-tuning. The resulting super-resolved
volume totaled approximately 795GB. As shown in Fig. 5a,
ZoomSEM significantly enhances the resolution of the low-
resolution input volume. It reveals clear cell boundaries and
intracellular organelle structures that are not visible in the
original input. Moreover, Otsu-based binarization highlights
structural features that are absent in the low-resolution data
but successfully reconstructed by ZoomSEM (Fig. 5b).
We also validated the cross-species generalization capa-
bility of ZoomSEM, expanding its utility from connectome
mapping to broader life science applications. We constructed
a small-scale human breast cell acini dataset. The acini
samples were derived from Jorgens et al. [31], and we re-
imaged them under the same pipeline described above. This
dataset comprises 200 serial EM sections with pixel-level
alignment across multiple resolutions. We used the first
100 sections to fine-tune ZoomSEM, reserving the remain-
ing 100 for testing; the fine-tuned network is referred to
as ZoomSEM-FT. We then compared ZoomSEM-FT with
ZoomSEM, CFAT, and IART, each trained from scratch on
100 acini images, by evaluating their performance on the
validation set from two perspectives: visual quality and con-
sistency with the GT segmentation. On the acini dataset,
ZoomSEM already outperforms the two baseline methods in
quantitative metrics. Moreover, ZoomSEM-FT significantly
surpasses ZoomSEM, especially in SSIM and VOI (Fig. 5c-d),
suggesting that the fine-tuned network achieves substantially
better structural similarity. Qualitative comparisons further
corroborate these findings (Fig. 5e-f). Notably, at 64 nm to
4 nm (16×) upscaling, other methods (including ZoomSEM)
fail to reconstruct the more homogeneous texture of acini,
whereas ZoomSEM-FT—benefiting from the data priors ob-
tained from the RealSEM-Pair dataset—achieves marked
improvements in fidelity and segmentation consistency with
the GT.
These results highlight two key outcomes. First, Zoom-
SEM consistently outperforms all baselines, confirming that
its performance advantages on the mouse entorhinal cortex
data generalize to the acini dataset. Second, the fine-tuned
variant ZoomSEM-FT further improves reconstruction qual-
ity, demonstrating the value of RealSEM-Pair as an effective
training prior. In effect, ZoomSEM trained on the RealSEM-
Pair dataset serves as a transferable foundation model, capable
of generalizing to new imaging contexts. Notably, it achieves
strong performance in cross-sample settings without any ad-
ditional training data, and can be adapted to cross-species
datasets with only a modest amount of registered image pairs
(just 100 in this case).
Ultrastructure recovery with ZoomSEM
We assess the practical implications of ZoomSEM by ana-
lyzing its output image quality on key subcellular structures
(including synapses, synaptic vesicles, spine necks, mitochon-
dria and microvilli), its impact on downstream 3D neuron
reconstruction accuracy, and the acceleration of imaging
throughput achieved by leveraging ZoomSEM.
Applying super-resolution techniques to visualize synaptic
structures with greater clarity is crucial for uncovering the
molecular mechanisms underlying neuronal communication,
synaptic plasticity, and pathology [32, 33]. Across 16 nm
to 4 nm, 32 nm to 4 nm, and 64 nm to 4 nm super-resolution
tasks, ZoomSEM enhances synaptic clefts from being blurred
or imperceptible to clearly distinguishable structures, with
fine details at 16 nm, recoverable synapses at 32 nm, and even
recognizable cellular contours at 64 nm (Fig. 6a).
Applying super-resolution to accurately visualize synaptic
vesicles is paramount for elucidating the molecular mech-
anisms of neurotransmitter release, vesicle recycling, and
activity-dependent membrane trafficking at synapses [34, 35].
With ZoomSEM super-resolution, synaptic vesicles can be
visualized distinctly and correctly (Fig. 6b), enabling direct
inspection of vesicle pools within a single axon bouton.
Dendritic spines—key postsynaptic sites for excitatory
signaling—also benefit markedly from ZoomSEM. Resolv-
ing their morphology is essential for understanding synaptic
plasticity and biochemical compartmentalization [36]. No-
tably, the ultra-thin spine neck, which is usually the most
difficult substructure to discern, is faithfully recovered under
all three super-resolution settings (Fig. 6c).
Mitochondria are another key structure; their cristae or-
ganize the oxidative-phosphorylation machinery to sustain
adenosine triphosphate production [37, 38], and ZoomSEM
faithfully recovers cristae from 32 nm to 4 nm super-resolution
(Fig. 6d).
Applying super-resolution to precisely visualize microvillar
architecture is essential for elucidating their roles in epithelial
absorption and mucosal defence, as microvilli are actin-
supported apical-membrane protrusions that assemble into
an ordered brush border, thereby expanding surface area and
enriching transporters and enzymes [39–41]. ZoomSEM-FT
restores microvillar structures that are indiscernible at low-
resolution imaging (Fig. 6e). Specifically, densely packed
microvillar regions remain unresolvable at 32 nm imaging but
are successfully reconstructed by ZoomSEM-FT (green arrow,
Fig. 6e). Larger microvillar structures (∼200 nm diameter)
are indistinguishable at 64 nm imaging but are accurately
reconstructed by ZoomSEM-FT (red arrow, Fig. 6e).
Finally, we selected the 32 nm to 4 nm super-resolution
model as a compromise between imaging throughput and
reconstruction fidelity. Using this model, we manually traced
and reconstructed an axon bouton-dendritic spine pair, in-
cluding short segments of the axon terminal and dendrite. As
shown in Fig. 6f, even the thinnest spine neck is faithfully
recovered, demonstrating the feasibility of our approach for

Discussion
Serial electron microscopy serves as the cornerstone for
mapping large-scale neural circuits at nanometer resolution
but is hindered by severe throughput limitations. Standard
single-beam scanning electron microscopes require months
or even years to image brain volumes at scales of hundreds
of micrometers. Advanced multi-beam SEM systems can
image substantially faster but remain largely inaccessible
due to high financial barriers. This limitation significantly
constrains large-scale connectomic studies, making high-
resolution serial EM challenging to use for many research
groups.
In this paper, we introduce ZoomSEM, a deep learning-
based method that restores high-resolution SEM images from
rapidly acquired low-resolution scans, enabling substantial
acceleration without sacrificing structural fidelity. Unlike
conventional super-resolution methods trained on synthetic
datasets, ZoomSEM leverages a unique, real-world dataset,
RealSEM-Pair, comprising precisely registered low- and high-
resolution EM image pairs. The use of real-world paired
data is critical, as low-resolution EM images capture authen-
tic degradation characteristics, including noise, aberrations,
and staining variations, which cannot be faithfully repli-
cated by synthetic data. By combining real-world training
data with an advanced transformer-based architecture, Zoom-
SEM effectively reconstructs ultrastructural features from
initial resolutions up to 16× coarser, translating into data
acquisition speedup of approximately 10 to nearly 30 times.
This dramatically improves the utility of standard single-
beam SEMs, enabling their imaging performance to approach
9


costly multi-beam instruments and democratizing access to
high-throughput volumetric EM. To our knowledge, this is the
first approach tailored for large-factor EM super-resolution in
real-world settings.
ZoomSEM reliably preserves fine biological details, in-
cluding synapses, synaptic vesicles, spine necks, mitochon-
dria, microvilli, and organelle morphologies across different
species and tissue types, demonstrating robust cross-domain
generalization, underscoring its potential as a broadly applica-
ble tool in neurobiology and cell biology. Our RealSEM-Pair
dataset itself represents a valuable resource for the EM com-
munity, enabling rigorous benchmarking and training of
future computational methods on authentic imaging data.
The accelerated imaging provided by ZoomSEM sub-
stantially impacts downstream analyses, notably automated
segmentation and connectome reconstruction. By produc-
ing high-fidelity reconstructions from low-resolution input,
ZoomSEM consistently achieves higher consistency with
ground truth (GT) segmentation than baseline methods, par-
ticularly at larger upscaling factors. Even in challenging
cases where other approaches fail to recover recognizable
cellular structures, ZoomSEM preserves key ultrastructural
features with sufficient accuracy to support reliable instance
interpretation. This advancement enables researchers to un-
dertake comprehensive brain-wide mapping or investigate
ultrastructural pathology at scales previously impractical with
existing SEM hardware.
Despite its strengths, ZoomSEM has boundaries that merit
consideration. The method is currently demonstrated on
SEM data. Extending it to other EM modalities might require
additional training data and potential architectural adjust-
ments. Image artifacts, such as section defects, charging, or
contamination, etc., are not explicitly handled. Moreover,
although a model trained on mouse entorhinal cortex gen-
eralizes reasonably well to mouse striatum, its performance
remains inferior to a counterpart fine-tuned directly on striatal
data—reflecting domain gaps introduced by sample prepara-
tion, imaging hardware, and brain-region-specific contrast.
To deploy ZoomSEM on other specimens or microscopes, one
can follow our training pipeline to acquire a modest number
of paired images and fine-tune the released model, thereby
improving accuracy on the target domain. Generalizing to
extremely low signal conditions (e.g. ultra-fast scans with
very low dose) may also demand further model robustness.
Future efforts will extend ZoomSEM’s capabilities to more
diverse EM modalities, imaging conditions and sample types.
Incorporating artifact detection or robust loss functions could
further improve reliability. Additionally, exploring unsu-
pervised or semi-supervised learning approaches may help
reduce dependence on paired ground truth data. Finally, inte-
grating ZoomSEM with downstream segmentation tools into
fully automated pipelines could ultimately establish end-to-
end workflows for rapid, accurate and scalable connectomic
and cellular imaging studies.
In summary, ZoomSEM significantly redefines the speed
and scale at which volumetric EM imaging can be conducted.
By bridging the gap between high-resolution quality and rapid
imaging, ZoomSEM stands to transform the accessibility and
feasibility of ambitious connectomics and ultrastructural
biology studies.
