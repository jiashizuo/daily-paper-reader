# A Mathematical Framework for EEG Functional Connectivity: Taxonomy, Measure Relationships, Identifiability, and Analytical Bounds 

Jerrin Thomas Panachakel 

School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering Technological University Dublin, Dublin, Ireland 

jerrin.panachakel@tudublin.ie 

Abstract 

Electroencephalographic (EEG) functional connectivity is represented by a large family of statistics that quantify linear dependence, spectral coupling, phase synchronization, amplitude-envelope association, information transfer, directed influence, cross-frequency interaction, or graph topology. The proliferation of these measures creates an under-formalized inferential question: given a reported value of one connectivity measure, what can be deduced about another? This paper develops a mathematical framework in which an EEG connectivity esti-mator is treated as a mapping from an explicitly defined admissible state space to an observable space. For a source connectivity functional Ci and a target functional Cj , the set of target val-ues compatible with an observed source value is defined as a cross-connectivity attainable set. The framework yields precise criteria for exact determination, conditional determination after state augmentation, analytical bounded inference, approximate inference, and non-identifiability. The formulation is extended to multichannel connectivity matrices, graph measures, and dy-namic connectivity trajectories. Particular attention is given to the fact that admissibility is experiment-dependent: filtering, referencing, volume conduction, source leakage, inverse model-ing, epoch duration, stationarity assumptions, frequency selection, and estimator regularization alter the state-to-observable map. Analytical case studies show that covariance determines correlation after marginal scale information is supplied, Gaussian mutual information is ex-actly determined by correlation only within the jointly Gaussian family, constant-amplitude narrow-band coherence reduces to PLV, while PLI and wPLI are generally non-identifiable from one another without further assumptions. The paper synthesizes foundational and recent EEG/MEG connectivity literature and converts recurring methodological cautions into explicit mathematical objects and propositions. The resulting framework is not a ranking of connectivity estimators; it is a language for stating, with assumptions made visible, what one connectivity result does and does not identify about another. 

Keywords: Electroencephalography, EEG, functional connectivity, coherency, phase synchroniza-tion, phase-lag index, weighted phase-lag index, mutual information, Granger causality, transfer entropy, cross-frequency coupling, graph theory, source leakage, identifiability, attainable sets, an-alytical bounds. 

# 1 Introduction 

Functional connectivity is commonly used to quantify statistical dependence among distributed neural signals, whereas effective connectivity additionally addresses directed interactions or model-1arXiv:2610.24732v1 [eess.SP] 17 Oct 2026 based influence [1, 2]. In electroencephalography (EEG), connectivity analysis is particularly at-tractive because millisecond-scale temporal resolution permits study of interactions across a wide range of temporal and oscillatory scales [3, 4]. The resulting literature is large and heterogeneous, spanning classical correlation and coherence, phase-based synchronization, envelope-based cou-pling, information-theoretic dependence, directed time-series methods, cross-frequency coupling, source-space connectivity, and graph-theoretic analysis [5, 6, 7]. The methodological diversity is scientifically useful but mathematically consequential. A con-nectivity statistic is a compression of an underlying signal configuration. Correlation preserves a normalized second-order relationship but discards absolute scale. Coherence preserves a frequency-specific linear dependence but not the full joint probability law. PLV preserves circular concentra-tion of relative phase, while PLI and wPLI impose different transformations on the same phase-lag information [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Mutual information is sensitive to general statistical depen-dence, but a single correlation coefficient does not in general determine it [16, 17, 18]. Directional measures introduce additional dependence on temporal embedding, model structure, conditioning variables, or spectral decomposition [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Cross-frequency measures require another layer of specification because phase and amplitude estimates depend on band definitions, filtering, waveform shape, and stationarity assumptions [27, 28, 29, 30]. Several influential reviews already provide strong descriptions of individual estimators, families of estimators, and common interpretational pitfalls [5, 31, 6, 7, 32]. Recent systematic reviews and perspectives further document the expanding use of EEG connectivity in disorders and connec-tomics, including epilepsy, depression, dementia, and acute brain injury, as well as the increasing emphasis on dynamic and network-level analyses [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. Yet one mathemat-ical question remains insufficiently formalized across these literatures: what does a value of one connectivity measure identify about another connectivity measure? 

The question is more demanding than asking whether two measures are correlated empirically. A deterministic relationship requires that the target measure be constant over every admissible state that produces the same source value. This is a statement about the fibers of two mappings. It is therefore distinct from an empirical regression, a high Pearson correlation between estimators, a common response to the same experimental manipulation, or agreement observed on a restricted benchmark dataset. The central idea of this paper is to formulate cross-connectivity inference as an attainable-set problem. Let X denote an explicitly specified set of admissible EEG states and let Ci, C j be source and target connectivity functionals. For an observed source value ci, define the source fiber 

Fi(ci) = {x ∈ X : Ci(x) = ci} (1) and the target attainable set 

Aj|i(ci) = {Cj (x) : x ∈ X , C i(x) = ci}. (2) If this set is a singleton, the target is exactly determined. If it is bounded but not a singleton, the source yields an interval or more general bounded set. If it is unbounded, no finite universal target bound follows from the source observation under the selected admissible state space. This framework has several advantages for EEG. Measurement and nuisance operations need not remain hidden in prose: the reference operator, forward mixing, filtering, source reconstruc-tion, analysis window, and estimator parameters may all be represented explicitly in the state-to-observable map. The same mathematical definition can then describe sensor-space and source-space connectivity, static and dynamic connectivity, pairwise and graph-derived measures. The frame-work also clarifies why common statements such as “measure A is equivalent to measure B” are 2meaningful only after the assumptions defining the admissible state space have been stated. Fig-ure 1 gives the corresponding geometric view. The paper makes six contributions. First, it formalizes EEG connectivity measures as func-tionals and the associated equivalence classes they induce on signal space. Second, it introduces the cross-connectivity attainable set and a directional hierarchy of exact, conditional, bounded, approximate, and non-identifiable relationships. Third, it provides analytical results and construc-tive counterexamples for major connectivity families. Fourth, it treats preprocessing, referencing, volume conduction, inverse solutions, and leakage correction as transformations of the admissible state space rather than merely practical details. Fifth, it extends the framework to dynamic con-nectivity, connectivity matrices, and graph metrics. Sixth, it translates the mathematical analysis into a reporting and study-design protocol intended to make cross-measure claims reproducible and falsifiable. The manuscript is structured as a Topical Review. Section II defines terminology, scope, and notation. Section III develops the attainable-set and identifiability framework. Section IV maps the information retained and discarded by major EEG connectivity families. Sections V–VIII derive representative relationships among linear, spectral, phase-based, information-theoretic, directed, and cross-frequency measures. Section IX incorporates measurement physics, referencing, filtering, and source leakage. Sections X and XI extend the framework to dynamic and graph-derived con-nectivity. Section XII addresses finite-sample estimation and uncertainty. Section XIII presents worked analytical examples. Section XIV proposes a unified decision framework, Section XV crit-ically synthesizes the literature, Section XVI provides reporting standards, Section XVII states limitations, Section XVIII develops future directions, and Section XIX concludes the paper. 

# 2 Terminology, Scope, and Mathematical Notation 

2.1 What is being treated as “connectivity”? 

Throughout this paper, functional connectivity is used in its statistical sense: a quantified depen-dence between signals, transformed signal features, reconstructed sources, or latent representations. A nonzero functional-connectivity statistic is not interpreted as proof of an anatomical connection or a causal mechanism. This distinction is especially important for scalp EEG, for which multiple distributed sources contribute to each measured channel [1, 40, 41, 42]. A connectivity object may be pairwise or multivariate, undirected or directed, static or dynamic, sensor-space or source-space, and scalar, matrix-valued, or graph-valued. The mathematical frame-work does not require a particular estimator. Instead, each estimator is represented by a map from an admissible state to an observable. 

2.2 Research questions 

The mathematical review is organized around five questions. RQ1: when does one EEG con-nectivity observable exactly determine another over a declared signal class? RQ2: when exact determination fails, what additional assumptions or measured variables are sufficient to make the target identifiable? RQ3: what analytical bounds can be obtained without replacing a many-to-one mapping by an unjustified point conversion? RQ4: how do acquisition, reference, filtering, source reconstruction, leakage correction, finite sampling, and dynamic-window choices alter the attainable set? RQ5: how should cross-measure claims be reported so that their mathematical strength is reproducible and falsifiable? 3These questions deliberately separate three layers that are often conflated in application papers: the population functional being estimated, the measurement/processing operator that produces the data supplied to that functional, and the estimator used on a finite sample. Two studies can therefore use the same named connectivity measure while operating on different effective functionals because their references, filters, windows, source models, or normalization conventions differ. The principal measure families and their inferential roles are summarized in Table 1. 

2.3 Literature-curation strategy 

The paper is a mathematically driven topical review rather than a conventional clinical system-atic review. Literature was curated around five evidence layers: (i) foundational mathematical definitions; (ii) original estimator papers; (iii) methodological and tutorial reviews; (iv) empirical validation, reliability, artifact, reference, and leakage studies; and (v) recent application-oriented reviews and perspectives. Priority was given to traceable journal articles or established conference papers with stable bibliographic identifiers. The final manuscript deliberately does not treat a large raw reference count as a surrogate for evidence quality. A larger bibliographic corpus is useful for literature discovery, but only sources that substantively support a claim should be cited in the main text. 

2.4 Scope 

The review covers covariance and correlation; partial correlation; coherency, coherence, and par-tial coherence; imaginary coherency and related lagged measures; PLV, PPC, PLI, and wPLI; amplitude-envelope connectivity; mutual information and entropy-based dependence; Granger causal-ity, DTF, PDC, and transfer entropy; phase-amplitude and related cross-frequency coupling; mul-tivariate and higher-order interactions; dynamic connectivity; graph/network measures; and the acquisition, reference, filtering, source-reconstruction, and leakage factors that change the resulting observables. 

2.5 Notation 

Let N denote the number of EEG channels and T the number of samples in an analysis window. The observed data matrix is Y ∈ RN ×T . A latent source process is denoted by S. A broad measurement model is 

Y = P R M (S, θ m) + E, (3) where M is a forward or mixing operator, R is a reference transformation, P collects preprocessing operations, θm denotes acquisition and model parameters, and E contains noise and unmodeled contributions. The operators may be linear or nonlinear, fixed or data-dependent. A connectivity functional is denoted by Ck. It may be scalar-valued, vector-valued, or matrix-valued. Unless stated otherwise, identifiability refers to the population or idealized functional, not to a particular finite-sample estimator. Section XII explicitly restores estimation uncertainty.

3.8 A theorem on factorization through a summary statistic 

The fiber criterion can be expressed in a form familiar from sufficient-statistic theory. Let S : X → S 

be any summary statistic and T : X → T be a target functional. Then T factors through S if and only if there exists g : S → T with T = g ◦ S. In the connectivity setting, the source connectivity value itself is the summary. The criterion therefore does not depend on the semantics of EEG; it follows from a general property of mappings. The connection with statistical sufficiency is informative but should not be overstated. A source connectivity measure may be sufficient for a particular target connectivity functional without being sufficient for the underlying signal distribution, experimental parameter, or causal mechanism. The notion used here is therefore target-specific sufficiency : sufficiency only for the requested cross-measure inference. 

3.9 Directionality of identifiability 

The notation Aj|i is intentionally directional. It is entirely possible that Cj determines Ci while 

Ci does not determine Cj . A simple analogy is a many-to-one map T = x2: knowing T does not recover the sign of x, whereas knowing x trivially determines T . In EEG, a target that retains more phase, amplitude, conditional, or multivariate structure can determine a compressed source statistic even when the reverse mapping is impossible. For random variables X and Y with finite nonzero variances,

pVar( X) Var( Y ) . (20) Hence covariance determines correlation when the two marginal standard deviations are available. Covariance alone does not. For any positive a, b , the transformed variables X′ = aX and Y ′ = bY

Proposition 4 (Conditional exactness of covariance-to-correlation conversion) . On an admissi-ble state space with known standard deviations (σX , σ Y ), correlation is exactly determined from covariance by r = c/ (σX σY ). On a state space in which the variances are free and unbounded, covariance-to-correlation conversion is not injective and correlation-to-covariance is unbounded.

5.6 Partial coherence and conditional spectral dependence 

Partial coherence is the frequency-domain analogue of conditioning in multivariate linear models. For a multivariate spectral density matrix S(f ), conditional spectral quantities can be derived from the inverse spectral matrix. The key identifiability point is immediate: a marginal coherence between X and Y contains no complete information about their conditional coherence after a third or fourth process is controlled. The hidden cross-spectra with the conditioning variables are part of the fiber. This is the spectral counterpart of the partial-correlation example. It also illustrates why multivariate connectivity should not be described merely as “more variables in the same measure.” Conditioning changes the target functional itself.

PLV is a circular concentration measure, not a raw coincidence count. Cross-measure inference must therefore state whether Ci and Cj refer to population functionals, biased sample estimators, or corrected estimators. 

6.7 Zero-lag sensitivity is not the same as mechanistic specificity 

Measures designed to reduce zero-lag contamination should be described precisely. PLI and wPLI similarly target nonzero-lag phase relations [10, 12]. However, a nonzero-lag relation can still arise from common driving, source 16 mixing, filter-induced phase structure, or other indirect mechanisms. The attainable-set framework thus separates robustness to a nuisance from identification of a biological mechanism . These are different mathematical claims.

7.2 Transfer entropy and Granger causality 

Transfer entropy can be written as a conditional mutual information between the future of one process and the past of another given the target’s own past. For suitable Gaussian processes and matched conditioning conventions, transfer entropy and Granger causality are mathematically equivalent up to the choice of logarithmic base and scaling convention [47, 60]. Outside that model class, the equivalence is lost. 

Proposition 8 (Model-conditional equivalence of Gaussian TE and GC) . For jointly Gaussian processes and a matched linear-history conditioning structure, the transfer-entropy functional and Granger-causality functional are deterministic transforms of one another. This does not imply a universal equivalence over arbitrary non-Gaussian processes. 

The distinction mirrors the correlation/MI result: a relationship can be exact in a restricted state space and non-identifiable in a larger one. Consequently, the state-space restrictions should be reported whenever one connectivity measure is converted into another. 

7.3 DTF and PDC 

Directed transfer function (DTF) and partial directed coherence (PDC) are frequency-domain rep-resentations derived from multivariate autoregressive structure. DTF is naturally associated with normalized transfer magnitudes, whereas PDC normalizes differently and is related to the autore-gressive coefficients themselves [21, 22, 23]. Connectivity analyses often involve many channel pairs, frequencies, conditions, or graph nodes. False discovery rate control and related multiple-comparison procedures remain essential when con-verting pairwise significance tests into network-level conclusions [97]. The attainable-set framework is complementary: multiplicity controls the probability of false positives, whereas identifiability specifies what can be logically inferred from the value of a statistic. 

12.4 Population identifiability versus estimator identifiability 

Let Ci and Cj be population functionals and bCi, bCj their finite-sample estimators. Population exactness means 

Cj = g(Ci) (55) on the admissible population state space. This does not imply that the finite-sample estimators satisfy bCj = g( bCi) exactly. Bias, variance, regularization, discretization, surrogate choices, and numerical tolerances can all intervene. 26 Conversely, an empirical regression may produce an accurate estimator of Cj from bCi on one data distribution even when population exactness fails. Such a regression is a predictive model, not a proof of identifiability.

12.6 Permutation and surrogate inference 

Connectivity analyses frequently use surrogate or permutation procedures. Such methods are valu-able for testing null hypotheses and controlling false-positive rates [96, 95, 98]. However, a signifi-cance test answers whether an observed statistic is unusual under a null; it does not answer whether a second connectivity functional is uniquely determined by the observed statistic. Identifiability and hypothesis testing should therefore be reported separately. 

12.7 Multiplicity and selection 

Connectivity studies often evaluate many channel pairs, frequencies, conditions, and graph metrics. The number of statistical tests can become very large, motivating error-control procedures such as the false discovery rate [97, 99, 100]. Multiple-testing correction changes the inferential threshold, not the underlying mapping Ci 7 → Cj . A statistically significant source-target association can still be non-identifying, while an identifying mathematical relation may hold regardless of the multiplicity procedure. 

12.8 Reliability is orthogonal to identifiability 

A connectivity measure can be highly reliable yet weakly informative about another measure, or noisy yet theoretically sufficient under a strict model. Test–retest reliability and identifiability therefore describe different properties [93, 42, 94]. Reliability asks whether the same functional is reproducible; identifiability asks whether one functional determines another over the admissible state space. 13.8 Case study: PLI and wPLI 

The constructive example in Section VI proves that the PLI value 0 .50 can coexist with wPLI values near 0 .50 and 0 .94. Hence a reported PLI value should not be converted to a unique wPLI value. 13.9 Case study: static connectivity to a dynamic trajectory 

Suppose only ¯C = 0 .4 is reported for a dynamic edge. One admissible trajectory can be constant at 0 .4. Another can alternate between 0 .1 and 0 .7 for equal durations. Both have identical means, but the second has nonzero variance and repeated transitions. A third trajectory can remain at 0.7 for 40% of the time and at 0 .2 for 60%, again matching the same mean. Without additional temporal constraints, the mean therefore identifies neither variance nor transition pattern. 

13.10 Case study: connectivity matrix to graph modularity 

Let W be a weighted matrix. A scalar modularity score Q defines a high-dimensional level set. Redistributing edge weights within communities can preserve Q while altering individual edge strengths. Conversely, two matrices can have similar average edge weights but markedly different modularity because the placement of those weights changes community structure. Therefore an 29 edge-level measure cannot in general be reconstructed from a global modularity value and vice versa. The inference direction has to be specified before any “network conversion” is discussed.

This is the directed analogue of the marginal-to-partial correlation problem and motivates explicit multivariate modeling [20, 22, 49, 26]. 

# 14 A Unified Decision Framework for Cross-Measure Inference 

A practical cross-measure question can be organized into the following sequence. First, define the source and target functionals exactly. Second, define the admissible state space. Third, identify variables discarded by the source measure but required by the target. Fourth, determine whether the target is constant over each source fiber. If not, impose realistic bounds or supply a minimal augmentation. Finally, distinguish population-level identifiability from finite-sample estimation. # 15 Critical Synthesis of the EEG Connectivity Literature 

15.1 Taxonomy alone is insufficient 

A conventional taxonomy tells the reader that coherence is spectral, PLV is phase-based, MI is information-theoretic, and graph measures are network-level. The present framework asks a stronger question: what information survives each transformation, and can the surviving informa-tion support an inference about a second transformation? This shift changes the unit of comparison from the name of the measure to its equivalence classes on state space.

# 18 Open Mathematical Problems and Future Directions 

Several mathematical extensions follow naturally. First, many attainable-set calculations are high-dimensional constrained optimization problems. Convex relaxations, moment methods, semidefinite programming, and optimal-transport formula-tions may provide scalable bounds when the state constraints admit tractable structure. Second, finite-sample identifiability should be studied jointly with population identifiability. The population attainable set describes logical inference; estimator distributions describe practical uncertainty. Their combination could yield probabilistic attainable sets or confidence envelopes for cross-measure inference. Third, the framework should be extended to multivariate and higher-order interactions. Multi-variate mutual information, conditional dependence, hypergraph representations, and higher-order phase interactions introduce richer fibers than pairwise measures. Fourth, dynamic connectivity can be formalized over path spaces rather than scalar intervals. State-space and optimal-transport methods may be useful for characterizing which temporal fea-tures are implied by one dynamic summary and which are not. Fifth, inverse uncertainty could be integrated directly. Rather than conditioning on a single 35 lead field or source-reconstruction solution, the admissible state space could include an ensem-ble of plausible forward and inverse models. The resulting target attainable set would quantify methodological uncertainty as part of the scientific inference. Sixth, standardized benchmark suites could be constructed with analytically controllable signal families. A benchmark could score connectivity methods by the structure of their attainable sets, estimator robustness, sensitivity to known nuisances, and cross-measure information preservation, rather than by downstream classification performance alone. Seventh, the same framework may be useful beyond EEG. MEG, intracranial electrophysiology, calcium imaging, and multimodal fusion all involve transformations from latent activity to statistical summaries. The mathematical question of what one summary identifies about another is therefore broader than any one modality. 

# 19 Conclusion 

EEG functional connectivity is not one observable but a family of statistical functionals that com-press different aspects of neural signals. Consequently, the value of one connectivity measure gener-ally does not determine another. This paper formalized that issue by defining the cross-connectivity attainable set: the set of all target values compatible with a reported source value over an explicitly stated admissible EEG state space. The framework distinguishes exact determination, conditional determination, analytical bounded inference, approximate identification, and non-identifiability. These distinctions were illustrated an-alytically for covariance and correlation, Gaussian mutual information and correlation, coherence and PLV, PLI and wPLI, Granger causality and transfer entropy, dynamic connectivity summaries, and graph metrics. The analysis also demonstrates why reference choice, filtering, volume conduc-tion, source leakage, inverse modeling, and thresholding belong inside the mathematical formulation rather than being treated solely as implementation details. The principal practical implication is straightforward. A cross-measure statement should al-ways answer three questions: what state information was retained by the source measure; what information was discarded; and which assumptions, bounds, or additional measurements make the target recoverable. When an exact factorization exists, it should be stated as such. When only a conditional relationship exists, the conditions should be written explicitly. When the attainable set is bounded but multi-valued, analytical bounds are more defensible than scalar conversion. When no useful bound exists, non-identifiability is the scientifically correct conclusion. The framework therefore does not seek to identify a universally superior connectivity estimator. Instead, it provides a common mathematical language for comparing estimators by the partitions they induce on signal space, the information they preserve, and the strength of the cross-measure inferences they support. This language becomes increasingly relevant as EEG research moves toward dynamic, multivariate, source-resolved, and graph-based connectomics.
