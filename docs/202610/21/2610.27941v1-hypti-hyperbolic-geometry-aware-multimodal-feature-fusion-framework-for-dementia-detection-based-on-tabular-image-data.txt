# HypTI: Hyperbolic Geometry-Aware Multimodal Feature Fusion Framework for Dementia Detection Based on Tabular-Image Data 

Jianfei Xu, Huizhi Liang, Judith Harrison, John-Paul Taylor, Jaume Bacardit, and Varun Ojha 

Newcastle University, Newcastle upon Tyne NE1 7RU, UK 

{j.xu65, huizhi.liang, judith.harrison, john-paul.taylor, jaume.bacardit, varun.ojha}@newcastle.ac.uk 

Abstract. AI-assisted dementia detection usually requires multimodal fusion of diverse data, such as clinical tabular assessments and neu-roimaging data. However, most existing approaches perform feature fu-sion in Euclidean space. This overlooks the geometric structure of repre-sentation, including hierarchical organization in clinical assessments and multi-scale anatomical patterns in neuroimaging data. Recent studies have proved hyperbolic space provides a natural geometry for modelling hierarchical and multi-scale structures due to its exponentially expanding space. Motivated by these, we propose HypTI, a hyperbolic geometry-aware multimodal fusion framework for dementia detection based on clinical tabular assessments and MRI scans. HypTI extends hyperbolic representation learning to an underexplored tabular-MRI setting, jointly modelling clinical tabular, MRI scans, and cross-modal interactions in hyperbolic space. Specifically, clinical tabular variables are represented through learnable Lorentz prototypes to capture hierarchical relation-ships among assessments, while MRI scans are encoded using ROI-guided multi-scale representation learning to capture anatomical patterns across brain regions and spatial scales in Lorentz space. The multimodal rep-resentations are integrated through an adaptive fusion module gener-ates subject-specific scale and shift coefficients to dynamically modulate and integrate the multimodal embeddings, enabling effective patient-level tabular-image representation learning for dementia detection. Experi-ments on OASIS-3 dataset show that the proposed framework achieves strong performance compared with representative approaches. 

Keywords: Dementia · Tabular Data · MRI · Multimodal Fusion 

# 1 Introduction 

Dementia syndrome detection requires integrating heterogeneous complementary evidence from clinical assessments and neuroimaging. Clinical tabular variables describe risk factors, cognitive impairments, functional decline, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and clinical staging, whereas T1-weighted MRI provides structural arXiv:2610.27941v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 2 Xu et al. 

evidence of neurodegeneration and regional brain atrophy [1]. Therefore, de-mentia classification is not only an image recognition problem, but also requires multimodal integration of clinical assessments and neuroanatomical evidence [2]. However, learning effective representations from clinical tabular data and MRI remains challenging. The two modalities exhibit substantial differences in their data structure and semantic granularity [3]. Clinical tabular variables fol-low a hierarchical organization consistent with dementia assessment workflows. Broad clinical domains are divided into subdomains and quantified through stan-dardized assessments in a NACC Uniform Data Set (UDS)-style structure, collec-tively characterizing disease severity [4]. Structural MRI is a high-dimensional 3D signal whose disease-related patterns appear across multiple anatomical scales, ranging from local patch-level tissue changes to region-level atrophy and broader brain-wide structural alterations [5]. In addition, the brain is organized hier-archically across multiple spatial scales [6]. Voxel-level measurements can be aggregated into anatomical regions, which are in turn embedded within larger structural and functional systems, while neurodegenerative changes often follow non-random spatial patterns across the hierarchy [7]. Such hierarchical structures are not naturally aligned with flat Euclidean geometry, whose representational capacity grows only polynomially with distance from the origin [8]. Most exist-ing multimodal dementia models perform feature fusion in Euclidean space us-ing concatenation-, convolution-, kernel-, or attention-based mechanisms [9–11]. However, such approaches largely overlook the hierarchical and multi-scale ge-ometric structure underlying clinical tabular and neuroimaging representations. Hyperbolic geometry provides a natural alternative for modelling hierarchical and branching structures, as it can compactly organize representations with different levels of specificity within a shared embedding space [8, 12]. Recent advances have extended hyperbolic representation learning to neural networks, graph learning, and vision models [13–15]. However, hyperbolic methods have fo-cused on brain connectivity graphs [9,16], while joint hyperbolic fusion of clinical tabular data and structural MRI remains largely unexplored. Motivated by these evidence, we propose a hyperbolic geometry-aware multi-modal feature fusion framework for dementia detection based on clinical tabular assessments and MRI scans. Our model treats dementia detection as geometry-aware multimodal disease modelling, where clinical tabular features and multi-scale MRI structural patterns are jointly embedded and fused in Lorentz space. In addition, geometric analyses of the learned Lorentz representations reveal en-hanced hierarchical organization, and class separation, providing further insight into the benefits of hyperbolic representation learning. 

Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We introduce HypTI, a hyperbolic geometry-aware multi-modal feature fuse framework for medical tabular-image representation learning. (2) We extend medical hyperbolic repre-sentation learning to patient-level tabular-MRI modelling, addressing an insuffi-ciently explored multi-modal clinical setting in hyperbolic space. (3) We propose a region-aware multi-scale hyperbolic MRI network (HypMRI-Net) for captur-ing hierarchical anatomical patterns of brain MRI scans in Lorentz space. (4) HypTI 3

We design a hyperbolic geometry-aware fusion module that refines multi-scale MRI embedding through hyperbolic self-attention and generates subject-specific scaling and shifting coefficients to modulate MRI and tabular representations. 

# 2 Related Work 

Multimodal learning for dementia. Dementia detection is supported by multimodal information, since neurodegeneration is reflected through comple-mentary structural, cognitive, and clinical signals. While single-modality ap-proaches based on MRI, PET, or cognitive assessments have achieved promising results, they provide only a partial view of the disease [2, 17, 18]. Recent surveys have reviewed multimodal learning for dementia, indicating that that combin-ing neuroimaging, cognitive assessments, and clinical measurements can improve dementia detection [19, 20]. However, multimodal dementia studies have shown that conventional feature concatenation and fixed latent aggregation often fail to fully exploit complementary information across modalities [21]. These limitations motivate more expressive representation learning and feature fusion frameworks for multimodal data, capable of jointly modelling cross-modal dependencies and disease-related structural patterns. 

Fusion of tabular and imaging Data. Existing tabular-imaging fusion meth-ods are categorized into early, late, and intermediate fusion. Early fusion directly concatenates clinical variables with image-derived features, but it often ignores modality-specific structure and allows high-dimensional imaging embeddings to dominate clinical signals [22]. Late fusion trains modality-specific predictors and combines their outputs at the decision level, which can improve robust-ness, but limits cross-modal interaction to the prediction stage. Intermediate fusion combines modality-specific representations in a latent space, but many approaches still rely on concatenation, gating, or coarse conditioning, limiting fine-grained cross-modal interactions [23]. Recent adaptive fusion strategies, in-spired by feature-wise affine modulation [24], use learned scaling and shifting coefficients to recalibrate multimodal representations and dynamically balance their modality contributions [1]. However, these approaches are formulated in Euclidean space and struggle to naturally model the hierarchical structures un-derlying clinical tabular and multi-scale MRI representations. 

Geometry-aware representation learning. Many conventional representa-tion learning models operate in Euclidean space [9–11], whose polynomial volume growth limits low-dimensional representations of exponentially branching hier-archies [8]. In contrast, hyperbolic space exhibits exponential volume growth with increasing radius, closely matching the geometry of hierarchies and en-abling compact representations of complex branching structures [8, 13]. Recent dementia studies provide evidence that hyperbolic geometry is useful for rep-resenting neuroimaging with hierarchical structure [9, 16]. Longhena et al. [16] demonstrated that hyperbolic embeddings of DWI and fMRI brain networks can better identify regions disrupted by neurodegeneration than conventional graph metrics. Similarly, hyperbolic graph learning has been explored for neurocog-4 Xu et al. 

nitive decline prediction, where DTI and fMRI brain networks are embedded in hyperbolic space to preserve hierarchical connectivity structures [9]. Despite these advances, existing hyperbolic studies have focused on single image modal-ity, while the joint hyperbolic representation learning of patient-level tabular and image representations remains underexplored. It remains unclear how clini-cal variables and imaging features can be jointly encoded and fused in hyperbolic space for dementia detection. This motivates a hyperbolic fusion framework that captures both fine-grained tabular–MRI interactions and the hierarchical orga-nization of the resulting multimodal representations. Overall, Euclidean multimodal models are limited in representing hierarchical and multi-scale structure. Hyperbolic space provides a better geometric match via exponential volume growth and compact low-distortion embeddings of tree-like data [8, 25], and hyperbolic attention enables MRI and tabular embeddings to be fused into a flat Euclidean latent space [14,26]. Existing hyperbolic medical models mainly focus on single-modality representations [9,16], leaving hyperbolic tabular-MRI fusion underexplored. 

# 3 HypTI: Hyperbolic Tabular-Image Framework

The proposed framework (see Figure 1) includes three key modules: First, the Multi-Scale Hyperbolic MRI Network (HypMRI-Net) constructs ROI-guided multi-scale MRI inputs, which are encoded by a 3D ResNet and enhanced via hyperbolic self-attention to model cross-scale anatomical dependencies. Second, the Hyperbolic Tabular Prototype Network (HypTab-Net) represents each tab-ular variable using a set of learnable Lorentz prototypes and employs a gating mechanism to adaptively aggregate them to form tabular embeddings. Third, an adaptive fusion module generates subject-specific scale and shift coefficients to dynamically integrate multimodal embeddings. 3.2 Multi-Scale Hyperbolic MRI Network (HypMRI-Net) 

HypMRI-Net module (see Figure 1) is designed to extract brain anatomical fea-tures from MRI patches at multi-scales and capture the relationships among them. Given an input MRI volume X, patches are sampled from multiple reso-lution levels, while an ROI guidance map increases the probability of selecting patches from brain regions highly associated with dementia. Each multi-scale patch is then individually encoded by a shared 3D ResNet backbone to obtain its corresponding patch-level embedding. These patch embeddings are mapped into a common feature space through scale-specific projections and augmented with learnable scale embeddings to indicate their scale type. The scale-aware patch embeddings are subsequently mapped onto the Lorentz manifold to introduce a hierarchical organization of multi-scale MRI embeddings. Finally, hyperbolic self-attention performs matching and aggregation among the patch embeddings, capturing relationships across different brain regions and spatial scales. 

ROI-guided multi-scale patch extraction. For each scale s ∈ S = {16 , 32 , 64 },we extract P local volumetric patches Xs,p of size s × s × s, p = 1 , . . . , P , from MRI volume X, yielding Nm = |S| P = 3 P patches in total. During training, patch centres are sampled according to an ROI weight map that assigns higher probabilities to dementia-related regions, including the hippocampus, entorhi-nal cortex, amygdala, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, temporal gyri, and parahippocampal gyrus 1. This sampling strategy exposes the network to local anatomical patterns at complementary spatial resolutions. 

Shared 3D ResNet patch encoder. All patches are encoded by a shared 3D ResNet with three residual blocks. Sharing the backbone across scales encourages a common anatomical feature space while avoiding separate encoders for different patch sizes. 3.4 Geometry-Aware Cross-Modal Fusion 

The fusion module (see Figure 1) integrates the refined MRI patch embed-dings M ∗ with the feature-wise tabular sequence T . We summarize the MRI sequence using average pooling (AvgPool) and max pooling (MaxPool), where AvgPool captures the overall multi-scale brain structure and MaxPool highlights the most discriminative ROI patterns across scales. The tabular sequence is flat-tened(Flatten) to preserve feature-specific clinical information:

The tabular context then modulates the MRI representa-tion through a feature-wise affine transformation ezm = α ⊙ zm + β, z = [ ezm; zt],where ⊙ denotes the Hadamard product, zm ∈ Rdm is the original MRI rep-resentation, zt ∈ Rdt is the encoded tabular context, and ezm is the tabular-conditioned MRI representation. to recalibrate MRI-derived features on a subject-specific basis instead of treat-ing the two modalities as equally informative under direct concatenation. The fused representation z is passed to an MLP classification head to pro-duce the multimodal prediction. During training, a tabular-only auxiliary clas-sifier is also applied to the tabular representation zt, producing a tabular-only prediction. This auxiliary classifier provides direct label supervision to HypTab-Net, as optimizing only the multimodal objective may cause the model to rely predominantly on MRI features and underutilize the tabular modality. The aux-iliary objective therefore encourages zt to retain discriminative clinical informa-tion and stabilizes the learning of the tabular branch. The overall objective is 

L = Lmulti + λtab Ltab , where Lmulti and Ltab are the cross-entropy losses for the multimodal and auxiliary tabular predictions, respectively, and λtab controls the auxiliary contribution. Lorentz prototypes and other manifold-constrained pa-rameters are optimized using Riemannian Adam, whereas unconstrained neural-network parameters are optimized using AdamW. 

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Dataset and Metrics 

We train our model using the OASIS-3 dataset 2, which is a multimodal neu-roimaging and clinical assessments from individuals spanning normal ageing to dementia. The dataset includes retrospective data from 1,378 participants, in-cluding 755 cognitively normal adults and 622 individuals at different stages of cognitive decline. OASIS-3 includes 2,842 MR sessions (4,116 raw T1-w MRI), and tabular data such as demographic variables, UDS clinical forms, cognitive assessments, and psychometric measurements. The OASIS-3 cohort used in our experiment was constructed by first deriving subject-level CN and dementia la-bels from clinical assessments 3, then intersecting 1,254 labelled subjects with those having usable T1-weighted MRI scans, yielding 540 subjects (see Table 1). Performance was evaluated on each test split using Accuracy (ACC), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and area under the receiver operating character-istic curve (AUC). We primarily report AUC for threshold-independent discrim-ination and MCC for balanced binary classification under class imbalance [28].

4.2 Preprocessing Tabular data. Each subject was represented by 37 clinical features derived from OASIS-3 assessments, including demographic, APOE genotype 4, cognitive scores, and medical histories [1]. Continuous features were Z-score normalized using training-split statistics. Missing values were zero-filled in the normalized space and encoded using a 37-dimensional availability mask, enabling the model to distinguish missing entries and exploit informative missingness patterns [29]. 

MRI data. T1-weighted MRI scans were converted to BIDS format 5 using Clin-ica’s oasis3-to-bids converter 6 and preprocessed with the t1-linear pipeline 7.Preprocessing included N4 bias correction, affine registration to the MNI-152 space, 1 mm isotropic resampling, skull stripping, and background cropping, yielding volumes of size 169 × 208 × 179 [30]. This placed all subjects in a com-mon anatomical space for downstream modelling. Before network input, MRI volumes were processed with MONAI 8. Voxel intensities were min-max normalized, and each volume was center-cropped to 

160 × 192 × 160 . During training, MONAI transforms were applied for data aug-mentation, including random contrast adjustment ( p = 0 .5), rotation ( ±0.05 

radian, p = 0 .2), and zooming ( [0 .95 , 1.05] , p = 0 .2) [18]. To capture neu-roanatomical patterns, we used ROI-guided patch extraction. For each MRI, 16 patches were sampled at each of three scales ( 64 3, 32 3, and 16 3).

Training followed a two-stage scheme: the tabular branch was first optimized with an auxiliary classification loss, followed by optimization of the MRI-tabular fusion network. # 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we proposed a geometry-aware multimodal framework(HypTI) for dementia classification. By leveraging the hierarchical structure of clinical as-sessments and the multi-scale anatomical patterns of neurodegeneration, the proposed framework moves beyond conventional Euclidean multimodal fusion. Experimental results show that this design achieves superior performance over representative unimodal and multimodal baselines. Ablation studies demonstrate that hyperbolic representation learning and fusion improve classification perfor-mance. These results indicate that the benefits of multimodal dementia detection arise not only from combining heterogeneous modalities, but also from learning representations in a geometry that better matches the underlying structure of the data. This opens new opportunities for geometry-aware multimodal learning in dementia research and broader medical AI applications. In future work, we will extend the evaluation to external datasets and longitudinal clinical measure-ments, while further analysing the interpretability of hyperbolic representations, and explore multilayer brain networks modelling [35], where hyperbolic repre-sentations may capture hierarchical cross-layer connectivity patterns.
