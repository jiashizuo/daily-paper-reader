# Abstract 

Spatiotemporal forecasting of 3D anatomical changes is a critical generative vision challenge for modeling Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) progression. While brain MRI forecasting from single-modality data has advanced, other key complementary multimodal biomarkers remain un-derutilized. In this work, we propose the Multimodal-

Conditioned Latent Diffusion Model ( MC-LDM ), a novel framework for patient-specific brain MRI synthesis. MC-LDM leverages multimodal biomarkers—MRI, tau-PET, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), and APOE ε4—during training, but relies only on baseline MRI, APOE ε4, and patient metadata at inference, reflecting realistic clinical availability. Our contributions are threefold: (1) a Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) fine-tuned via Low-Rank Adapta-tion (LoRA) for data-efficient learning; (2) a multimodal conditioning that integrates molecular and clinical signals to model heterogeneous disease trajectories; and (3) auxil-iary predictors estimating unobserved multimodal biomark-ers from MRI features and patient covariates to condi-tion the diffusion model, removing the need for these as-sessments at inference. Trained on 1,355 visits from 852 subjects in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initia-tive (ADNI), MC-LDM reduces average regional volumet-ric MAE by 18% over the second-best baseline on an inde-pendent test set of 664 visits. To our knowledge, MC-LDM represents the first diffusion-based framework to leverage multimodal priors for MRI forecasting. This conceptual advancement enables patient-specific trajectory generation that captures aspects of AD heterogeneity not observable from structural anatomy alone, bridging the gap between rich multimodal supervision and the strict constraints of routine clinical deployment. Code is available at: https: //github.com/antonioscardace/MC-LDM .

# 1. Introduction 

Forecasting 3D anatomical changes is a challenging spa-tiotemporal problem in generative computer vision, par-ticularly for modeling progressive pathologies such as Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). As an age-related neurodegen-erative disorder affecting over 57 million people worldwide, AD is projected to nearly triple in prevalence by 2050 [21]. It typically manifests in older adults through insidious cog-nitive decline, memory impairment, and behavioral alter-ations that severely disrupt daily life activities and ulti-mately lead to a complete loss of independence [9]. As a result, its clinical management critically depends on early detection and accurate monitoring of structural and patho-logical brain changes over time. In clinical practice, capturing the temporal evolution of AD may require a multimodal assessment involving com-plementary diagnostic tests to evaluate biomarkers associ-ated with three major pathological processes: amyloid-β

deposition (A), pathological tau (T), and neurodegeneration (N). Within this ATN framework [8, 9], each biomarker cat-egory is classified as either positive or negative. Among genetic factors, the APOE ε4 allele is the strongest known risk factor for AD, promoting early amyloid-β accumulation through impaired peptide clear-ance and reducing neuronal injury response [14, 17]. While this genetic vulnerability can be readily assessed via blood or saliva sampling [29], its downstream pathological consequence—the accumulation of amyloid deposits in the brain—can be directly visualized using amyloid Positron Emission Tomography (amyloid-PET). In turn, amyloid pathology is thought to drive subsequent tau aggregation, which can be measured via tau-PET and is strongly associ-ated with cognitive decline [18]. Despite their strong biological relevance, PET scans re-main limited in routine clinical use due to high cost and ex-posure to radioactive tracers, restricting repeated longitudi-nal assessments. As AD progresses, these molecular abnor-arXiv:2610.25481v1 [cs.CV] 18 Oct 2026 malities, such as amyloid and tau accumulation, contribute to neurodegeneration, ultimately manifesting as structural brain changes. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) thus remains central to AD evaluation: it enables the assessment of macrostructural biomarkers such as hippocampal atrophy and ventricular enlargement [5], and is well suited for rou-tine monitoring thanks to its low-cost and non-invasive na-ture. Finally, clinical measures such as the Clinical Demen-tia Rating (CDR) [19] relate these biological and structural changes to functional impairment in daily life. To support early diagnosis, Disease Progression Mod-eling (DPM) provides a temporal framework for integrat-ing heterogeneous biomarkers into coherent disease trajec-tories. Because AD progresses non-linearly over decades with marked inter-patient variability, forecasting individual trajectories is crucial not only for personalized care plan-ning, but also for clinical trial design—enabling the se-lection of participants expected to exhibit measurable end-point progression within the trial window to maximize treat-ment effect size [16]. Methodologically, DPM is com-monly categorized into population-level approaches, which estimate average progression patterns across subjects, and individual-level models, which aim to provide personalized forecasts from baseline clinical and imaging data. Despite recent advances, current state-of-the-art methods still face three critical bottlenecks: 1. Many approaches require thousands of aligned longitu-dinal scans for stable convergence, given the models’ millions of trainable parameters. This issue is further exacerbated when multimodal aligned inputs are intro-duced, as such datasets are also inherently scarce, in-creasing the risk of overfitting. 2. Existing methods typically rely solely on MRI and ba-sic demographic variables, overlooking complementary molecular, cognitive, and genetic information. This lim-ited representation restricts the models’ ability to capture the pronounced heterogeneity of AD progression. 3. Even when multimodal data are available, practical bar-riers in clinical settings remain, as key modalities such as tau-PET are rarely acquired longitudinally, leaving MRI as the most accessible imaging tool. Consequently, mod-els that depend on rich multimodal inputs would face significant obstacles to routine clinical deployment. To address these challenges, we introduce MC-LDM, a multimodal-conditioned latent diffusion framework for individual-level AD progression forecasting from 3D brain MRI. To overcome limitation (1), we explicitly condition the latent diffusion process on molecular, genetic, and clinical signals using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [7] for parameter-efficient training, mitigating overfitting while guiding MRI synthesis in small multimodal cohorts. To address limitation (2), MC-LDM incorporates multimodal priors into the generative process, allowing more accurate modeling of progression heterogeneity. To address lim-itation (3), auxiliary models predict regional tau uptake and CDR scores from MRI-derived volumetric features and patient metadata, enabling multimodal supervision during training without requiring extensive clinical assessments at inference. This strategy is motivated by prior evidence that complementary biomarkers from other modalities, such as PET, are reflected in MRI-derived patterns [10, 33, 36, 38], enabling effective cross-modal supervision during training. In our design, MC-LDM supports both cross-sectional and longitudinal synthesis. To the best of our knowledge, it represents the first generative framework to fully integrate multimodal AD biomarkers into 3D MRI forecasting while remaining clinically practical. Beyond improved forecast-ing performance, the core novelty of this work lies in ad-vancing DPM towards a practical multimodal paradigm. By adopting an asymmetric conditioning strategy, our frame-work effectively bridges the gap between rich multimodal supervision during training and the strict missing-modality constraints of real-world clinical deployment. 

# 2. Related Work 

In this section, we review related work on (i) population-level disease progression modeling, and (ii) individual-level disease progression modeling approaches. 

2.1. Population-Level DPM 

Population-level disease progression models aim to learn shared trajectories of disease evolution from longitudi-nal cohorts, often using statistical formulations such as mixed-effects models [37] to capture inter-subject variabil-ity around a common progression pattern. Recent works have combined these statistical formu-lations with deep generative models to capture high-dimensional clinical data. For example, [34] and [2] model continuous disease trajectories in low-dimensional Eu-clidean and Riemannian latent spaces, respectively, while incorporating shared clinical characteristics. However, these methods tend to represent disease progression through shared trajectories within the population, potentially ob-scuring clinically relevant heterogeneity. To address this is-sue, recent approaches [42] have introduced subtype-aware formulations that capture multiple progression patterns. 

2.2. Individual-Level DPM 

Individual-level progression models adopt a flexible formu-lation, directly predicting future disease states from subject-specific baseline observations. By modeling individual tra-jectories, they can naturally use chronological age as a tem-poral reference for personalized forecasts. This contrasts with population-level methodologies, which typically re-quire the estimation of a latent biological timeline to align subjects at different stages of the disease cascade. Compared to population-level methods, this flexibility often comes at the cost of reduced interpretability, as per-sonalized predictions are less constrained by an explicit shared disease model. Early approaches primarily relied on scalar clinical scores and biomarkers [22, 46], providing limited spatial interpretability of pathology [4, 43]. More recent spatiotemporal models operate directly on high-dimensional imaging data, enabling voxel-wise localization of structural changes and richer representations of disease progression [30]. However, a persistent challenge in these latent-space individual architectures is their inability to for-mally isolate the mathematical specificity of each biomarker in driving the personalized trajectory, often obscuring the underlying phenotypical subgroups. To generate high-fidelity forecasts, deep generative mod-els have recently gained traction for longitudinal MRI forecasting. Early diffusion-based approaches, such as DDM [11], were developed for deformable registration to model anatomical changes between a baseline and a target scan and predict intermediate progression. Because the tar-get scan is required, however, they cannot forecast an un-observed future state. More recent approaches instead aim to predict future anatomical states from baseline informa-tion, without requiring the target scan as input. For ex-ample, CounterSynth [27] employs a GAN-based counter-factual framework. While effective at generating realistic counterfactuals, it can suffer from training instability and limited temporal consistency. Recent works have therefore shifted toward latent diffusion. BrLP [28] leverages tem-poral information and clinical priors for high-fidelity syn-thesis, while TADM-3D [13] predicts voxel-wise intensity residuals, conditioning forecasts on arbitrary time intervals. MambaControl [45] guides the diffusion process by explic-itly modeling connections between brain regions and the patient’s long-term history. Finally, ∆-LFM [3] models patient-specific disease progression from longitudinal im-age data by conceptualizing progression as a continuous ve-locity field in latent space, enabling individualized temporal evolution over future time points. Despite these advances, current state-of-the-art architectures remain primarily uni-modal, relying largely on structural anatomy and basic de-mographics while overlooking the complementary prognos-tic value of molecular, genetic, and cognitive biomarkers. 

# 3. Preliminaries 

In this section, we present the core concepts required to con-struct MC-LDM. We first establish the clinical rationale for targeting structural MRI, before detailing the latent diffu-sion architecture and the parameter-efficient conditioning mechanisms necessary to integrate multimodal priors. 

3.1. Structural MRI as the Forecasting Target 

A key design choice of our framework is to forecast struc-tural MRI rather than alternative modalities. While tech-niques such as tau-PET are highly informative in the early and pre-symptomatic stages of AD, they present important physical, economic, and practical limitations [20]. More generally, several non-structural modalities, including PET, CSF biomarkers, and functional imaging, are either inva-sive, costly, less widely available—a key requirement for training our model—or difficult to acquire longitudinally at scale. These limitations directly affect their suitability as prediction targets in real-world clinical settings and con-strain their use for training data collection. In particular, MRI provides high-resolution ( ≈ 1 mm) imaging of brain anatomy, enabling direct assessment of macroscopic neurodegeneration and atrophy, which are closely linked to cognitive decline. MRI also serves as a reference modality for interpreting and correcting PET scans, whose clinical resolution ( ≈ 4–5 mm) is substan-tially lower, through Partial Volume Correction (PVC) [39], further highlighting its central role in multimodal analysis. 

3.2. Diffusion Models 

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) [6] learn to model the data distribution p(x) by gradually re-versing a predefined forward noising process. In the for-ward process, Gaussian noise is incrementally added to a data sample x0 ∼ pdata (x) over T steps according to a fixed Markov chain. For sufficiently large T , the resulting vari-able xT converges to pure Gaussian noise, xT ∼ N (0 , I ).Let c denote a conditioning vector; the model then learns a denoising network ϵθ (xt, t, c ) to approximate the reverse transition pθ (xt−1 | xt, c ).Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) [31] extend this paradigm by performing the diffusion process in a low-dimensional latent space z = E(x) obtained via an au-toencoder, rather than in the high-dimensional image space. This enables more efficient training and high-resolution synthesis. While DDPMs operate directly in pixel space, LDMs shift the diffusion process into the latent domain. The reverse process operates on latent variables zt: starting from Gaussian noise zT ∼ N (0 , I ), the model iteratively denoises the representation until z0 is obtained, which is fi-nally decoded as ˆx = D(z0). 3.2.2. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning 

Fully fine-tuning diffusion models to incorporate new con-ditioning signals typically requires large datasets and can lead to catastrophic forgetting. To mitigate this, LoRA [7] introduces a parameter-efficient adaptation strategy that keeps the original weights fixed while learning low-rank up-dates. Specifically, LoRA freezes the pre-trained weight matrix W0 and optimizes a decomposition ∆W = BA ,where B and A are trainable low-rank matrices. 

# 4. Methods 

In this section, we present our pipeline, comprising four stages depicted in Figure 1: (A) LoRA-based LDM fine-tuning, (B) ControlNet training, (C) predictive models train-ing, and (D) inference. All modules are trained sequentially, with no end-to-end optimization. 

4.1. Multimodal Conditioned LDM 

Our framework is built upon BrLP [28] by adapting its LDM to support multimodal-conditioned forecasting under realistic clinical constraints (step A in Figure 1). In par-ticular, each MRI x is encoded into a latent representa-tion z = E(x) using an autoencoder (E, D). The diffusion model is trained using the standard noise prediction objec-tive, minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the true noise ϵ and the predicted noise ϵθ (zt, t, c ), where 

zt denotes the noisy latent at timestep t and c is the multi-modal conditioning vector. During training, all components of the conditioning vector c—age, sex, diagnosis, regional volumes, tau uptake, APOE ε4 status, CDR global score, and Total Intracranial Volume (TIV)—are extracted from ground-truth acquisitions, ensuring supervision from real molecular and clinical measurements. Instead of retraining the LDM backbone from scratch, LoRA injects trainable low-rank matrices into the query Q, key K, and value V of both self-attention and cross-attention layers. While the LDM captures global disease-related infor-mation through c, it provides limited control over subject-specific anatomy. Following BrLP, we therefore subse-quently train a ControlNet [47] branch (step B in Figure 1) conditioned on the latent encoding of the baseline MRI, 

z(A) = E(x(A)). During this stage, the LoRA-adapted U-Net is kept fully frozen, while only the ControlNet param-eters ϕ are optimized. The ControlNet operates alongside the frozen U-Net during denoising, yielding a unified noise predictor ϵθ,ϕ (zt, t, c, z (A)) without violating the sequential training scheme. This branch anchors the generation pro-cess to the patient’s baseline anatomy, encouraging the fore-casted scan to preserve subject-specific structural character-istics while modeling their future evolution. 4.2. Auxiliary Predictive Models 

To condition the LDM, our framework requires estimates of the patient’s future biomarkers at the target age B. How-ever, AD induces region-specific structural changes at vary-ing rates [26], making a direct mapping from baseline MRI to future multimodal biomarkers challenging. We therefore first employ a Disease Course Mapping (DCM) framework [12]. Given a subject’s historical re-gional volumes and age, the DCM, denoted as fψ , mod-els continuous volumetric trajectories to forecast future re-gional volumes ˆv(B) at target age B. It also estimates two subject-specific parameters: the time-shift τ (measured in years), which quantifies how much later or earlier the dis-ease onset is compared to the population average; and the log-acceleration factor ξ, a scalar modulating the overall rate of volumetric decline (with positive values indicating faster progression than the average). We then compute the biological age ∆bio = age B − τ , which explicitly aligns the subject along a common pathological timeline. We next define a target demographic vector s(B) contain-ing age B , diagnosis A, sex, TIV, and APOE ε4 status. To strictly prevent temporal data leakage, all patient-specific variables are derived exclusively from the baseline visit. TIV is assumed constant throughout adulthood, while age B

is specified by the user as the target forecasting age. We then concatenate s(B), ∆bio , and ˆv(B) to train three indepen-dent auxiliary models responsible for estimating the future clinical and pathological descriptors required for the diffu-sion conditioning (step C in Figure 1). Specifically, future mean tau uptake in the amygdala ( ˆu(B)amy ) and hippocampus (ˆu(B)hipp ) are predicted via two separate RBF-kernel Support Vector Regressors (SVRs), while a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classifies the future CDR global score ( ˆy(B)cdr ). These predictors are not intended as clinical surrogates for actual PET scans or formal cognitive assessments. In-stead, their outputs act as informative priors that provide complementary multimodal guidance during forecasting. 

4.3. Inference Process 

Let x(A) denote the MRI acquired at age A, with its re-gional volumes v(A). Our goal is to synthesize the future MRI ˆx(B) at target age B > A . Because patient trajecto-ries may be influenced by additional factors not explicitly modeled in our framework, including undocumented treat-ments, lifestyle variations, and comorbidities, our inference process represents future brain anatomy as a probability dis-tribution over plausible outcomes rather than as a single de-terministic prediction. Since the final output must be a single MRI, we follow prior work that uses the average latent prediction as the final estimate, as this has been shown to produce robust results. More specifically, we adopt the Latent Average Stabiliza-tion (LAS) strategy introduced in [28, 33]. Here, individual latent forecasts ˆz(B,i ) can be viewed as samples from the model’s predictive distribution at age B. Averaging m in-dependently generated forecasts attenuates high-frequency stochastic variation and yields a more stable estimate of the expected latent representation, defined as:

4.4. Pipeline Settings 

We use the U-Net and ControlNet implementations pro-vided by the MONAI Generative Models library [1, 25]. All the auxiliary predictive models were implemented us-ing the scikit-learn library [23]. The autoencoder and the DCM are adopted from BrLP [28], using their publicly available pre-trained model. The pre-trained LDM backbone is kept frozen during training. We inject LoRA adapters into the U-Net, which are optimized for 120 epochs using the AdamW [15] opti-mizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −4, a weight decay of 0.01, and a batch size of 8. LoRA modules use rank r = 32 ,scaling factor α = 64 , and dropout p = 0 .1. The Control-Net parameters ϕ are randomly initialized and trained under the same settings as in BrLP [28]. At inference, we set the LAS hyperparameter m = 8 .All model training procedures and experiments were performed on a workstation equipped with an I NTEL (R) CORE (TM) I7-14700 CPU, 32 GB of RAM, and a single NVIDIA RTX 4070 T I SUPER GPU (16 GB of VRAM).

5.1. Dataset 

The dataset includes 1,355 visits corresponding to 852 sub-jects from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 1/2/3/4/GO cohorts [24]. The cohort has a mean age of 73 .0 ± 8.0 years and comprises 55.7% female and 44.3% male patients. Each visit includes a T1-weighted brain MRI, a tau-PET scan acquired with the 18 F-AV1451 tracer, a CDR global score, and APOE ε4 status. Follow-ing the protocol described in [36], MRI and tau-PET scans were grouped into the same multimodal visit if acquired within a 6-month window [40], while the CDR global score was required to fall within a 12-month window [44]. All MRI and tau-PET scans were then preprocessed using the 

neuroflux pipeline [35, 36]. To rigorously prevent data leakage, all dataset partitions were strictly defined at the subject level using unique study identifiers (Roster IDs), ensuring that all longitudinal visits from a single individual remain within the same partition. For model development, the 1,355 visits were partitioned into training and validation sets using a 75/25 subject-level split, resulting in 639 training subjects (1,080 visits) and 213 validation subjects (275 visits). An independent test set comprising 664 visits from 197 subjects within the same ADNI cohorts was used for evaluation and includes only T1-weighted MRI and APOE ε4 status. Thus, the complete study encompasses 1,049 unique subjects, with zero overlap between the development and test sets. 

5.2. Auxiliary Predictive Models Evaluation 

To evaluate the auxiliary predictors described in Sec-tion 4.2, we assess their standalone performance using 5-fold nested cross-validation and fit the final models ex-clusively on the LDM training set. The SVR predictors achieved a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.220 ±0.018 for the amygdala and 0.168 ±0.013 for the hippocampus. These errors correspond to 8.2% and 7.0% of their respective ob-served ranges ( 0.30 –2.99 and 0.37 –2.77 ). All tau uptake measurements, including errors and ranges, are reported as Standardized Uptake Value Ratios. The CDR global score classifier achieved an accuracy of 82 .03% .Overall, these results indicate that structural MRI and basic demographic features provide predictive information about molecular and cognitive states. The auxiliary predic-tors therefore provide informative priors for the MC-LDM generator, enabling it to operate without future tau-PET and clinical assessments at inference time. 

5.3. Evaluation Metrics 

We evaluate MC-LDM using (i) image-level metrics— Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) and MSE—which cap-ture voxel-wise differences and structural consistency, and 

(ii) region-wise volumetric errors, assessed via MAE with respect to true follow-up scans. We focus on three AD-relevant structures—the hippocampus, amygdala, and lat-eral ventricles—known to exhibit characteristic atrophy and ventricular enlargement during disease progression.

5.4.1. Multimodal Integration Strategy 

We first compared two strategies for integrating multimodal covariates into the pre-trained LDM: direct fusion, where the new multimodal features are concatenated with the ex-isting conditioning features and only the relevant cross-attention blocks are fine-tuned (25M trainable parameters), and the proposed LoRA-based modulation, which requires only 3M trainable parameters. As reported in Table 1 (A), direct fusion leads to performance degradation, whereas LoRA-based integration provides a more effective adapta-tion of the pre-trained model. Specifically, it significantly reduces volumetric MAE by 30.6% in the hippocampus, 15.8% in the amygdala, and 47.5% in the lateral ventricles, while reducing MSE by 25% ( p < 0.05 , with large effect sizes d > 1.0 across all metrics), supporting LoRA as an effective strategy for multimodal conditioning. 

5.4.2. Contribution of the Clinical Modalities 

Using the LoRA-based configuration, we next evaluated the contribution of each clinical prior through a leave-one-modality-out analysis. As shown in Table 1 (B), remov-ing any modality degrades performance relative to the full model, although the magnitude of the effect varies across re-gions and metrics. Removing tau-PET produces the largest degradation in volumetric accuracy, increasing MAE by 27.7% in the lateral ventricles, 18.7% in the amygdala, and 11.8% in the hippocampus. Removing CDR increases MSE by 33.3% and lateral ventricles MAE by 25.4%, while removing APOE ε4 increases lateral ventricles MAE by 22.8% and hippocampus MAE by 8.8%. Although these single-modality removals do not reach statistical signifi-cance ( p > 0.05 ), the consistent degradation across anatom-ical regions and error metrics suggests that molecular, cog-nitive, and genetic priors provide complementary prognos-tic information beyond baseline anatomy alone. 

5.4.3. Predicted Biomarker Error Propagation 

Finally, we assessed the effect of errors introduced by the auxiliary predictors in our pipeline. We compared the fully automated pipeline with an oracle configuration con-ditioned on the ground-truth future biomarkers, thereby iso-lating the impact of auxiliary prediction errors. As shown in Table 1 (C), the predicted-biomarker configuration pro-duces only small absolute increases in volumetric MAE, of 1.9% in the lateral ventricles and 6.6% in the amygdala, while SSIM decreases by only 0.1%. No statistically sig-nificant differences were observed across the evaluated met-rics, indicating that forecasting performance is relatively in-sensitive to auxiliary prediction errors and supporting the use of predicted biomarkers when future multimodal assess-ments are unavailable at inference. 

5.5. Benchmarks 

We compare our framework against three spatiotemporal generative models: CounterSynth [27], TADM-3D [13], and BrLP [28]. In particular, BrLP serves as our direct MRI-only baseline to quantify the performance gains ob-tained from multimodal conditioning. All baselines were implemented using their official, publicly available code and evaluated under the same data and evaluation proce-dures, ensuring a fair and consistent comparison. We evaluate these methods across two settings: cross-sectional forecasting, which relies on a single baseline MRI, and longitudinal forecasting, which exploits multiple histor-ical scans when available. 

5.5.1. Quantitative Results 

As shown in Table 2, MC-LDM achieves the best over-all performance. In the cross-sectional setting, it outper-forms the second-best method (BrLP) across all image-level metrics, improving SSIM by 0.9% and reducing MSE by 20%, indicating better structural fidelity. # 6. Conclusions 

We introduced MC-LDM, a multimodal-conditioned latent diffusion framework for patient-specific brain MRI fore-casting that integrates heterogeneous disease descriptors within a single generative model. By leveraging rich mul-timodal supervision during training, MC-LDM remains ap-plicable at inference using only baseline MRI, APOE ε4,and available patient metadata, enabling disease progres-sion modeling under limited longitudinal data. High-value modalities such as tau-PET provide direct pathological in-sight but are costly and sparsely acquired, limiting the ap-plicability of conventional multimodal methods. MC-LDM learns statistical relationships between molecular, clinical, and genetic factors and future anatomical changes, while replacing unavailable modalities at inference with auxiliary predictions. Beyond quantitative improvements, our work provides a step toward multimodal DPM for patient-specific 3D brain forecasting under realistic clinical constraints through an asymmetric conditioning paradigm that decou-ples multimodal supervision during training from modality availability at deployment. Despite these promising results, some limitations re-main. Our framework relies on a sequential training strat-egy, which may lead to error accumulation across inter-mediate stages. In addition, our evaluation is restricted to the ADNI cohort. Although external evaluation is impor-tant to assess generalizability, it is currently constrained by the limited availability of independent longitudinal co-horts with the specific combination of multimodal acqui-sitions, such as concurrent MRI and tau-PET, required by our pipeline. As more comprehensive longitudinal datasets become available, future work will prioritize rigorous cross-cohort evaluation. Further ablation studies will also inves-tigate the individual contributions of multimodal condition-ing and parameter-efficient fine-tuning, including a compar-ison with a same-architecture BrLP baseline using LoRA fine-tuning without multimodal conditioning, to better clar-ify the sources of the observed improvements.
