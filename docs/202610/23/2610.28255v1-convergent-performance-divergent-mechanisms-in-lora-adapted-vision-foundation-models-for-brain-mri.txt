A B S T R A C T 

Adapting Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) to medical imaging via LoRA has become standard practice, yet model selection is guided almost entirely by downstream task metrics, treating backbones with similar performance as interchangeable. We investigate whether equal performance implies equal internal representational shift. We analyse four ViT-B/16 backbones, DINOv3, SigLIP2, MerMED-FM, and BiomedCLIP, pre-trained with different objectives (self-distillation vs. contrastive vision-language) and on different data domains (natural vs. medical images). We adapt each to brain tumour segmentation and evaluate on six datasets covering both segmentation and Alzheimer’s disease classification. Using attention divergence, weight-change analysis, and activation patching, we find that the four models converge to similar segmentation performance (mean Dice 78–81%) through markedly different internal dynamics, yet diverge in cross-task transfer: segmentation adaptation improves Alzheimer’s disease classification for DINOv3 but degrades it for the other three backbones. We reveal a dissociation between modification magnitude and causal importance: the most heavily modified layers are not the causally relevant ones, with early-to-mid layers identified as sufficient for segmentation recovery. Restricting LoRA to these layers matches or approaches full-layer seg-mentation while reducing classification degradation in the three affected backbones, providing a mechanistically grounded strategy that is more parameter-efficient and more robust than standard full-layer adaptation. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/fkekwick/Brain_Lora .

## 1. Introduction 

Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), which are pre-trained on large-scale image datasets, provide powerful visual feature encoders applicable to diverse downstream computer vision tasks [1]. Adapting them via Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) has become standard practice, with methods like LoRA [2] achieving competitive results across diverse data domains [3–9] at a fraction of full fine-tuning costs. In medical imaging tasks, such as brain tu-mour segmentation, LoRA-adapted foundation models now rival fully supervised baselines [10, 11]. Given the numer-ous publicly available VFMs backbones [12–15], choosing which backbone best serves the downstream task becomes a key design decision. Currently, model selection is guided almost entirely by downstream task metrics [16]; i.e., two backbones that reach the same segmentation Dice score are treated as interchangeable. In this paper, we investigate an intriguing mechanistic question: whether equal performance implies equal internal representation shift, as differences in how models reshape their representations may expose

A model whose representations degrade on one task while improving on another poses a deployment risk that task-specific evaluation cannot detect. Understanding how models adapt, not only how well they perform, is therefore necessary for informed backbone selec-tion. Mechanistic interpretability tools developed for natural language and vision models [17] offer a principled way to characterise this representational shift and to identify which layers drive task performance. We study four ViT-B/16 backbones, comparing both pre-training objective (self-distillation vs. contrastive vision-language) and source domain (natural vs. medical im-ages): DINOv3 [12], SigLIP2 [13], MerMED-FM [14], and BiomedCLIP [15]. All four models are adapted via LoRA to brain tumour segmentation on multi-parametric MRI and evaluated across six datasets spanning three seg-mentation tasks (BraTS-GLI [18–20], BraTS-Africa [21], BraTS-PED [22]) and three classification tasks (OASIS-1 [23], ADNI binary, ADNI multi-class [24]). We analyse the adaptation process through the lens of four comple-mentary mechanistic tools: attention Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence [25], LoRA weight-change ratios [2], off-brain attention fraction, and layer-wise activation patching [26].

Guided by the mechanistic diagnosis, we further propose and validate an early-layer LoRA strategy that restricts adaptation to the layers identified as causally important. Our main findings are as follows. (i) Although the four backbones converge to similar segmentation performance after LoRA adaptation, they diverge substantially in cross-task classification transfer. Mechanistic analysis reveals that segmentation convergence masks fundamentally dif-ferent representational shift profiles, and medical-domain pre-training confers no consistent segmentation advantage. 

(ii) The most heavily modified layers are not the causally important ones: activation patching consistently identifies early-to-mid layers as sufficient to recover full segmenta-tion performance from a frozen encoder, while late-layer adaptation can damage cross-task transfer, as shown by the degraded linear probing classification performance. (iii) Re-stricting LoRA to early layers matches or approaches full-layer segmentation while improving classification perfor-mance relative to all-layer adaptation in the three backbones whose cross-task transfer degrades, providing a mechanisti-cally grounded strategy that is both more parameter-efficient and more robust than adapting all layers. Together, these findings suggest that integrating layer-wise interpretability tools with multi-task downstream as-sessment reveals adaptation risks invisible to standard met-rics and motivates practical strategies that would not emerge from task-specific evaluation alone. While our study focuses on brain MRI, the analytical framework can be applied to any attention-based encoder and data domain. 

## 2. Related Work 

Adapting Vision Foundation Models to medical imaging via PEFT has grown rapidly in recent years. LoRA [2] and its variants have been applied to diverse segmentation tasks [3, 4], including brain tumours [8, 10]. A common approach in the literature is adapting a single foundation model per study and evaluating it on diverse datasets within one single downstream task, treating the backbone as a fixed design choice justified by its performance metric. Comparative analyses across backbones remain limited to performance tables without examining how different pre-training paradigms shape the internal adaptation [5, 6, 27]. Beyond downstream metrics, mechanistic interpretabil-ity offers tools to examine how models internally reorganise during adaptation, including attention analysis, probing, and causal interventions such as activation patching [17, 26]. Recent work has begun applying these tools to LoRA-tuned models [28], using mechanistic analysis to guide adapter placement [29,30], to decode attention head behaviour under distribution shift [31], and to disentangle clinical from class-agnostic features during few-shot adaptation [32]. These studies provide valuable insight into individual models but, by analysing a single backbone in isolation, cannot reveal whether the same adaptation procedure produces different internal dynamics across architectures. Furthermore, the connection between mechanistic diagnosis and a concrete change in adaptation strategy remains largely unexplored in the vision domain: a recent exception in NLP uses causal and geometric analysis to guide LoRA placement across layers [33], but interpretability findings in medical imaging are still reported descriptively rather than validated through intervention. Our work addresses both gaps by comparing mechanistic profiles across four backbones and by using the diagnostic findings to motivate and test a layer-selective LoRA strategy. The question of which layers to adapt has also received attention. Some studies dynamically allocate rank budgets across layers [34] based on gradient-derived importance scores [35–37], while domain-specific heuristics select lay-ers using task performance proxies [5] or Fisher informa-tion [38]. These approaches tend to favour later layers, consistent with the intuition that deeper representations are more task-specific [39, 40]. Our results show that activation patching identifies early-to-mid layers as causally sufficient for segmentation, and that restricting adaptation to these lay-ers preserves cross-task transferability that full-layer adapta-tion can compromise. This finding motivates layer selection based on causal importance and multi-task evaluation rather than gradient magnitude. 

## 3. Method 

3.1. Adaptation Pipeline 

As illustrated in Figure 1, we adapt four ViT-B/16 back-bones [41] to brain image segmentation task. Four brain MRI modalities (T1n, T1c, T2w, T2f) are fused via a trainable 

1×1 convolution that maps these four input channels to three, matching the RGB input expected by all encoders. All four modalities carry complementary clinical information [42], so we retain all of them and let the learned convolution deter-mine the optimal combination. This fusion layer is initialised with uniform weights of 1∕4 with no bias term, equivalent to average pooling, and trained end-to-end independently for each backbone to learn modality-specific contributions. The ViT-B/16 encoder is kept frozen; adaptation is achieved through LoRA modules injected into each of the 12 transformer blocks. From the adapted encoder, we extract multi-scale representations by tapping four uniformly spaced blocks {3 , 6, 9, 12} (1-indexed), each taken after the encoder’s final layer normalisation and reshaped from the token sequence into a 14 × 14 spatial feature map with 768 channels. 240×240 pixels. The LoRA modules and segmentation head are jointly trained with an equal-weight combination of Dice and cross-entropy loss. 3.2. Mechanistic Analysis 

We analyse the adaptation process [43] through four complementary tools: (1) attention Jensen-Shannon (JS) di-vergence [25], (2) LoRA weight-change ratio [2], (3) off-brain attention fraction, and (4) layer-wise activation patch-ing [26]. JS divergence and weight-change ratio quantify the magnitude of adaptation in output space and parameter space, respectively. As the LoRA update Δ𝑊 𝑄 = 𝐵 𝑄 𝐴 𝑄 

modifies the query projection, it directly alters the attention logits 𝑄 ⋅ 𝐾 ⊤, changing the resulting attention distribution. The weight-change ratio measures this modification in pa-rameter space, while JS divergence measures its downstream effect on the attention pattern; together, they capture the same adaptation from complementary viewpoints. Off-brain attention fraction examines the adaptation cost by measuring how much frozen attention must be redirected toward task-relevant regions. Finally, activation patching isolates which layer adaptations causally drive task performance.

3.3. Layer-Selective LoRA 

To check whether the causal importance patterns identi-fied by activation patching translate into a practical adapta-tion strategy, we split the 12 transformer blocks at the mid-point, applying LoRA to blocks 1–6 ( early ) or 7–12 ( late ), alongside the default all (1–12). The same cut-off is used across all four backbones to test whether a single, model-agnostic configuration is viable rather than optimising the split point per backbone. In each configuration, LoRA mod-ules are injected only into the selected blocks, with non-selected ones retaining their original frozen weights. The segmentation head and fusion convolution are trained from scratch for each configuration to ensure a fair comparison, so performance differences reflect the effect of the LoRA module placement. 

3.4. Linear Probing Classification 

To further assess whether representations learned dur-ing segmentation transfer to an unrelated task, we evaluate each backbone on Alzheimer’s disease classification via linear probing [45]. Features are extracted from the final transformer block with layer normalisation applied, and the patch token sequence is mean-pooled into a single 768-dimensional vector per slice. The 2𝐷 representations of all slices belonging to the same subject are then mean-pooled into a single subject-level representation. A logistic regression classifier is fitted on the training set and evaluated on the test set. During feature extraction, the LoRA-adapted segmentation encoder is used as a fixed feature extractor: the adapted weights (including LoRA modules) are loaded but not updated, so the features reflect the post-segmentation-adaptation representations without any further training.

4.2. Datasets 

For glioma tumour segmentation, models are adapted on the BraTS-GLI [18–20] adult glioma dataset using all four MRI modalities (T1n, T1c, T2w, T2f) as input. Seg-mentation is evaluated at the 2D slice level on three nested tumour sub-regions: whole tumour (WT), tumour core (TC, combining enhancing and necrotic cells), and enhancing tumour (ENH, active infiltrating cells). The dataset com-prises 153 ,105 2 D slices from 1,251 patients, with a patient-stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split. Out-of-distribution segmentation generalisation is evalu-ated on BraTS-Africa [21] ( 7,462 slices from 60 patients from Sub-Saharan African populations with glioma) and BraTS-PED [22] ( 11 ,923 slices from 99 pediatric patients with glioma), without any retraining. For the classification tasks, we use the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS-1) [23] ( 39 ,438 2 D slices from 416 subjects) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) datasets [24] ( 174 ,458 slices from 1,494 subjects), taking the first T1n scan per subject in case of a longitudinal study. We use the default L2 regularisation strength ( 𝐶 = 1)without tuning, so no validation set is used; each cross-validation fold uses a subject-level 70/30 train/test split (Section 4.5). On OASIS-1, we perform binary Alzheimer’s disease classification, and on ADNI both binary (CN vs. AD) and 3-class (CN vs. MCI vs. AD) classification, where CN is the label for a scan from a Cognitively Normal study participant, MCI denotes Mild Cognitive Impairment, and AD represents the presence of Alzheimer’s Disease. 

4.3. Preprocessing 

We applied volume-wise z-score normalisation, fol-lowed by 2𝐷 slicing. 4.4. LoRA Adaptation Implementation Details 

All trainable components (LoRA modules, fusion con-volution, and segmentation head) are jointly trained for 50 epochs using the AdamW optimiser [46] with a batch size of 32 and weight decay of 0.05 . We use a learning rate of 10 −4 

for the LoRA modules and 10 −3 for the fusion convolution and segmentation head. Each experiment is run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and repeated with 3 random seeds; seg-mentation results report mean ± standard deviation across seeds, while classification results are additionally averaged across cross-validation folds. 

4.5. Linear Probing Classification Implementation 

In this task, only a logistic regression classifier is trained on the features extracted from each backbone, using L2-regularised logistic regression ( 𝐶 = 1 ) with class-balanced weights and the L-BFGS solver [47], with a maximum of 2,000 iterations. Since OASIS-1 and ADNI provide only single-modality T1 scans, the fusion convolution is not used during feature extraction; instead, the input channel is repli-cated to three channels and normalised with each backbone’s specific preprocessing. The logistic regression classifier uses a 3-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation evaluation. The results are averaged across folds and across encoder training seeds for the LoRA adaptations. The L-BFGS solver is determin-istic, so no seeds were varied for the probe. 

## 5. Results 

5.1. Segmentation Performance 

Table 1 reports in-distribution segmentation on BraTS-GLI. Across all four backbones, LoRA adaptation yields substantial improvements over the frozen baseline, raising mean Dice by ∼8–11% points regardless of the backbone. After adaptation, the four models converge to a narrow per-formance range (mean Dice ∼78–81% at 𝑟 = 8 ), despite their different pre-training configurations. DINOv3 achieves the highest and BiomedCLIP the lowest mean Dice in both the frozen and adapted settings, while SigLIP2 and MerMED-FM lie in between: frozen MerMED-FM outperforms frozen SigLIP2, and after adaptation the two differ by less than 0.5 points. Pre-training domain alone therefore does not determine the ranking. The rank ablation reveals negligible sensitivity: for each backbone, the difference between the best and worst rank is below ∼1% in mean Dice, and the differences between ranks fall within the standard deviation across seeds, confirming that rank tuning is unnecessary for this task. We therefore fix 𝑟 = 8 for all subsequent experiments, following the default configuration in [2]. Table 2 extends the evaluation to out-of-distribution settings. LoRA adaptation improves mean Dice by 15-19% on both BraTS-Africa and BraTS-PED across all four backbones, suggesting that adaptation captures domain-general brain MRI representations rather than overfitting to in-distribution features. The relative ordering of backbones is preserved, with DINOv3 achieving the highest Dice and BiomedCLIP the lowest. Figure 2 provides qualitative examples across the three segmentation datasets, illustrating how LoRA sharpens region boundaries for all four encoders.

5.5. Layer-Selective LoRA 

Figure 6 summarises performance across the three LoRA configurations ( early , all , late ) on all six datasets, with the frozen baseline marked as a black star where it falls within the plotted range. These experiments serve as a validation of the activation patching findings: if the early-layer recovery observed in Figure 5 merely reflected signal accumulation rather than genuine causal sufficiency, restricting LoRA to early layers would substantially degrade performance. In segmentation (upper row), early-layer LoRA matches or approaches all-layer performance for all four backbones across the three datasets, while late-layer LoRA consistently underperforms both. This validates the activation patching finding that early-to-mid layers are causally sufficient for segmentation. DINOv3 exhibits the smallest variation across configurations, consistent with its more uniform mechanistic profile (Figure 3, panels a and b). In classification (bottom row), the effect of layer selec-tion is more pronounced. All-layer LoRA degrades classi-fication relative to the frozen baseline for 3/4 backbones by up to ∼6% AUC (Table 3); early-layer LoRA reduces this gap to ∼3% or eliminates it entirely, and in some cases recovers or exceeds frozen performance. Late-layer LoRA, while occasionally outperforming all-layer adaptation, in the majority of the cases remains below early-layer performance and exhibits greater instability across datasets. This general pattern could suggest that the late-layer representational shift identified by activation patching may be responsible for the cross-task damage observed in Section 5.2: by not adapting those layers, early-layer LoRA preserves the representations that classification relies on. DINOv3, however, is a clear outlier in the classification results, in five out of the six tasks, the all layer LoRA adaptation gives the best results. This is consistent with the classification results in Table 3, where DINOv3 was the only backbone to show improved classification performance after LoRA adaptation.

## 6. Discussion 

Our results show that four backbones with comparable segmentation performance diverge substantially in cross-task classification transfer. Mechanistic analysis traces this divergence to different representational shift profiles and reveals a dissociation between modification magnitude and causal importance: the largest attention divergence and weight changes concentrate in later layers (Figure 3), yet activation patching identifies early-to-mid layers as causally sufficient for segmentation across all four backbones (Fig-ure 5). The layer-selective LoRA experiments support this finding: restricting adaptation to early layers matches or approaches all-layer segmentation while mitigating the clas-sification degradation that all-layer adaptation causes (Fig-ure 6). DINOv3 is the exception to this pattern. Although its later layers are also less causally important than its early ones, all-layer adaptation yields classification results similar to or better than early-layer adaptation, and DINOv3 is the only backbone whose classification performance improves after segmentation adaptation. Among the adapted models, it also shows the lowest JS divergence and weight-change ra-tios, the smallest variation across LoRA configurations, the highest mean Dice on all three segmentation datasets, and the best classification performance. This is compatible with the view that a smaller representational shift better preserves the representations that other tasks rely on. The relationship, however, is not deterministic: BiomedCLIP undergoes a substantial attention shift yet remains approximately neutral in classification on OASIS-1. The mechanistic tools are therefore best understood as diagnostics that reveal how a model adapts, rather than as direct predictors of downstream task performance. In our experiments, medical-domain pre-training confers no consistent advantage for segmentation adaptation: after LoRA, DINOv3 achieves the highest and BiomedCLIP the lowest mean Dice on all three segmentation datasets, while MerMED-FM performs on par with SigLIP2. This is con-sistent with the hypothesis that representation quality scales with pre-training data diversity [50], though dataset scale and pre-training objective vary simultaneously, preventing causal attribution to any single factor. Notably, the frozen medical-domain models already attend to anatomically rel-evant regions more than their natural-domain counterparts (Figures 3c and 4), yet this initial advantage does not trans-late into superior adaptation performance, suggesting that where a model attends matters less than the quality of the representations it computes. In classification, the picture dif-fers: frozen MerMED-FM achieves the highest AUC across all three settings, yet this advantage is entirely lost after segmentation adaptation, showing the largest classification drops among the four models. 

Practical guidelines. While our study is limited to four backbones and one imaging domain, the results suggest some considerations for selecting and adapting VFMs for medical imaging. First, backbone selection should not rely on segmentation metrics alone: evaluating on at least one additional task can reveal cross-task degradation invisible to Dice scores. Second, per-layer mechanistic profiles, par-ticularly JS divergence and weight-change ratios, can serve as low-cost diagnostics before out-of-distribution data is available: in our experiments, the backbone with the most 

Diana-Albelda, Kekwick et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 10 of 14 Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms 

uniform and lowest divergence profile (DINOv3) consis-tently exhibited the best generalisation. Third, restricting LoRA to early-to-mid layers might be a conservative de-fault when cross-task robustness matters, as it matched or approached full-layer segmentation for all four backbones while reducing classification degradation in 3/4 of them. Finally, we found no evidence that medical-domain pre-training provides an advantage over natural-domain pre-training for segmentation adaptation, though this compari-son is confounded by dataset scale and should not be taken as a general conclusion. 

Limitations & Future Work. Our study is restricted to the brain MRI domain, to evaluating only four backbone models, to LoRA as the sole PEFT method, and to the ViT-B/16 architecture. In addition, whether the observed patterns generalise to other imaging modalities, adaptation strategies (e.g. adapters, prompt tuning), and larger model sizes (e.g. ViT-L, ViT-H) remains an open question. Segmentation ex-periments use three random seeds and classification probes use three cross-validation folds, which suffices to establish the reported trends but limits the precision of variance esti-mates. Future work would involve extending this framework to additional architectures, scales, imaging domains, and PEFT methods. 

## 7. Conclusion 

We have presented a mechanistic evaluation of four LoRA-adapted vision encoders for brain MRI, combining layer-wise interpretability tools with multi-task downstream assessment across six datasets. Our analysis reveals three key findings: (1) segmentation convergence masks substantial divergence in classification transfer, with medical-domain pre-training conferring no consistent segmentation advan-tage in our setting; (2) the most heavily modified layers are not the causally important ones, with early-to-mid layers consistently identified as sufficient for segmentation recov-ery; and (3) restricting LoRA to these early layers matches or approaches full-layer segmentation while improving clas-sification performance relative to all-layer adaptation in the three backbones whose cross-task performance degrades after adaptation. Together, these findings support a practical protocol for adapting vision encoders in clinical settings: evaluate on multiple tasks to detect cross-task degradation invisible to segmentation metrics, use activation patching to identify causally relevant layers, and adapt preferentially those layers to balance task performance with representational robust-ness. While our study focuses on brain MRI and LoRA, the analytical framework is applicable to any attention-based encoder and PEFT method.
