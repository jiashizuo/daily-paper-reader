Abstract 

Alzheimer’s disease prediction involves irregular visits, heterogeneous measurements and incomplete modalities. This study presents a multimodal multitask framework combining an adapted SFCN MRI encoder, four causal clinical Transformers, shared fusion and task-specific ODE-GRU dynamics. Fine-tuning and LoRA adapt the final two MRI blocks. Task-DRO balances task losses, while Group-CVaR targets cohort and comorbidity strata. Branch-specific input controls, subject-grouped partitions and empirical causality checks support longitudinal evaluation. Across 2,649 subjects and 17,317 visits from ADNI, OASIS-2 and MIRIAD, internal validation yields diagnosis, stage-1 progression and first-stage-1-visit progression AUROCs of 0.935 ±0.002, 0.884 ±0.003 and 0.870 ±0.005, respectively (mean ±SD across three seeds). Corresponding hybrid AUROCs are 0.951, 0.909 and 0.896. Next-visit MMSE mean absolute error (MAE) is 1.61 points; worst-stratum diagnosis AUROC is 0.827 ±0.008. Sampled ADNI explanations identify task-specific input dependence. OASIS-3 external validation yields network and hybrid diagnosis AUROCs of 0.763 and 0.767, hybrid next-visit progression AUROC of 0.764, diagnosis calibration error decreasing from 0.197 to 0.052, and next-visit MMSE MAE of 0.86. Seed-42 paired ablations of six components yield pooled diagnosis and progression AUROC di ff erences between −0.004 and +0.004; removing clinical encoder inputs lowers diagnosis AUROC by 0.272. The framework integrates longitudinal prediction, missing-modality handling, auxiliary comorbidity modelling and subgroup evaluation within a common pipeline. 

Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Multimodal learning, Multitask learning, Progression prediction, Neural ODE, Comorbidity, Leakage control 

1. Introduction 

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and a major cause of dementia. Global dementia burden and its associated societal costs motivate improved diagnosis and prognosis [1, 2]. These estimates concern dementia overall and should not be attributed exclusively to AD. Reliable forecasts of progression may support patient management, clinical trial design and therapeutic research. However, AD progression is highly heterogeneous, influ-enced by genetic factors, lifestyle and comorbidities such as vascular disease, diabetes and depression. Lamar et al. [3] demonstrated that vascular comorbidities significantly a ff ect qualitative error patterns in executive function tests among AD patients, suggesting that vascular pathology modifies the cogni-tive presentation of AD rather than simply accelerating pro-gression. Using latent Dirichlet allocation, Rajamaki et al. [4] identified distinct comorbidity patterns in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease compared to those without, revealing that

certain comorbidities co-occur more frequently in AD patients. Duara and Barker [5] reviewed heterogeneity in AD diagnosis and progression rates, emphasising implications for therapeutic trials. Maciejewska et al. [6] provided a comprehensive re-view of the mechanisms underlying selected comorbidities in Alzheimer’s disease. Moreover, clinical visits occur at irregular intervals, and data modalities (e.g., structural MRI, cognitive tests) are often missing. These challenges render many existing machine learning models impractical for real-world clinical de-cision support. As highlighted by Li et al. [7], building models for longitudinal clinical data faces additional hurdles, including mismatched dimensionality between high-dimensional images and low-dimensional tabular variables, as well as inconsistent intervals in follow-up data. Existing work addresses subsets of these challenges: irregu-lar sampling, shared prediction tasks, comorbidity information and subgroup performance. The proposed model addresses these challenges jointly. 

Irregular sampling and discrete-time assumption. Some fore-casting methods place irregular observations on a fixed grid or require missing-value handling [8]. Continuous-time neural models provide an alternative representation of elapsed time [9, 10, 11, 12]; the experimental protocol therefore compares an ODE formulation with a time-aware discrete GRU.

Disjoint task formulation and static weighting. Diagnosis (cur-rent visit status) and prognosis (future cognitive decline) are typically treated separately, missing the opportunity to learn shared representations that benefit both tasks. Multitask learning has been applied sporadically, but often with fixed, heuristic task weights that ignore varying task di ffi culty, label noise and data availability [13, 14, 15]. Multitask learning has shown promise in clinical settings [16]. Nevertheless, these approaches do not address comorbidity interactions. 

Neglect of comorbidities and subgroup fairness. Comorbidities can influence the clinical presentation and progression of AD [3, 5, 17]. Subgroup-aware learning and distributionally robust optimisation provide tools for examining uneven performance [18, 19, 20]. Recorded-comorbidity strata inform the robust training objective and the subgroup evaluation. 

Temporal leakage and unverifiable explanations. Longitudinal AD benchmarks are especially exposed to information leakage: visits of one subject placed in both training and test sets, features that are imputed backwards from future visits, cognitive scores that are themselves part of the diagnostic definition, or prepro-cessing statistics computed on the whole cohort [21]. Such leaks inflate reported performance and make post-hoc explanations unreliable, because a model that has seen the answer has no reason to rely on disease biology. Few studies test for leakage empirically, and fewer still relate their explanations to known clinical and biological markers of AD. To address these gaps, this study introduces a unified, leakage-controlled framework that integrates 3D structural MRI and longitudinal clinical data with continuous-time dynamics, multitask learning and explicit comorbidity modelling. The main contributions are: 1. Multimodal multitask model for irregular visits. An SFCN brain-age encoder, whose last two blocks are adapted by fine-tuning, low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and an image-level objective, is fused with four task-specific causal clinical Transformers through shared causal attention. Each branch is evolved over irregular visit times with an ODE-GRU. A single model jointly predicts current diagnosis, 3-year stage-1 progression , current and next-visit MMSE, next-visit atrophy, and nine comorbidities, with task balancing by a scale-free, capped Task-DRO. 2. Comorbidity-aware learning and subgroup audit. Nine comorbidities derived from ADNI medical histories are predicted as an auxiliary task under an explicit availability mask; a Group-CVaR penalty targets the worst-performing cohort and comorbidity strata during training; and both primary tasks are evaluated within every comorbidity stra-tum. 3. Partition controls and empirical leakage audits. All visits of a subject stay in one fold; all inputs are computed causally (last observation carried forward only, rates from past visits only); preprocessing statistics are fitted on the training fold; branch-specific prohibited inputs prevent circular use of diagnostic criteria; and three empirical checks (partition, feature causality, model causality) are run for every fold model. 4. Task-specific explanations. Integrated Gradients and group occlusion describe model dependence on available clinical and biological inputs. The explanations are inter-preted in the light of the branch-specific input rules. 5. Systematic evaluation. The evaluation comprises three training seeds, eight paired component and modality ab-lations with subject-level bootstrap tests, calibration anal-ysis, a per-stratum subgroup audit and external validation on the held-out OASIS-3 cohort. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in multimodal AD diagnosis, continuous-time modelling, multitask learning and robust optimisation. Section 3 describes the proposed architecture and training objectives. Section 4 details the datasets, preprocessing pipeline, leakage controls and experimental setup. Section 5 presents the main results, ablations, subgroup analyses and interpretability analyses. Section 6 discusses the implications and limitations, and Section 7 concludes the paper. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Multimodal Diagnosis and Prognosis 

Reviews describe machine-learning and deep-learning ap-proaches to AD diagnosis and conversion prediction [22, 23, 24]. Residual imaging networks, joint prediction of clinical out-comes, multimodal recurrent networks, latent representations and contrastive fusion illustrate distinct modelling strategies [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Recent work includes explainable mul-timodal prediction, longitudinal survival models, clinical–MRI embeddings and cognitive-decline prediction [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 7]. Their outcomes, horizons and validation protocols dif-fer, so published scores are not treated as matched benchmarks here. Bélanger et al. studied dementia identification in home-health assessment records [36]; their OASIS assessment system is distinct from the OASIS neuroimaging cohorts used in this study. 

2.2. Continuous-Time Modelling 

Fixed-grid forecasting has been used in the TADPOLE set-ting [8, 37]. Neural ODEs, latent ODEs, GRU-ODE models and neural controlled di ff erential equations provide representations for irregularly sampled sequences [9, 10, 38, 11, 12]. Applica-tions include longitudinal volumetric modelling and multidimen-sional AD progression models [39, 40]. Sharma et al.’s cLDM-ODE, continuous-time biomarker operators, progression-guided MRI generation and stage-aware mixture-of-experts models ad-dress related forecasting problems [41, 42, 43, 44]. 

2.3. Multitask Learning and Task Balancing 

Uncertainty weighting, GradNorm and clinical time-series benchmarks motivate shared representations and adaptive task 2weights [13, 14, 16]. AD applications include longitudinal pro-gression, correlated cognitive outcomes and task-relationship learning [15, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. Survival analysis provides a complementary formulation [50]. The proposed Task-DRO update adapts the weights to relative losses on top of fixed base weights. 

2.4. Comorbidities and Robust Learning 

Clinical studies and reviews examine vascular e ff ects, co-morbidity patterns and AD heterogeneity [3, 4, 5, 6, 17]. Pro-gression subtypes and longitudinal survival models address patient heterogeneity from complementary perspectives [51, 52]. Group-DRO, healthcare subgroup comparisons and under-represented subgroup fine-tuning motivate robust objectives [18, 19, 20]. Site bias, demographic alignment and population-specific dementia models also highlight transportability con-cerns [53, 54, 55]. The Group-CVaR penalty targets cohort and recorded-comorbidity strata; its e ff ect on the worst strata is assessed directly.

3.5.5. Monotonicity Constraints 

For subjects with a dementia label at any visit, a small hinge penalty Lmono (λmono = 0.01) discourages increases in predicted MMSE and atrophy-target values between consecutive visits, averaged over visit pairs. Here the atrophy targets are volume measures (nWBV or normalised hippocampal volume), so the discouraged direction is an increase in volume, rather than an increase in tissue loss. The soft penalty applies to the current-value estimates of MMSE and the atrophy target. It encodes a preference for non-increasing trajectories rather than a hard biological constraint: upward predictions remain possible, and actual measurements may fluctuate. Its purpose is regularisation, not a claim that every individual follows a strictly monotonic course.

3.6. Network–Tabular Hybrid and Calibration 

Gradient-boosted trees remain very strong on tabular clinical data. The complementary tabular stack is fitted on the training fold only and comprises the mean rank of a class-balanced logis-tic regression and a histogram gradient-boosting classifier using the same causal features as the corresponding network branch (no MRI). 4.2. Preprocessing and Leakage Control 

Figure 2 summarises cohort alignment, subject-level folds, fold-wise transformation fitting and model-ready sequences. 

4.2.1. Imaging 

T1 volumes undergo foreground masking using Otsu thresh-olding and the largest connected component, are resized to 128 3

and intensity-normalised with the statistics of the brain mask of the same volume . No dataset-level statistic is involved. No spatial registration or voxel-spacing resampling is applied: each volume is resized as a whole to the fixed grid, and the encoder operates in native orientation. 

4.2.2. Causal Feature Construction 

Time is measured in years since each subject’s first visit. Sparse longitudinal measures (MMSE, volumes, PET, CSF, cog-nitive tests) are filled with last observation carried forward only ; the first visit is never back-filled, and each filled value is accompanied by an availability flag and the time since its last observation. Rates of change are computed from the two most recent observations at or before the current visit (minimum gap 0.25 years). All remaining medians, scalers, target standardisers and the comorbidity class weights are fitted on the training fold and applied unchanged to the validation fold. Inputs are clipped to ±5 standard deviations. 

4.2.3. Branch-Specific Prohibited Inputs 

Several variables are part of how the target itself is defined and are therefore removed from the corresponding branch (Ta-ble 4). In particular, the diagnosis branch never sees MMSE, any cognitive or functional test, CDR or the current clinical stage, because these assessments can contribute to clinical diagnosis or encode closely related target information. The diagnosis branch therefore uses biological measures, demographics and history. The forecast branch may use cognitive tests and, for ADNI, the current stage (MCI or not), because these are known at the time of the forecast and are not the future outcome. The MMSE and atrophy branches start from the forecast input set and re-move their own targets: the MMSE branch excludes MMSE, its rates and composite scores that contain MMSE, and the atro-phy branch excludes nWBV and hippocampal volume and their rates. Under the evaluated configuration, both therefore also re-ceive current clinical-stage information for ADNI (MCI or not), which is part of the clinical diagnostic assessment, together with the remaining cognitive tests and regional volumes. The stage indicator is set only for cohorts with a clinical diagnosis and is never derived from CDR, so OASIS-2 stage is not an input. CDR

4.2.4. Cross-Validation 

Internal evaluation uses 5-fold StratifiedGroupKFold 

grouped by subject (split seed 42): every visit and every MRI file of a subject belongs to exactly one fold. In each fold the model is trained on four-fifths of the subjects and evaluated on the held-out fifth, which also drives early stopping, the conversion threshold, the temperature and the hybrid weights. The resulting metrics are therefore internal-validation estimates. The subject-bootstrap intervals condition on the saved predictions. Mini-batches are balanced across cohorts. 

4.2.5. Empirical Leakage Checks 

Three checks are run for every fold model and written to a fold-level report: (P1) partition : zero subjects and zero MRI files are shared between training and validation, and no label-like column or prohibited input reaches any branch; (P2) feature causality : for 60 random subjects, every derived feature at visit 

k is recomputed after deleting all visits after k and must be identical; (P3) model causality : for 40 random subjects, every model output at visit ≤ k must be unchanged (tolerance 10 −3)when visits > k are deleted. 

4.3. Training Details 

Training uses AdamW (weight decay 5 × 10 −4) with di ff erentiated learning rates: Transformers and fusion 5 × 10 −5,classification heads 1 × 10 −4, regression heads 3 × 10 −4, MMSE head 3 × 10 −5, ODE-GRU 1 × 10 −4, LoRA adapters 1 × 10 −4

and the fine-tuned SFCN blocks 1 × 10 −5. The batch size is 8 subjects with gradient accumulation over 2 steps, automatic mixed precision (ODE integration in float32), gradient-norm clipping at 0.5 and a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler. Training runs for at most 25 epochs with early stopping (patience 5, minimum 7 epochs) on a validation selection score 0.35 AUC dx + 0.45 AUC conv + 0.10 S MMSE + 0.10 AUC com ,where S MMSE = clip(1 − MAE next /MAE persist , 0, 1) compares the next-visit MMSE error with that of carrying the current value forward. The latent dimension is 128 throughout. The remaining hyperparameters are γ = 0.5, qmax = 0.3, 

α = 0.7, λcvar = 0.1, λmono = 0.01, λcon = 0.05 ( κ = 0.1), 

λdrift = 10 −3, λimg = 0.2, LoRA r = 8, αLoRA = 16. The full model is trained with three seeds (42, 123, 2026) on the same folds. 5.2. External Validation on OASIS-3 

OASIS-3 was excluded from model development and re-served for external validation after the model, fold preproces-sors, MMSE and atrophy scalers, tabular baselines and temper-ature scalers were frozen on the internal folds. The five fold models trained on ADNI, OASIS-2 and MIRIAD were applied unchanged to every OASIS-3 visit; no OASIS-3 label, image or statistic entered fitting, threshold selection, calibration or hybrid weighting. An attribute-based overlap screen was ap-plied to the OASIS-3 candidate pool before model evaluation to reduce potential participant overlap with OASIS-2. A subject was flagged when sex, education and handedness matched an OASIS-2 participant, the OASIS-3 baseline age exceeded the OASIS-2 baseline age by 3–20 years, the absolute di ff erence in estimated total intracranial volume was at most 30,000 mm 3, and the OASIS-3 baseline age was at least 60. The screen excluded 135 participants; the frozen exclusion list is reused by the build script, which asserts that no excluded subject appears in the prepared cohort. One T1 was indexed per MRI session, and each session was linked to the nearest UDS visit within ±270 days, a matching window that permits imaging and clinical assessments to occur months apart. The prepared cohort contains 1,152 sub-jects and 2,407 visits (1,995 diagnosis eligible, 707 progression eligible, 980 comorbidity eligible); every prepared visit carries a T1, so the MRI encoder was evaluated on all records. Within each seed, every fold model is applied with its own training-fold preprocessing, tabular stack and hybrid weight; each fold’s saved temperature is applied to its own predictions, and raw and calibrated predictions are then averaged per subject–visit across the five fold models, with the decision threshold set to the mean of the fold thresholds. The primary external result is the per-visit average of the three seeds’ predictions, computed before any metric is evaluated. External progression is evaluated at the next observed visit, not at a fixed 3-year horizon. The external analysis evaluates next-visit transfer separately from the internal 3-year endpoint. Table 6 summarises the external results. The diagnosis AU-ROC is 0.763 for the network and 0.767 for the hybrid. For next-observed-visit progression, the network achieves 0.733 and the hybrid 0.764, compared with 0.710 for logistic regression. The tabular stack achieves a progression AUROC of 0.768, while logistic regression achieves the highest diagnosis point estimate (0.787). Transferred temperature scaling reduces diagnosis ECE from 0.197 to 0.052; progression ECE changes from 0.401 to 0.303. Next-visit MMSE prediction achieves an MAE of 0.86 and R2 = 0.24, and current MMSE estimation achieves an MAE of 1.80 and R2 = −0.03. Comorbidity prediction was evaluated externally for eight of the nine conditions (hypertension, dia-betes, depression, hyperlipidaemia, arthritis, cancer, coronary artery disease and stroke); COPD was not evaluable because none of the 980 OASIS-3 subjects with comorbidity records was COPD-positive. The mean comorbidity AUROC across these eight conditions is 0.565. These results extend the evaluation to a separate cohort across diagnostic, progression, cognitive and auxiliary outcomes. External atrophy prediction is outside the present evaluation.

5.9. Subgroup Robustness 

Table 11 and Figure 5 evaluate diagnosis and conversion within each of the nine comorbidity strata (ADNI only, since comorbidity status is unknown in OASIS-2 and MIRIAD). Diag-nosis ranges from 0.819 (COPD) to 0.954 (cancer); conversion is more sensitive, with its worst stratum in stroke (0.765; 24 subjects with at least one positive conversion label across 74 positive MCI visits), about 0.12 below the pooled value, identi-fying stroke as a priority subgroup for further evaluation. The COPD stratum contains only 5 subjects with at least one posi-tive conversion label across 15 positive MCI visits (conversion AUROC 0.785), and its estimates are correspondingly uncer-tain. Without Group-CVaR, the worst diagnosis stratum is 0.828 (COPD) and the worst conversion stratum 0.768 (stroke), against 0.819 and 0.765 with it; the mean of the three worst strata and the best–worst disparity are also similar. 

5.10. Interpretability 

Network input dependence is assessed with Integrated Gra-dients (IG) [63] and group occlusion. Network explanations use the first batches of each validation loader, capped at 250 subjects (the batch boundary can extend this to 256). This subset comprises ADNI participants without raw MRI. The network explanations therefore describe a sampled ADNI tabular popu-lation rather than all three cohorts. MRI embeddings are cached and held fixed during IG, which is computed for diagnosis and forecast input tensors relative to a zero-input baseline. The scalar target is the sum of eligible-output logit contrasts; the attribution describes aggregate model dependence and is not a causal e ff ect or an explanation of one selected visit. Progression explanations include eligible progression-labelled visits and are not restricted to the MCI population used for the primary conversion metric. 

5.10.1. Group Occlusion 

Occlusion measures how much AUROC is lost when a whole input group is removed (set to “never measured”), which ex-amines joint dependence on a feature group but can introduce out-of-distribution missingness and remains sensitive to corre-lated groups (Figure 6). For diagnosis , removing the structural MRI volumes costs 0.099 ±0.027 AUROC (mean ±SD over folds) and removing PET /CSF biomarkers 0.022 ±0.011. For conver-sion , removing cognitive tests costs 0.062 ±0.018, PET /CSF 0.020 ±0.011 and structural volumes 0.016 ±0.014. The raw-MRI perturbation is not informative in this subset because these subjects lack raw MRI; it is omitted from the figure. The two methods assess di ff erent forms of input dependence. Network occlusion replaces a group by its missing-input representation, whereas tabular permutation disrupts its correspondence with the outcome. A negative bar, such as family history in the network progression panel, means that AUROC increased after pertur-bation. It does not imply that family history protects against disease. This pattern can reflect correlated inputs, sampling vari-ability or perturbation sensitivity. Fold SD describes variability, not a confidence interval or a significance test. 

5.10.2. Top Features 

The top-ranked individual features include structural, metabolic and fluid measures associated with AD (Figure 7). For diagnosis , the ranking is led by hippocampal and entorhinal volume and FDG-PET, followed by ventricular and middle-temporal volume, amyloid PET (AV45), whole-brain volume, CSF A β42 , p-tau and t-tau, and fusiform volume. These are the established medial-temporal, metabolic and amyloid /tau markers of AD. For conversion , the ranking is led by the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), followed by hippocampal volume, FDG-PET, ADAS-13, AV45, middle-temporal volume, CSF A β42 , MMSE and RAVLT immediate recall, i.e. early loss of instrumental activities of daily living and episodic memory, supported by neurodegeneration and amyloid markers. Attribution magnitudes identify the inputs the model relies on, not causal e ff ects. 

6. Discussion 

6.1. Main Findings 

A single leakage-controlled model jointly performs diagno-sis, conversion forecasting, cognitive and atrophy prediction, and comorbidity prediction, with diagnosis and stage-1 progres-sion AUROCs of 0.935 and 0.884 across three seeds and low seed variation. The sampled ADNI tabular explanations identify dependence on features associated with AD: diagnosis relies on hippocampal and entorhinal volume, FDG-PET and amyloid and tau markers, whereas conversion relies on functional decline (FAQ) and episodic memory, supported by the same biomarkers. Part of this division of evidence is imposed by the branch-specific input rules, and the attributions describe model associa-tions rather than causal mechanisms. The contribution of this work is the implemented multimodal multitask framework and its evaluation: leakage-controlled inte-gration of heterogeneous cohorts, tasks and modalities, comor-bidity prediction without condition-defining records, stratified subgroup analysis and external validation. 6.2. Continuous-Time Modelling 

With elapsed time entering the GRU gates in both tempo-ral models, no statistically detectable AUROC di ff erence was observed between ODE integration and a time-aware GRU in this configuration. The ODE additionally provides a latent state defined at any time between visits, which supports future work on forecasting at chosen horizons. 

6.3. Comorbidity and Modality Contributions 

Auxiliary comorbidity modelling and subgroup evaluation provide complementary views of the clinical setting. The head predicts nine recorded conditions without receiving their defin-ing medical-history, medication or laboratory records, achieving a mean AUROC of 0.571 over three seeds. Recorded condi-tions also define training strata for Group-CVaR and support condition-specific diagnosis and progression analyses. This de-sign integrates auxiliary supervision and subgroup assessment without using recorded conditions as direct predictive inputs. With and without Group-CVaR, the worst diagnosis and conver-sion strata were similar (0.819 vs. 0.828; 0.765 vs. 0.768), and conversion in patients with a history of stroke was the lowest-performing stratum. The sampled ADNI explanations identify dependence on derived imaging volumes, PET /CSF biomarkers and permitted clinical features. The imaging-focused comparator achieves di-agnosis AUROCs of 0.597 on MIRIAD and 0.621 on OASIS-2, compared with 0.424 and 0.629 for the fused network. Together, the cohort and modality analyses characterise how performance varies with input availability and population. The tabular attribu-tion analysis covers ADNI subjects without raw MRI; individual raw-image localisation is outside its scope. 

6.4. Limitations and Future Work 

The validation folds also guide model selection, threshold and temperature fitting and hybrid weighting, so internal esti-mates may be optimistic; external evaluation on OASIS-3 pro-vides an independent assessment. The binary diagnosis and pro-gression endpoints are cohort-defined, and external progression uses the next observed visit. Comorbidity labels are rule-derived from medical histories without independent clinical adjudica-tion. On the internal folds, the tabular stack alone exceeds the network AUROC. Raw MRI is not available for ADNI, and vol-umes are processed without anatomical registration. Ablations use a single seed. Future work will strengthen the image path-way, harmonise comorbidity labels against clinical records and extend external evaluation to cohorts with matched endpoints. 

7. Conclusion 

This study presents an integrated framework for cohort-defined diagnosis, longitudinal progression, cognitive and vol-ume prediction, and auxiliary comorbidity modelling under missing modalities and irregular observation times. Its con-tribution combines an adapted SFCN encoder, causal clinical streams, task-aware fusion, ODE-GRU dynamics, task balanc-ing, subgroup objectives and explicit input controls in a common pipeline. Three-seed internal validation achieves mean diagnosis and stage-1 progression AUROCs of 0.935 and 0.884, increas-ing to 0.951 and 0.909 with the tabular hybrid.
