Visual decoding from fMRI is typically siloed by participant and experiment, obscuring whether heterogeneous neural
measurements can be organized within a common computational geometry.
Here we introduce BRAID-fMRI (Brain
Representation Alignment across Individuals and Datasets), a shared CLIP-supervised decoding framework. BRAID-fMRI
uses a single ROI-wise Transformer with optional participant conditioning across eight visual-fMRI datasets comprising
93 dataset-specific participant entries, 430,007 single-trial responses and 162,839 unique stimuli. Regional brain activity
is aligned with 512-dimensional CLIP ViT-B/32 representations using a multi-positive contrastive objective that treats re-
peated stimuli across participants and datasets as positives. BRAID-fMRI supports retrieval across seven evaluation datasets.
On eight matched participant entries, it achieves 35.0 ± 11.1% Top-10 accuracy, exceeding the observed mean accuracy of
the two evaluated baselines—the MindEye-style pooled-CLIP decoder (27.1 ± 5.1%) and ridge regression (21.1 ± 9.1%)—and
attaining the highest observed accuracy for seven of eight entries. In separately trained participant-agnostic models, expand-
ing the source pool increased target-dataset holdout accuracy by up to 92.1% relative to the initial source-training condition.
The learned space preserves graded semantic structure, while ablations and saliency highlight ventral and early visual cortex
and category-specific motion and attentional systems. These results support scalable cross-dataset decoding into a common
CLIP-aligned space, with model sensitivity concentrated in ventral and early visual inputs.

Introduction
Modern visual neuroscience faces a paradox: fMRI datasets are becoming larger and visual decoders in-
creasingly powerful, yet the resulting models remain largely confined to the participants and experiments
on which they were trained. Individual studies differ in participants, scanners, acquisition protocols, exper-
imental tasks, stimulus distributions and sampling density. Consequently, visual decoders are commonly
developed for one participant or one dataset at a time, yielding highly optimized but fragmented models that
are difficult to compare, scale or transfer across studies. This fragmentation leaves a fundamental question
unresolved: can heterogeneous neural measurements support a common visual representational geometry that
transfers to participants and experiments never encountered during training? A related challenge is to de-
termine whether such commonality reflects structure preserved in the neural representations themselves,
rather than simply their alignment to the same pretrained visual target space.
Visual decoding from fMRI has progressed substantially over the past two decades. Early encoding-based
approaches demonstrated that activity in visual cortex could identify previously unseen natural images [1],
while subsequent work used deep neural representations and generative priors to reconstruct increasingly
complex visual content from brain activity [2, 3]. More recently, large vision–language models such as CLIP
[4] have provided a semantically structured target space in which neural responses can be aligned with
natural images, enabling high-accuracy retrieval and reconstruction [5, 6, 7]. Voxelwise encoding studies
additionally show that CLIP features predict held-out responses in high-level visual cortex [8], supporting
their use as a shared visual target without establishing that their distances reproduce native cortical ge-
ometry. Large, densely sampled resources such as the Natural Scenes Dataset have been central to this
progress [9]. However, much of the strongest performance has been obtained by fitting or adapting map-
pings to extensively sampled participants within a single experimental setting. Recent approaches have
begun to share information across participants. MindEye2, for example, pretrains across participants be-
fore adapting to a new individual [6], but such transfer has largely involved participant-specific adaptation
within a common dataset and acquisition regime. Other recent work has extended this direction across
datasets: NeuroFusion jointly learns across datasets and also evaluates leave-one-dataset-out transfer with

A complementary line of work has shown that neural representations themselves contain structure
shared across individuals. Hyperalignment identifies high-dimensional transformations that place participant-
specific cortical responses into a common representational space [15], while shared-response models
explicitly factor multi-participant fMRI data into individual mappings and a shared latent response [16].
Representational similarity analysis provides a complementary framework for comparing the geometry of
neural and computational representations independently of their native coordinate systems [17, 18]. Recent
work has gone further, showing that independently learned participant-specific fMRI spaces within NSD
can exhibit approximately isometric geometry and be related through unsupervised orthogonal transforma-
tions [19]. Those spaces were learned from repeated neural measurements without visual-model targets;
here, geometry is evaluated after supervised alignment to CLIP. Together, these studies establish that com-
mon structure can be recovered across people under coordinated experimental conditions. Cross-dataset
learning poses a more stringent problem: inter-individual variability is compounded by differences in scan-
ner hardware, spatial and temporal resolution, experimental design, stimulus statistics, and the amount of
data available for each participant. It therefore remains unclear whether a common relational geometry
persists when representations are learned jointly across independently acquired datasets, and whether that
geometry can support direct transfer to participants and experiments absent from training.
MOSAIC makes it possible to test this question at a substantially broader scale [20]. The resource harmo-
nizes eight independently collected visual-fMRI datasets: the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), THINGS-fMRI
(THINGS), Deeprecon, BOLD5000, the BOLD Moments Dataset (BMD), Generic Object Decoding (GOD), the
Human Actions Dataset (HAD) and the Natural Object Dataset (NOD). MOSAIC comprises 93 dataset-specific
participant entries from 77 individuals, 430,007 single-trial fMRI–stimulus pairs and 162,839 unique stimuli.
Data are uniformly reprocessed with fMRIPrep, registered to the fsLR32k cortical surface and converted to
single-trial beta estimates with GLMsingle, with curated splits preventing duplicate or perceptually similar
train–test stimuli. These datasets sample different portions of both the human population and the visual
world. MOSAIC itself establishes a framework for aggregating these datasets and demonstrates the value of
multi-dataset training for brain encoding. Here we use this harmonized resource to address a distinct de-
coding question: whether neural responses from all eight datasets can be mapped by one shared model into
the same visual-semantic coordinate system, whether that mapping transfers without target-participant
calibration, and whether its internal brain-space geometry is shared across datasets. Rather than treating
this heterogeneity solely as nuisance variation to be removed, we ask whether it can provide complementary
training signal from which a representation transferable across both datasets and individuals can emerge.
We address this question with a single ROI-wise brain-to-CLIP model trained jointly across all eight
datasets. Anatomically corresponding regions of interest are mapped into a common token space using
ROI-specific encoders, augmented with learned ROI embeddings and, when used, participant embeddings,
and integrated by a Transformer shared across every participant and dataset [21] (Fig. 1). Attention pool-
ing produces a brain representation projected into the frozen 512-dimensional image-embedding space of
CLIP ViT-B/32 [4]. A symmetric multi-positive contrastive objective allows repeated observations of the
same stimulus, including repetitions across participants or datasets, to reinforce a common representation
rather than act as false negatives. Learned participant embeddings provide a lightweight mechanism for
accommodating participant-specific variation when identity is available, paralleling the participant align-
ment or adaptation used in previous cross-participant decoders [22, 6]. Crucially, participant conditioning
is optional; disabling participant embeddings provides a participant-agnostic shared encoder that can be
evaluated directly on participants and datasets absent from training. This design therefore separates two
questions that are often conflated: whether participant-specific conditioning can accommodate individual
variation when identity is known, and whether the shared representation itself is sufficiently invariant to
operate without such conditioning.
We find that a single model supports stimulus retrieval across seven evaluation datasets and, across
eight matched participants, performs best for seven relative to Ridge regression [23] and a MindEye-style
retrieval baseline. Following Benchetrit et al. [14], this MindEye-style comparison predicts a single pooled
CLIP embedding for retrieval, without the diffusion prior or image reconstruction used in the full MindEye
framework [5]. Separately trained participant-agnostic models also support target-dataset holdout retrieval
without fitting participant embeddings. Expanding the source pretraining pool was associated with relative
gains of up to 92.1% in target-dataset holdout accuracy; this comparison jointly changes dataset diversity
and the amount of training data. Within the CLIP-supervised prediction space, representations exhibit a
graded hierarchy from stimulus identity to semantic relatedness, and category-level geometry shows corre-
spondence among the evaluable dataset pairs. Functional-network ablations and ROI-level saliency further

indexed decoder across heterogeneous visual-fMRI datasets: matched-participant retrieval, target-dataset

anatomical sensitivity within the CLIP-supervised prediction space.
Results
A shared cross-dataset encoder combines anatomical correspondence and optional participant
conditioning
We trained BRAID-fMRI, a single model using the naturalistic MOSAIC training split, pooling single-trial
responses across all eight source datasets and all dataset-specific participant entries. Brain activity was
represented in a common atlas space using 379 cortical and subcortical regions of interest (ROIs). Each
ROI contains a variable number of cortical vertices or subcortical voxels; therefore, a separate two-layer
tokenizer mapped each ROI vector into a common 512-dimensional token. A learned ROI identity embedding
encoded anatomical correspondence, whereas a learned participant embedding was broadcast across all
ROI tokens from the same dataset-specific participant. The resulting tokens were processed by a four-layer
Transformer whose parameters were shared across all datasets and participants. Attention pooling and a
projection head produced a 512-dimensional prediction in the image space of the frozen CLIP ViT-B/32
encoder (Fig. 1).
This construction provides separate parameter components for ROI identity and participant condition-
ing. ROI-specific tokenizers accommodate differences in regional dimensionality, while participant embed-
dings provide a lightweight participant-dependent shift of the common token space. The shared Trans-
former must nevertheless learn interactions among anatomically corresponding regions that are useful
across the complete training collection. Repeated presentations and stimuli shared between participants or
datasets were handled by a multi-positive InfoNCE objective, preventing valid stimulus matches from being
incorrectly treated as negatives. We use participant conditioning when evaluating held-out stimuli from par-
ticipant entries represented during training; in the zero-shot experiments below, participant embeddings
are disabled entirely to isolate transfer supported by the shared encoder.
One model retrieves visual stimuli across datasets and participants
We first asked whether a single model trained jointly across heterogeneous datasets and participants could
identify held-out visual stimuli from fMRI. For each query, the predicted brain embedding was compared by
cosine similarity with precomputed CLIP ViT-B/32 image embeddings, and unique candidate stimuli were
ranked accordingly. In the most stringent evaluation, the candidate pool comprised all unique validation
stimuli across the seven evaluation datasets. Dataset-level unique-stimulus Top-10 accuracy reached 38.1%
for BMD, 21.7% for NSD, 8.7% for THINGS, 8.6% for GOD, 6.6% for BOLD5000, 5.6% for Deeprecon and 0.3%
for NOD, compared with a global chance level of 0.08% (Fig. 2a). Observed accuracy exceeded the global
random-ranking reference across datasets, despite differences in their stimulus distributions and numbers
of evaluation queries. Participant-level performance also varied within datasets, indicating heterogeneity
in decoding difficulty across individuals and experimental settings (Fig. 2b).
We next restricted the candidate pool to unique validation stimuli from the query participant’s dataset.
This dataset-restricted evaluation generally increased retrieval accuracy, particularly for GOD, BOLD5000,
Deeprecon and THINGS (Fig. 2c). Because the number of candidate stimuli differed across datasets, the cor-
responding Top-10 chance levels also varied. The improvement relative to the global setting indicates that
part of the retrieval challenge arises from discriminating among stimuli drawn from multiple experiments
and visual domains.
Accuracy above global random-ranking chance does not by itself exclude dataset-
specific cues: a predictor that identifies the source dataset can already restrict the plausible candidate
set. Stimulus discrimination therefore also needs to be evaluated against the corresponding restricted-
candidate reference.
We then compared BRAID-fMRI with Ridge regression and a MindEye-style pooled-CLIP decoder using
the eight participants for whom matched results were available in BOLD5000 and NSD. Across this subset,
BRAID-fMRI achieved a mean Top-10 accuracy of 35.0%, compared with 27.1% for MindEye and 21.1% for
Ridge regression (Table 1). It achieved the highest participant-level accuracy in seven of the eight compar-
isons and the highest dataset-level mean for both BOLD5000 and NSD. Thus, jointly learning from heteroge-
neous datasets did not require sacrificing participant-level performance and, under the matched evaluation
considered here, generally outperformed established participant-specific baselines. A broader compari-
son against Ridge regression across 63 dataset–participant entries is provided in Supplementary Table S2;

The participant-agnostic encoder supports transfer in target-dataset holdout evaluations
We next asked whether cross-dataset transfer depended on learned participant identities. For these exper-
iments, participant embeddings were disabled throughout training and evaluation. Each target dataset—
including all of its participants and stimuli—was excluded from training, while the remaining source pool
was expanded cumulatively by adding datasets and their selected participants. Thus, no target-participant
embedding or other target-specific decoder parameter was fitted.
In this participant-agnostic setting, the final curriculum condition exceeded the initial condition for all
seven targets (Fig. 3g). Relative gains from the first to the final step were 92.1% for NSD, 69.0% for BMD,
60.9% for BOLD5000, 32.0% for NOD, 25.3% for THINGS, 11.7% for Deeprecon and 10.9% for GOD. Individual
curriculum steps were not uniformly beneficial, indicating that source datasets differ in their relationship to
a given target. Nevertheless, the positive final gain for every target shows that the shared encoder captures
cross-participant and cross-dataset structure that can transfer without participant-specific conditioning,
with a positive final relative gain in each of the seven reported target comparisons.
Retrieval performance follows visual-semantic organization
The large, heterogeneous candidate pool enabled us to ask whether retrieval performance was structured
by stimulus semantics. We clustered unique validation stimuli by applying k-means (k = 40) to their ℓ2-
normalized CLIP embeddings, using t-SNE only to visualize the resulting organization. The clusters formed
recognizable semantic groups, including people and portraits, tennis actions, marine life, consumer elec-
tronics, trains, rural scenes and architecture (Fig. 3a). Descriptive cluster names were assigned manually
after inspecting representative stimuli from each cluster and played no role in the clustering procedure.
Most selected clusters contained stimuli contributed by multiple datasets, although NOD supplied a large
fraction of many categories because of its broad stimulus set (Fig. 3b). Note that cluster IDs (C0–C39) are
arbitrary k-means labels and do not encode any particular ordering. A complete atlas of all 40 clusters,
together with their retrieval performance, size and cross-dataset composition, is shown in Supplementary
Fig. S1.
Retrieval differed markedly across clusters. People and portraits achieved the highest Top-10 accuracy,
followed by several coherent categories including tennis actions and marine life, whereas home appliances
and some architectural categories were among the most difficult (Fig. 3c). High- and low-performing groups
occurred across a range of stimulus counts and dataset compositions (Supplementary Fig. S1). To avoid se-
lecting categories after inspecting their content, we ranked all 40 clusters by retrieval accuracy and retained
every fourth cluster, yielding ten categories at ranks 1, 5, 9, …, 37. This performance-stratified sampling
scheme spans the observed accuracy range and provides an illustrative subset for subsequent geometric
and neuroanatomical analyses.

The learned space preserves graded semantics and category-specific cortical structure
A successful retrieval model could in principle separate individual stimuli without preserving relationships
among them. We therefore compared the cosine similarity between each predicted brain embedding and
three classes of CLIP targets: its matched stimulus, a non-matching stimulus from the same semantic cluster
and a stimulus from a different cluster. Across participants, matched pairs were more similar than same-
cluster non-matches by a mean margin of 0.094 (P = 6.4 × 10−12). Same-cluster non-matches were, in
turn, more similar than different-cluster stimuli by a mean margin of 0.043 (P = 3.9 × 10−10; Fig. 4a). This
ordering was visible across the seven evaluation datasets and across the representative semantic clusters,
although the magnitude of the margins varied by dataset and category (Fig. 4b,c). The model therefore
preserves both stimulus identity and broader semantic neighbourhood structure. The same analysis across
all 40 semantic clusters showed substantial variation in the strength of both margins while preserving the
broader hierarchical pattern (Supplementary Fig. S2b).
We next examined how this geometry was expressed across cortical systems.
For each query, ROI
saliency was defined as the absolute gradient-times-input contribution to matched brain–CLIP cosine simi-
larity. Cluster selectivity contrasted an ROI’s mean saliency for one semantic cluster with its mean saliency
for all other clusters, standardized by pooled variability. Cluster-associated beta maps were spatially dis-
tributed and differed systematically among categories (Fig. 4d). People and portraits, tennis actions, marine
life, consumer electronics and scene-related categories emphasized partially distinct patterns across occip-
ital, ventral temporal, parietal and motion-sensitive cortex.
To distinguish selectivity from differences in network size, we compared the number of top positively se-
lective ROIs in each network with the count expected from that network’s share of all ROIs. Early visual and
ventral visual/object systems were enriched across several clusters, whereas the balance of network con-
tributions varied substantially across semantic content (Fig. 4e; Supplementary Fig. S2c). Cluster-specific
ablations provided complementary functional evidence (Fig. 4f). People and portraits were most impaired
by ventral visual/object ablation (−8.0 percentage points). Tennis actions depended jointly on ventral vi-
sual/object (−8.7), parietal attention (−8.0) and early visual regions (−6.7), whereas trains and rail trans-
port were particularly sensitive to early visual ablation (−8.2). Thus, the trained decoder shows broadly
shared visual-input dependence and category-associated variation in masking effects, although category
and dataset composition are not disentangled here.
Shared semantic geometry persists across datasets and retrieval difficulty
The preceding analyses showed that the model preserves semantic structure relative to CLIP and recruits
partly distinct cortical systems across stimulus categories. We next asked whether CLIP-supervised brain
predictions preserve semantic relationships across datasets and remain informative as retrieval becomes
progressively more difficult.
We first compared pairs of predicted brain embeddings without using CLIP vectors in the pairwise simi-
larity calculation. Across participant pairs, representations of the same stimulus were most similar, followed
by representations of different stimuli from the same semantic cluster, whereas stimuli from different clus-
ters were least similar (Fig. 5a). Participant pairs drawn both within and across datasets contributed to this
graded organization, indicating that the hierarchy from stimulus identity to broader semantic relatedness
is not confined to a single experimental setting.

distances among semantic-cluster centroids and compared these geometries between datasets. The five
evaluable dataset pairs, involving NSD, THINGS, BOLD5000 and NOD, showed positive correspondence af-
ter multiple-comparison correction, with Spearman correlations ranging from 0.61 to 0.88 (Fig. 5b). Thus,

We next quantified how retrieval scales as the candidate space expands. Using each query’s rank in
the full candidate set, we computed the exact expected Top-10 accuracy for progressively larger candidate
pools. Performance declined as the number of competing stimuli increased, but the across-participant
trajectory remained above random Top-10 chance throughout the tested range (Fig. 5c). Dataset-specific
trajectories differed substantially, with some datasets retaining strong retrieval even in large search spaces.
These results show that the learned ranking structure remains informative as the discrimination problem
becomes increasingly demanding.
We additionally tested retrieval with distractors from the target’s CLIP-derived semantic cluster rather
than arbitrary distractors. We therefore progressively increased the fraction of candidate distractors drawn
from the target’s own CLIP-derived semantic cluster while holding candidate-pool size fixed. Expected
9


Top-10 accuracy declined only moderately at the population level and remained well above chance even
when all distractors were drawn from the same semantic cluster (Fig. 5d). Small non-monotonic changes
in individual datasets are expected because same-cluster membership constrains semantic relatedness but
does not guarantee greater difficulty for every individual query. Retrieval therefore cannot be explained
solely by coarse category identification: the learned representation retains information that discriminates
individual stimuli within semantic neighbourhoods.
Finally, we asked whether local relationships among unique stimuli were preserved between brain and
CLIP space. Across neighbourhood sizes from k = 5 to k = 250, the overlap between brain-space and CLIP-
space nearest neighbours consistently exceeded the analytical expectation under random neighbourhood
correspondence (Fig. 5e). Absolute overlap remained modest, indicating partial preservation rather than
replication of CLIP’s local topology. Nevertheless, its systematic elevation above chance shows that stimuli
occupying nearby locations in CLIP space also tend to remain neighbours in the learned brain representa-
tion.
Together, these analyses show that the learned representation supports substantially more than point-
wise brain–image alignment. Its CLIP-supervised predictions preserve graded semantic relationships across
participant entries and relational correspondence among the evaluable datasets, remains informative under
large and semantically challenging candidate spaces, and partially preserves the local organization of the
target visual-semantic space.
Discussion
By jointly modelling eight visual-fMRI datasets, we show that heterogeneous neural measurements can be or-
ganized within a common visual-semantic representation despite large differences in participants, stimulus
distributions and acquisition protocols. A single model retrieved held-out stimuli across seven evaluation
datasets and outperformed matched participant-specific baselines for seven of eight participants. More
importantly, the shared encoder did not require learned participant identities to generalize: participant-
agnostic models supported target-dataset holdout retrieval, with the final source-pool condition improving
accuracy by up to 92.1% relative to the initial condition. The learned representation was also structured be-
yond retrieval accuracy; stimulus identity and semantic relatedness were reflected directly in brain–brain
geometry, semantic organization was conserved across datasets, and retrieval remained robust as both
the candidate space and prevalence of semantic hard negatives increased. Together, these findings sup-
port reusable mappings from heterogeneous visual-fMRI measurements into a shared supervised prediction
space.
A central design principle is to provide participant-specific conditioning while sharing the remaining
model parameters. Why retain participant conditioning if the encoder can transfer without it? Anatomical
harmonization does not eliminate inter-individual differences in functional organization and measurement
characteristics, and previous cross-participant decoders have therefore used explicit participant alignment
or participant-specific adaptation to map individual responses into shared spaces [22, 6]. Here, participant
embeddings provide a considerably lighter mechanism: a single learned vector can modulate all ROI to-
kens for a known participant while the ROI tokenizers, Transformer, pooling and projection remain shared.
Their role is therefore to provide capacity for participant-specific variation, not to define the common rep-
resentation itself. The zero-shot results make this distinction explicit: models trained without participant
embeddings still support target-dataset holdout retrieval.
The zero-shot curriculum analysis further indicates that cross-dataset heterogeneity can be a source
of transferable information rather than merely a nuisance to be removed. With participant embeddings
disabled, every target dataset and all of its participants remained unseen throughout training, while the
source pool was progressively expanded. The final, more diverse pretraining condition outperformed the
initial condition for all seven targets, although individual curriculum steps were not uniformly beneficial.
This non-monotonicity is informative: additional neural data are not interchangeable simply because they
increase sample size, and their utility depends on relationships among source and target stimulus distribu-
tions, participants and acquisition regimes. Nevertheless, the uniformly positive final gains demonstrate that
broader pretraining can expose regularities that transfer directly to unseen participants and experiments.
Controlled scaling experiments that independently manipulate numbers of trials, participants, unique stim-
uli and datasets will be important for determining which forms of diversity contribute most strongly to this
transfer.

pointwise brain–image associations. Brain embeddings corresponding to the same stimulus were most sim-
ilar, embeddings from different stimuli within the same semantic cluster occupied an intermediate regime,
and embeddings from unrelated clusters were least similar. Importantly, this ordering persisted for com-
parisons across datasets. At a broader scale, semantic representational geometries showed substantial cor-

The retrieval stress tests reinforce this interpretation. Accuracy decreased as the candidate pool ex-
panded over several orders of magnitude, as expected, but remained above the corresponding random
baseline throughout the tested range. More importantly, replacing increasingly large fractions of the can-
didate set with same-cluster distractors produced only a moderate population-level decline, with retrieval
remaining well above chance even when the search space was dominated by semantically related alterna-
tives. Thus, the representation cannot be explained solely by coarse category information; it retains infor-
mation that distinguishes individual stimuli within semantic neighbourhoods. Consistent with this result,
nearest-neighbour structure in brain space overlapped with that of CLIP more than expected by random cor-
respondence across neighbourhood scales. The absolute overlap was modest, however, indicating partial
preservation rather than a wholesale reproduction of CLIP’s local topology. Together, these results support
partial preservation of meaningful visual-semantic neighbourhood structure from CLIP, while the incom-

plete overlap leaves room for prediction error, regularization, measurement noise, and potentially distinct
representational structure.
The neuroanatomical analyses further ground this shared geometry in recognizable cortical organization.
Early and ventral visual systems formed the most consistent backbone across datasets and participants,
whereas motion and parietal-attention systems contributed more selectively across stimulus categories.
Cluster-specific response maps, saliency and ablation therefore point to a representation that is both shared
and compositional; a common set of visual systems supports decoding broadly, while the relative contribu-
tion of additional networks changes with visual content. The convergence between saliency and ablation is
particularly useful because the two analyses probe different aspects of the model. Gradient-based saliency
identifies inputs to which an individual prediction is locally sensitive, whereas ablation measures the loss
of retrieval performance when an entire network is removed. Their agreement is consistent with category-
associated input dependence in the fitted mapping, but the two analyses share the same model and data
and do not independently establish biological category selectivity.
Several limitations define the scope of these conclusions. First, although participant-agnostic models
support target-dataset holdout retrieval, we have not yet quantified how efficiently limited participant-
specific data could improve upon this initial representation. Comparing zero-shot transfer with few-shot
estimation of a new participant embedding while freezing the shared encoder would clarify how much
individual adaptation remains beneficial. Second, CLIP provides the supervisory target and consequently
constrains the geometry that the model can recover. Although our analyses show that CLIP-like semantic
relationships survive prediction from fMRI, they do not establish that this geometry is uniquely or com-
pletely representative of human visual organization. Comparisons with behaviour-derived similarity spaces,
alternative self-supervised vision models and other multimodal representations could separate structure
imposed by the target model from structure consistently supported by human neural data. A stronger test
would compare raw-response, pre-projection and output geometries on held-out stimuli against CLIP, in-
dependently pretrained visual models and human similarity judgments, accounting for measurement reli-
ability. THINGS behavioural judgments and their behaviour-only similarity embedding provide one such
reference [24]; CLIP-based predictions that extend this embedding to new images [25] should be distin-
guished from direct behavioural measurements. Alternative cluster definitions would test sensitivity to the
grouping scheme, whereas alternative supervision would require retraining the decoder. Third, the source
datasets remain highly imbalanced in participants, trials and stimulus composition, making it difficult to iso-
late diversity from scale. Finally, network masking and gradient attribution characterize the fitted decoder
and can reflect group size, redundant inputs, and distribution shift. Biological interpretation would addi-
tionally require attribution validation and perturbation controls [26]; these analyses do not establish causal
necessity of the corresponding brain systems.
More broadly, this work suggests a different strategy for scaling computational models of human brain
activity. Progress need not depend exclusively on collecting a single, uniformly acquired dataset of ever-
increasing size. Independently acquired experiments contain shared information that can become compu-
tationally compatible when anatomical correspondence, cross-participant structure and stimulus semantics
are modelled jointly. Our target-dataset holdout results support decoding without fitting participant em-
beddings to the target data. Extending this framework to few-shot participant adaptation, behaviourally
grounded targets and other sensory or cognitive domains could provide a route towards neural models that
improve as heterogeneous datasets accumulate— transforming cross-study variation from an obstacle to
scaling into a source of transferable structure.
Methods
MOSAIC dataset and harmonization
Dataset aggregation.
We used MOSAIC, a harmonized collection of eight independently acquired visual-
fMRI datasets [20]: the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), THINGS-fMRI (THINGS), Deeprecon, BOLD5000, the
BOLD Moments Dataset (BMD), Generic Object Decoding (GOD), the Human Actions Dataset (HAD), and the
Natural Object Dataset (NOD). Together, these datasets span substantial variation in acquisition protocol,
scanner, stimulus domain, experimental design, and amount of data collected per participant. After har-
monization and cross-dataset filtering, MOSAIC contains 93 dataset-specific participant entries, 430,007
single-trial fMRI–stimulus pairs, and 162,839 unique stimuli.
The curated splits comprise 144,360 naturalistic training stimuli associated with 353,924 single-trial re-
sponses, 18,145 naturalistic test stimuli associated with 68,011 responses, and 334 artificial test stimuli as-
sociated with 8,072 responses. The experiments reported here use the naturalistic training and validation
partitions. HAD contributes to model training but is not included in the retrieval evaluation.
A dataset-specific participant entry is treated as a distinct modelling unit. Accordingly, participant iden-
tity is defined jointly by dataset and participant label. For example, sub-01 from two different source datasets
12


is assigned two different participant indices. This convention allows the model to accommodate dataset-
specific differences in acquisition and recording conditions even when participant labels coincide.
Cross-dataset split construction and leakage control.
The scale of MOSAIC creates an important po-
tential source of leakage; the same or highly similar visual stimulus can occur in more than one constituent
dataset. MOSAIC therefore constructs its splits at the level of stimulus identity rather than individual fMRI
trials. Identical stimuli assigned to conflicting source-dataset splits are first reconciled so that all observa-
tions of a stimulus remain in the same partition.
A second filtering stage removes near-duplicate visual content. Training stimuli are compared with test
stimuli using cosine similarity between DreamSim embeddings [27]; training stimuli exceeding a similarity
threshold of 0.8196 to a test stimulus are excluded. This procedure removed 1,186 training stimuli. Conse-
quently, repeated observations of the same stimulus—including observations obtained from different par-
ticipants or datasets—remain together, while identical or highly similar images cannot appear on opposite
sides of the train–test boundary.
fMRI preprocessing and single-trial response estimation.
Rather than combining the heterogeneous
preprocessing products released with the original datasets, MOSAIC reprocesses the constituent studies
through a common pipeline [20]. Functional data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep v23.2.0 [28] and reg-
istered to the HCP fsLR32k cortical surface space. Single-trial response amplitudes were estimated using
GLMsingle [29], with dataset-specific treatment of acquisition timing, stimulus duration, repeated presen-
tations, and nuisance regressors.
Within each session or session group, beta estimates were normalized using statistics estimated exclu-
sively from training trials. The resulting training-derived mean and standard deviation were then applied
unchanged to both training and validation responses. No spatial smoothing was applied. This harmoniza-
tion step places measurements from otherwise heterogeneous experiments into a common anatomical and
statistical representation while preventing information from the validation partition from entering the nor-
malization procedure.

CLIP target representations.
Target stimulus representations were precomputed using the image en-
coder of OpenAI CLIP ViT-B/32 [4]. For static-image stimuli, the presented image was passed directly
through the frozen CLIP image encoder, yielding a 512-dimensional target embedding C(j) ∈R512.
For datasets containing dynamic video stimuli, including BMD and HAD, each video was represented by
its temporally central frame. Specifically, the frame at, or nearest to, the temporal midpoint of the pre-
sented video clip was extracted and passed through the same CLIP ViT-B/32 image encoder. The resulting

that video stimulus. This provided a single fixed-dimensional image-space representation for each dynamic
stimulus while keeping the target representation consistent with that used for the static-image datasets. Ac-
cordingly, this target evaluates correspondence to a representative frame and does not separately establish
decoding of temporal dynamics or actions.
The CLIP encoder remained frozen throughout training; gradients were propagated only through the
brain encoder, attention-pooling module, and projection head.

Model optimization
Models were optimized with AdamW using an initial learning rate of 10−4 and weight decay of 10−3. Bias
parameters, normalization parameters, and the learned contrastive logit scale were excluded from weight
decay. The learning rate followed a per-epoch cosine-annealing schedule. Training used 256 samples per
GPU across four GPUs with distributed data parallelism, giving an effective batch size of 1,024. Embeddings
were gathered across GPUs so that each contrastive loss term was computed against all 1,024 samples in the
global batch. Models were trained in 32-bit precision with gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 and random seed

Zero-shot cross-dataset transfer
For all zero-shot curriculum experiments, participant embeddings were disabled throughout model training
and evaluation. For each target dataset, the dataset was excluded in its entirety, including all of its partic-
ipants and stimuli. Consequently, no target dataset’s participant embeddings or other parameters were
learned or estimated, and no training data from the target dataset were available to the model. The target
evaluation set remained fixed while the source training pool was expanded cumulatively. At each curriculum
step, one additional dataset was added to the training pool; the target dataset was never introduced during
this sequence.

Cross-dataset semantic geometry and retrieval robustness
Hierarchical geometry within the learned brain space.
To test whether stimulus identity and se-
mantic structure were preserved directly within the learned brain representations, we compared predicted
brain embeddings across participant entries. Because the same stimulus can have multiple fMRI observa-
tions within a participant, embeddings were first averaged for each participant–stimulus combination and
ℓ2-normalized. We then considered stimuli observed by at least two distinct participant entries and formed
pairs of embeddings corresponding to the same stimulus. To prevent stimuli seen by many participants from
disproportionately influencing the analysis, at most ten participant pairs were sampled per stimulus.

ized brain embeddings. We constructed two control conditions using stimuli available for the corresponding
participants. For the same-cluster condition, the embedding from participant i was compared with up to five
randomly sampled embeddings from participant j belonging to the same semantic cluster but corresponding
to different stimuli; the reverse comparison, from participant j to participant i, was computed analogously,
and the two directional means were averaged. The different-cluster condition was constructed identically,
except that control stimuli were sampled from semantic clusters different from that of the target. Sampling
was without replacement.

Complete semantic organization of the validation stimulus space
To complement the representative clusters shown in the main text, we examined the complete set of 40 se-
mantic clusters defined from normalized CLIP embeddings of the unique validation stimuli (Supplementary
Fig. S1). The clusters span a broad range of visual content, including people, animals, actions, landscapes,
vehicles, architecture and manufactured objects (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Retrieval accuracy varied sub-
stantially across this full semantic spectrum and did not simply follow the number of stimuli assigned to
each cluster (Supplementary Fig. S1b). Cluster composition also varied across source datasets, with many
semantic categories represented by stimuli from multiple datasets (Supplementary Fig. S1c). Together, these
results show that the content-dependent retrieval differences observed in the main analysis extend across
the complete stimulus taxonomy rather than being specific to the ten clusters selected for detailed neu-
roanatomical analysis.
Semantic geometry and cortical contributions across all stimulus clusters
We next extended the geometric and neuroanatomical analyses from the ten representative clusters used
in the main text to the complete set of 40 semantic clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2). Network ablation
effects were strongly content dependent: ventral visual/object and early visual regions produced some of
the largest retrieval impairments across the semantic space, but the magnitude and relative contribution of
individual networks varied considerably among clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2a).
The two semantic-similarity margins likewise varied across categories (Supplementary Fig. S2b). Most
clusters showed positive stimulus-identity and/or semantic margins, although the strength and statistical
support of these effects differed across the stimulus taxonomy. Finally, the functional-network composition
of positively cluster-selective ROIs showed marked variation across all 40 clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2c).
Together, these analyses show that the category-dependent geometric and cortical effects highlighted in
the main text form part of a broader, continuously varying organization across the full semantic stimulus
space.

Robustness to checkpoint selection
To assess whether the main findings depended on checkpoint selection, we compared the checkpoint se-
lected during training by validation retrieval with the final checkpoint from the same training run. Both were
evaluated on the same held-out set of 65,395 trials corresponding to 12,819 unique images from 63 partici-
pants across seven datasets (Fig. S3). The selected checkpoint achieved moderately higher global retrieval
(Fig. S3a), with a +1.6 percentage-point difference in overall Top-10 accuracy (95% CI, 1.1–2.2 pp; Fig. S3c).
Dataset-level performance showed a similar ordering across checkpoints (Fig. S3b,c), whereas retrieval us-
ing participant-specific galleries was essentially unchanged overall (+0.1 pp; 95% CI, −0.3–0.5 pp; Fig. S3c,d).
The semantic-category retrieval profile was also highly preserved (Spearman ρ = 0.98; Fig. S3e), as was the
decline in expected Top-10 accuracy with increasing candidate-pool size (Fig. S3f). Thus, checkpoint selec-
tion modestly affected absolute global retrieval but did not materially alter the dataset-level, semantic, or
retrieval scaling patterns reported in the main analyses.
The selected checkpoint was chosen during training by validation Top-5 retrieval and was used for all
main-text analyses; the last checkpoint corresponds to the final epoch of the same run. Accuracy is unique-
stimulus Top-10 unless stated otherwise. Confidence intervals were estimated from 2,000 bootstrap resam-
ples. For pooled retrieval analyses, resampling was performed by image so that repeated trials of the same
image remained grouped, with identical resamples used for both checkpoints to preserve pairing.
