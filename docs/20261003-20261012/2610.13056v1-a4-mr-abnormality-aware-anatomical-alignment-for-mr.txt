Abstract
With the advent of large scale foundation models, significant progress has been
made on their application to Magnetic Resonance Imaging. Recent vision language
MR models generalize well across a wide variety of clinical and research based
MR tasks and perform on par with task-specific models. However, we find anatomy
specific localization and pathological analysis still remains woefully inadequate,
especially in any real-world scenarios. To this end, we propose A4-MR, a 3D
vision-language foundation model trained on around 50k MR studies (491k MR
series), about half with corresponding radiology reports. We aim to solve the
following tasks, 1) localize anatomies through large scale pretraining leveraging
noisy labels, 2) align image and text representations at the anatomy level, 3) natively
learn separate embedding representations for healthy and unhealthy anatomies. We
demonstrate that our model surpasses existing foundation models on a diverse set
of tasks (e.g. +1.7 AUROC on abnormality detection against MedSigLIP). With
a frozen encoder, it also outperforms end-to-end task-specific models on knee
segmentation (+1.4 Dice against MedNeXt) and is on par with them on abdominal
organ segmentation (within 0.4 Dice of the best model on AMOS). Without any
prompt, it localizes 3D anatomies as well as MedSAM2 given a ground-truth box.

Introduction
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has transformed medical imaging by enabling safe, non-invasive
visualization of soft tissue across a wide range of clinical tasks, including disease classification,
organ-at-risk segmentation, and tumor identification and monitoring. Despite these advantages,
automated MR analysis remains challenging. MR scans vary substantially across scanner hardware,
acquisition protocols, spatial resolutions, and anatomical regions, while clinically relevant pathologies
are often subtle and difficult to distinguish. These challenges are further compounded by the limited
availability of large, diverse datasets with high-quality annotations.
In recent years, foundation models have emerged as a promising strategy for addressing these limita-
tions. By leveraging large-scale unlabeled data, these models learn general-purpose representations
that can be transferred to downstream tasks. For example, BERT and GPT-2 [10, 32] transformed
natural language processing, while ViT [12] played a similar role in computer vision. Self-supervised
methods such as DINOv2 [28] and MAE [14] have further enabled large visual models to learn

methods such as YOLO and DETR [34, 6] have demonstrated strong progress in object-level visual
understanding, with DETR showing the effectiveness of transformer architectures for detection.
These advances have increasingly been adapted to medical imaging, where foundation models have
pushed performance in modalities such as CT [3, 30], X-ray [22, 37, 52], and MR [21, 47, 36].
Prior work has explored both multimodal and modality-specific representation learning. Multimodal
models are trained across mixtures of imaging modalities [45, 46], enabling support for diverse image
types and downstream tasks. In contrast, modality-specific models focus on a single imaging domain,
including X-ray [37, 48], MR [47, 11], and CT [54], often yielding stronger representations for their
target modality. These models have also been adapted to specialized downstream tasks, including
disease classification [39], segmentation [40, 51], and retrieval [47].
However, existing medical foundation models remain limited in several important ways. Some focus
on narrow anatomical regions [8, 2, 41], others are optimized primarily for a single task such as
segmentation [42, 43], and some operate on two-dimensional slices rather than full three-dimensional
volumes [11]. Models trained jointly across CT, X-ray, and MR may also generalize poorly to MR,
where acquisition heterogeneity is high and large-scale labeled data are scarce. Moreover, many
existing models learn image-level representations [15, 11, 47], in some cases aligned with radiology
reports, that lack explicit anatomy-level structure, making it difficult to distinguish normal from
abnormal findings within a specific organ or region.
To address these issues, we introduce A4-MR, an anatomy-aware multimodal foundation model
for 3D MR imaging. Our approach uses a two-stage training strategy designed to support broad
generalization across MR tasks (Fig. 1). In the first stage, self-supervised vision learning with a
teacher–student framework enables the model to learn from a large-scale MR pretraining dataset.
In the second stage, the model learns anatomy-targeted representations from bootstrapped labels
while aligning image and text embeddings at both the image and anatomy levels. This design
enables A4-MR to support anatomy-level localization, multimodal retrieval, and abnormality-aware
representation learning, leading to improved performance over state-of-the-art foundation models on
tasks such as triaging.

Related Work
Decipher-MR [47] and OmniMRI [15] are 3D MRI vision-language foundation models trained
on large corpora of MRI series with associated radiology reports, spanning diverse anatomical
regions, pathologies, and acquisition protocols. Both approaches use multi-stage training for visual
and language encoders, also referred to as towers: a self-supervised vision model first learns MR
image representations, followed by a second stage that aligns radiology reports with MR images.
Decipher-MR uses cross-modal training in the second stage as a form of weak supervision for MR
representation learning, whereas OmniMRI freezes the visual tower, aligns the text encoder, and later
performs instruction tuning with additional annotated supervision.
These approaches enable general-purpose representation learning and perform strongly across re-
trieval, classification, and segmentation tasks compared with task-specific models. Inspired by this
line of work, we adopt a multi-stage pretraining strategy in which free-form radiology reports provide
weak supervision, and we further extend the representation granularity to the organ level.
MRI-CORE [11] is a vision foundation model for MRI, pretrained in a self-supervised fashion on
over 100k MRI volumes across 18 body regions. The model uses DINOv2 as its self-supervised
training objective, combining global and image-level objectives in a student–teacher framework,
and initializes its vision backbone from SAM-pretrained weights. MRI-CORE demonstrates strong
performance in segmentation across body regions and in zero-shot classification tasks. However, the
absence of a language component limits it to vision-only tasks and restricts its utility for applications
such as retrieval and grounding. Its coverage of anatomical diversity and pathological variation is
also narrower than our training corpus, motivating our approach of training natively with paired text
reports across a broader range of anatomies and clinical findings.
X-DETR [4] is a versatile end-to-end architecture for instance-wise vision-language tasks, designed to
efficiently align free-form language with individual object instances rather than whole images. Based
on DETR [6] and MDETR [20], it uses a visual object detector, a language encoder, and a lightweight

3
Methodology
3.1
Pretraining Data
To improve generalization to real-world settings, we compile a diverse dataset of MRI studies
collected across multiple hardware platforms and geographic locations. These studies cover 16 body
regions and a broad range of sequence types, scanner vendors and patient demographics (Fig. 1a).
We rigorously preprocessed the data to ensure quality, excluding scans with non-uniform spacing,
missing slices, low resolution, excessive noise, or too few slices. After preprocessing, the dataset
consisted of 50k studies comprising 491k MRI series, with reports available for 25.5k studies. Each
study included several MRI series spanning multiple protocols. We split the dataset into training and
evaluation sets, using 85% of studies for pretraining and 15% for downstream validation. Half of the
pretraining set was used for first-stage pretraining, and the full training set was used for second-stage
pretraining.
3.2
Pretraining Data Preparation
Medical annotation is a laborious and costly process. To provide supervision at scale, we weakly anno-
tate MR images and their corresponding radiology reports using existing models. In order to provide
3


localization supervision, we applied standard MRI processing techniques (i.e., intensity normalization
and foreground cropping; details in the Supplementary Material) and utilized an open-source segmen-
tation model [43] together with proprietary custom-built deep learning (DL) segmentation models to
delineate anatomical regions. We utilized DICOM header information to translate mask coordinates
across different protocols in the absence of a DL model for a given sequence, as a means of weak
supervision. Finally, we converted the resulting delineation masks into bounding-box coordinates to
capture anatomy location. Full preprocessing details (foreground cropping, intensity scaling, and
crop sizes for each pretraining stage) are provided in the Supplementary Material (Section 6.2).
To better support cross-modal training and localization, we standardized free-form radiology reports
using an LLM (Claude 3.7 Sonnet). We instructed the LLM to extract medical anatomies, information
describing the health status of each anatomy, and corresponding supporting evidence. Finally, we
manually reviewed a subset of processed reports to check for hallucinations and ensure output
consistency. A list of anatomies and additional details on anatomy-text pairing are provided in the
Supplementary Material (Section 6.6).
3.3
Pretraining
We implement a two stage pretraining strategy following earlier work [47, 15, 11]. The first
stage consists of pretraining of the image and text encoders via self-supervised methods. Unlike
earlier work that focuses on image level representation, in the second round, we extend multimodal
pretraining with anatomy localization where we train the encoder to identify image anatomies and
align the image and text encoder embedding spaces. For the text encoder, we start with a pretrained
PubMedBERT [13] model trained on biomedical text. We then train this model on our MR reports
using a masked language modeling loss. For the image encoder, we use the DINOv2 pretraining
strategy of a student-teacher network trained via self supervision. The student network learns to
match the teacher’s representation of the MR scans at the image and patch level.
As we mentioned earlier, our goals are to train a foundation model that can natively do anatomy level
localization, learn embedding representations at the image and anatomy level, and natively learn an
anatomy level representation that differentiates normal and abnormal appearances.
For the first goal, we leverage a DETR style decoder trained on weakly annotated data to classify
and learn bounding boxes for our predefined list of anatomies. For our second goal, we use the
CLS token of our encoder and leverage the fact that DETR provides output embeddings for each
identified anatomy. We pass these through a projection layer and use a contrastive loss to align the
anatomy with the corresponding text representation. In addition, we also align the CLS token of
the encoder with the text encoder. For our final goal, we leverage abnormality information from
the reports to structure the CLIP loss in such a way that the model learns to separate normal and
abnormal anatomies corresponding to the same organ. Each of these is described in detail in the
following sections.
3.4
Model Architecture
Fig. 2 gives an overview of the second-stage architecture and losses. Augmentation details for
both pretraining stages and study-level, organ-aware data sampling strategy are provided in the
Supplementary Material (Section 6.3 and Section 6.4 respectively).
We employ a 3D Vision Transformer (ViT-Base) as our vision encoder similar to [11, 38, 47, 40].
We use an 8 × 8 × 8 cubic non-overlapping patch size. The model consists of a projection layer
that operates on flattened patches to generate patch embeddings. A linear position embedding
interpolation allows handling of different image sizes during downstream and inference tasks. Our
model follows the rest of the standard ViT-B architecture [12]. Our decoder used for localization is
based on DETR [6]. We use 200 object queries, 126 classes including the no object class and a 6 layer
transformer, that consist of self attention, cross attention and an MLP for bounding box regression and
classification. In addition, we use the output object embedding from the decoder projected through
a linear layer to align the anatomy representation with text embeddings. Section 3.4.1 provides a
more detailed description of the DETR architecture. We use a BERT style encoder initialized from
PubMedBERT, which was trained on biomedical text data. We train this encoder via a projection
layer to align with the CLS token of the ViT-based teacher encoder, and object query embeddings of
the DETR decoder. An image-text contrastive learning approach [33] was used for this task.

3.4.1
DETR for bounding box detection
We use the Detection Transformer architecture proposed by [6] to build our anatomy localizer. As a
refresher, DETR operates as follows.
It consists of a backbone such as ResNet, a transformer encoder, and a transformer decoder. We get
rid of the ResNet and directly feed images into a ViT based transformer encoder. The output tokens
from the transformer are Z. The transformer decoder takes learnable input queries Q, and consists of
L stacked decoder blocks. Each block consists of a self attention layer followed by a cross attention
layer. The latter makes the object queries interact with the encoder tokens Z. The output embeddings
from each decoder layer are then used to train an MLP classifier to classify each embedding into
a predefined list of classes or a no object found class. A regression head then generates the box
coordinates for classes other than no object found. In addition, we add a CLIP projection head to the
output embeddings of the last layer, which is then used to align anatomy text representations. We
describe the working of this in Section 3.4.2.
Following DETR, we employ a fixed set of N object queries, where N is chosen to exceed the
maximum number of anatomical structures present in an MRI scan. The ground-truth set ˆy is
correspondingly padded with ∅(no-object) labels to match. This formulation casts detection as a set
prediction problem, which we solve via bipartite matching using the Hungarian algorithm.

Results
We compare our model to the following models:
• Vista3D CT-MR [16], a multimodal 3D foundation model designed to jointly learn from CT and
MR volumes for robust cross-modality medical image segmentation.
• MedSigLIP [36], a medical adaptation of the SigLIP [49] vision-language model that aligns
clinical images with text for improved multimodal understanding and retrieval.
• BiomedParse [53], a large-scale biomedical parsing model that unifies segmentation and structured
understanding across diverse medical imaging tasks and anatomies.
• MRI-CORE [11], a foundation model trained specifically on MRI data to capture modality-specific
representations for downstream tasks such as segmentation and diagnosis.
• Decipher-MR [47], where a 3D volumetric image level contrastive objective is utilized to align
visual and text towers.
• MedSAM2 [23], a promptable medical segmentation foundation model that propagates 2D box
prompts through a 3D volume.
• nnUNet3D [17], a deep learning-based segmentation method that automatically configures itself,
including preprocessing, network architecture, training and post-processing, for any new task.
• MedNeXt [35], a ConvNeXt-based 3D segmentation network trained end-to-end.
• VNet [25], a 3D convolutional volumetric segmentation model, originally proposed for prostate
MRI.
• BiomedCLIP [50], a multimodal vision-language foundation model trained on image-text pairs
from biomedical scientific articles.
• MedImageInsight [7], a medical imaging embedding model trained on images and text.
• Qwen2.5-VL [1], a general-purpose vision-language model, used as a baseline for report genera-
tion.
• No Abnormality, an ablation of A4-MR trained with the same setup but without the abnormality-
aware contrastive loss (Section 3.4.2). It is reported as “No Abnormality” in the tables.

4.1
Cross Modal Retrieval
Zero-shot cross-modal retrieval enables matching between MRI images and text—such as retrieving
scans from text queries or linking relevant report descriptions to a given image—without the need for
additional model fine-tuning. We evaluated A4-MR’s zero-shot cross-modal retrieval performance on
text-to-image and image-to-text tasks on a dataset which contains reports and MRIs across sixteen
body regions, comparing it against other biomedical vision-language models.
Using the held-out test set from our pretraining dataset, we performed paired report-to-image retrieval,
using both full radiology reports and conclusion sections as text queries (6.5). Performance was
measured by the percentage of cases where all top N retrieved scans belonged to the same body
region as the text query, evaluated at top 1, 3 and 5 results.
As shown in Tables 1 and 2, A4-MR achieves the strongest overall performance across both image-to-
text and text-to-image retrieval tasks, consistently outperforming Decipher-MR, BiomedCLIP, and
MedImageInsight. In Table 1, A4-MR obtains the highest scores for all Entire Report image-to-text
retrieval metrics and achieves the best performance in three of four Organ Description metrics, with
only a marginal decrease in @1 compared to the Decipher-MR (85.42 vs. 86.60). Similarly, Table 2
shows that A4-MR demonstrates the strongest overall text-to-image retrieval performance across
the Entire Report and Organ Description settings, with the exception of Entire Report @1, where
No Abnormality achieves the highest score. The largest gains are observed in mAP and higher-
rank retrieval metrics (@3 and @5), indicating improved ranking quality and stronger cross-modal
alignment rather than only better top-1 retrieval. Overall, these results demonstrate that A4-MR
effectively captures both global report-level semantics and fine-grained organ-specific information,
leading to more robust and accurate medical image-text retrieval.

4.3
Segmentation
We evaluate whether the representations learned by A4-MR transfer to dense prediction by training a
lightweight segmentation decoder on top of the frozen vision encoder. In all experiments, the encoder
weights are kept fixed and only the segmentation decoder is trained, allowing us to isolate the quality
of the pretrained image representations.
We use a SegResNet-style decoder [27] with skip connections from the A4-MR encoder. Specifically,
the final patch embeddings and intermediate embeddings from zero indexed layers 2, 5 and 8 are
provided to the decoder to preserve spatial detail at multiple resolutions. The decoder contains
approximately 8 million trainable parameters; for KneeSeg we use a larger variant (Section 4.3.1).
We experimented with several voxel spacings and found that smaller spacings consistently improved
segmentation performance, even when the original acquisition spacing was coarser. Unless otherwise
stated, we use the 25th percentile spacing for each dataset. Models are trained with a learning rate
of 5 × 10−4, cosine decay, and a minimum learning rate of 10−6. Training is performed for 60k
batches per GPU across 8 GPUs with a batch size of 2. We use random cropping with a crop size of
384 × 384 × 48 and additional augmentations adapted from AMOS [19].
We benchmark A4-MR against alternative pretrained models on three segmentation datasets:
AMOS [19], an internal Dataset A (pelvis), and CirrMRI600+. Dataset A is out of distribution
relative to the pretraining corpus. For AMOS, we use the official train/validation split. For Dataset
A and CirrMRI600+, we evaluate on held-out test sets. Knee cartilage segmentation is analyzed
separately in Section 4.3.1. Unless otherwise stated, all results are reported as mean Dice across
segmentation classes. Extended per-organ ablations (encoder variant, curriculum augmentation,
decoder warm-start) are provided in the Supplementary Material.

4.4
Localization and Detection
We evaluate 3D organ localization on the AMOS22 MR validation set (247 image–organ pairs after
exclusions; see Datasets in Section 4). A4-MR predicts a 3D bounding box for each organ directly
from the full volume, without prompts or task-specific fine-tuning; we report the same for its No
Abnormality variant. Localization is measured by the 3D intersection-over-union (IoU) between
predicted and ground-truth boxes, averaged over images per organ and then over the 13 organs.
Preprocessing and metric details are given in Supplementary Section 7.4.
To our knowledge, no public model predicts organ bounding boxes directly from full 3D MRI
volumes. We therefore compare against MedSAM2 [23], a promptable medical foundation model
that propagates 2D box prompts through the volume. It receives ground-truth boxes on one slice
(1 box) or on three slices (3 slices) per organ, so its results show what prompted segmentation can
achieve rather than fully automatic localization. As an upper bound, we use the nnUNet3D trained on
AMOS from Section 4.3 and convert its predicted masks to bounding boxes.
Table 7 summarizes the results. Without any prompt, A4-MR (66.3) slightly outperforms MedSAM2
given a ground-truth box (65.5), while MedSAM2 with three prompted slices (74.8) and the AMOS-
trained nnUNet3D (82.4) remain stronger. On AMOS, the abnormality-aware loss also improves
localization (66.3 vs. 65.0 without it), with gains on 11 of the 13 organs and up to +4.4 IoU on the left
adrenal gland; the only decrease, on the inferior vena cava, is 0.5. Because the two models share the
same localization decoder and box supervision and differ only in how positive pairs are formed in the
contrastive batch, this indicates that separating normal and abnormal occurrences of an organ yields
more discriminative organ-level features that also benefit localization. Per organ (Supplementary
Table 23), the picture depends on organ shape and size. For large, compact organs (liver, spleen,
kidneys), a single prompted box already pins down most of the extent, and MedSAM2 reaches 90–97
IoU versus 75–81 for A4-MR. For elongated or hollow organs that span many slices with changing
cross-sections, slice-wise propagation struggles: A4-MR outperforms MedSAM2 with one box on the
esophagus (61.2 vs. 38.4), stomach (76.0 vs. 51.4), inferior vena cava (70.7 vs. 47.9) and duodenum
(60.9 vs. 41.1), and even MedSAM2 with three slices on the first three. Small organs remain hardest
for all models; for the adrenal glands and the gallbladder, A4-MR reaches only 42–52 IoU, consistent
with the 8-voxel patch size of its encoder. Figure 6 shows qualitative examples for volumes with the
best, median and worst localization performance.

Prostate Structured Report Generation
We frame automated prostate MR report generation as a vision-conditioned lesion identification,
characterization and measurement problem. Given a single patient study, which may comprise several
co-acquired 3D MRI series, the model outputs one structured report as a JSON object (schema
in Supplementary Section 7.5) that describes clinical findings and enumerates identified lesions
(anatomical location, size in millimeters, and PI-RADS [44] category) together with the estimated
prostate volume. Here we aim at structured findings extraction (lesion detection, measurement, and
PI-RADS characterization), not free-text narrative report writing. This choice makes the output
directly parseable and evaluable.

5
Limitations and Future Work
• Weak and noisy pretraining supervision. During pretraining, anatomy boxes are derived from
TotalSegmentator, proprietary segmentation models, and DICOM-based coordinate transfer, and
anatomy health status is extracted from reports by an LLM. Only a subset of the processed
reports was manually reviewed, so label noise in both localization targets and abnormality labels
shapes the learned representations; downstream tasks are trained and evaluated on their own
curated labels with the encoder frozen. The weak masks are also reduced to bounding boxes. A
segmentation head trained with a masked loss during pretraining, applied only to anatomies for
which a mask is available, would let the encoder learn from voxel-level shape information instead
of the box extent alone.
• Patch resolution. With 8 × 8 × 8 patches, structures smaller than a single patch along any
axis cannot be resolved, and zero-shot localization still trails prompted (multi-slice) MedSAM2
and dataset-specific supervised models, particularly on small organs such as the adrenal glands.
Smaller or anisotropic patches, or multi-scale tokenization, could improve localization and
segmentation of small anatomies at a manageable computational cost.
• Coarse abnormality modeling. Abnormality is represented as a binary healthy/abnormal state
per organ, propagated to all mentions of that organ within a report. This does not capture
severity, multiple co-occurring findings, or lesion-level detail, and our abnormality evaluation
focuses mainly on a single organ and disease (liver cirrhosis). Abnormality is learned implicitly
through the structure of the contrastive targets. A classification head on the per-anatomy decoder
embeddings, trained with an explicit abnormality loss, could provide a more direct training signal,
enable organ-level abnormality prediction without a downstream probe, and serve as a first step
toward severity and lesion level labels.
• Fixed anatomy list. The localization decoder detects a fixed list of anatomy classes. Because
each detected anatomy has an embedding aligned with the text encoder, this list can already be
queried with free text rather than class names: a query such as “anatomies required for vision”
matches all related classes, including the left and right eyes, lenses and optic nerves. Howeever,
anatomies outside the vocabulary list cannot be localized. Extending the list, together with the
corresponding weak labels and report findings, would broaden anatomy-level alignment and
abnormality awareness to more body regions and structures, towards open-vocabulary setup.
• From anatomies to pathologies. Existing MR foundation models such as MRI-CORE [11],
OmniMRI [15] and Decipher-MR [47] learn image-level representations, in some cases aligned
with radiology reports. A4-MR extends this alignment to anatomical regions across the body. The
natural next step is to bring lesions, tumors and other pathologies into pretraining in the same way:
16


findings extracted from reports provide weak text supervision, and existing lesion detection and
segmentation models can bootstrap their locations. Any pretrained model can be fine-tuned for
lesion detection or delineation; the goal here is to make pathology grounding part of the learned
representation itself.

Pretraining Details
This section provides the image preprocessing, augmentation, and data sampling details referenced
from the main paper’s Methodology section.
6.1
Training details.
The second pretraining stage is trained with AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999) for 100 epochs of
1,250 iterations (125k iterations) on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a batch size of 12 per GPU (global
batch size 96), using FSDP with fp16 mixed precision. The base learning rate of 5 × 10−3 is scaled
by
p
96/1024 to 1.53 × 10−3, warmed up linearly over 2 epochs and then decayed with a cosine
schedule to 10−6. Weight decay follows a cosine schedule from 0.04 to 0.4, and gradients are clipped
at a norm of 3.0. We apply layer-wise learning-rate decay of 0.9 and scale the learning rate by 0.1
for the pretrained vision and text backbones and by 0.2 for the contrastive projection heads. Both
contrastive losses use a learnable logit scale initialized to 1/0.07 (i.e., a temperature of τ = 0.07).
6.2
Image preprocessing
Following standard MRI processing techniques, we used Otsu’s method to crop the foreground
using 256 bins. This removes the black background. We then used percentile scaling to scale the
pixel intensities between -1 and 1 using the 1st and 99th percentile pixel as references. First stage
pretraining used a global crop size of 256,256,24, and a local crop size of 128,128,16. For the second
stage, we resized the image to 384,384,48 while preserving aspect ratio. This larger crop size was
chosen to minimize information loss and better help train the localization and contrastive tasks.
6.3
Data augmentations
For the first stage, we use the same augmentations as [47]. They propose a modified version of
DINOv2’s cropping strategy specifically suited for 3D MR data. These augmentations were designed
to train the model to be invariant to MRI resolution, size and acquisition protocols, across different
scanner and body regions. We start with two global crops for the teacher network. One global crop
corresponds to the entire image scaled to the model’s input dimension, while the second is selected at
a random resolution. Combined with augmentations like rotation and flipping, this introduces scale
diversity, allowing the same scan to be seen at multiple resolutions. In addition, we generate local
crops for each of these global crops, allowing the model to learn consistent image level representations.
For the second stage we resize images to match the resolution of the model’s input, padding where
necessary. In addition we have augmentations like random rotation to aid the model to learn a rich
set of image features to help with localization. We adjust the ground truth bounding box image
coordinates for the various anatomies as necessary.
6.4
Data sampling
We adopt a study level data sampling strategy at the organ level. MRIs and corresponding reports
describing different anatomies and body regions tend to be fairly easy to distinguish between,
exhibiting marked differences. However, similar body regions make this a far more difficult task.
There are often subtleties and nuances in clinical pathology that are hard to distinguish. In order to
have our model learn these distinctions, we try to increase the homogeneity within a batch. This
batch-wise sampling, proposed in [47], is an implicit form of hard negative mining. We use the organ
name attribute from our LLM processed reports to achieve this. With some random probability, we
try to ensure the batch is composed of other similar organs from other series. However, a key point
to note, we do not use the same series from within a batch as a negative contrastive sample. This
approach allows the model to learn nuanced differences and learn to separate different anatomies
within its embedding space, without the need for explicit hard negative mining. This further helps
with downstream tasks that require the model to differentiate between similar clinical pathologies,
such as our healthy and unhealthy use case. In addition, for a given series within a report, our
LLM preprocessing methodology yields multiple text samples. As such, we randomly sample ntext
reports from a study, and broadcast our ViT image embeddings ntext times. This strategy allows for
contrastive training in a one-to-many fashion between image and text. Our effective batch size is thus
B × ntext, where B is the original batch size.

provides explicit anatomical and pathological context for each report. Figure 7 shows an example.

Ablation Studies
7.1
Segmentation: Setup and Ablation Design
7.1.1
Decoder Architecture and Benchmarks
Figure 8: Architecture of the segmentation decoder
Our model shows strong performance on segmentation. We evaluate this by training a segmentation
decoder on the output of the vision encoder. The encoder was kept frozen while training. Evaluation
was performed on a held out test set.
A SegResNet decoder was used for segmentation. Output patch embeddings from the encoder, as
well as intermediate embeddings from layers 3, 6 and 9, were fed to the decoder via skip connections.
The latter helped preserve spatial information. Our decoder consisted of about 8 million parameters.
We experimented with various voxel spacings and observed consistent improvements with smaller
spacings, even if the original data wasn’t captured at this spacing. We used the 25th percentile spacing
for all results here. A learning rate of 5e-4 with cosine decay and minimum learning rate of 1e-6 was
used. The model was trained for 60k batches per GPU across 8 GPUs, with a batch size of 2. Random
cropping was used to aid data augmentation, with a random crop size of 384,384,48. Additional
augmentations were adapted from AMOS [19].
We benchmarked our model on two datasets: AMOS and an internal pelvis dataset (Dataset A), which
was out of distribution relative to our pretraining set.
For AMOS we use the official train/val split; and for Dataset A, we benchmark on a held-out test set.
Unless stated, results are mean Dice across segmentation classes.
We perform controlled ablations for multi-organ MR segmentation by varying encoder pretraining/fine-
tuning strategy and decoder initialization, while keeping the downstream segmentation pipeline fixed.
7.1.2
Task and Model Setup
Let x ∈RH×W ×D denote a 3D MR volume and y ∈{0, 1}C×H×W ×D the multi-label target mask
for C organs. Our model uses: (i) a frozen foundation encoder (ViT family) to extract volume
embeddings, from the 2, 5, 8 and last layers, and (ii) a trainable 37-layer 3D SegResNet decoder to
predict organ masks

7.1.3
Preprocessing and Sampling Protocol
All experiments use the same preprocessing pipeline (except where explicitly stated): loading,
channel-first conversion, spacing normalization, canonical orientation, foreground cropping, and
percentile intensity normalization. For segmentation labels provided as per-organ masks, masks are
stacked into a multi-channel label tensor.

Task-specific geometry.
We use task-specific voxel spacing and crop sizes:
• Pelvis: spacing (0.52, 0.52, 0.6), crop size 384 × 384 × 48.
• AMOS (abdomen): spacing (0.8, 0.8, 1.2), crop size 224 × 160 × 80.
During training, we feed in random 3D spatial crops of volumes and labels considering GPU memory
constraints.
7.1.4
Training Parameters
We train for 60k iterations with a batch size of 2 per GPU on 8 A100 GPUs (effective batch size 16)
using PyTorch FSDP.
7.1.5
Optimization Control Across Ablations
To isolate ablation effects, all non-ablated components are fixed: decoder architecture, loss function,
crop policy, preprocessing, and augmentation framework. In transfer settings, decoder optimization
uses lower learning rates with a layer-wise linear learning-rate increase across decoder depth.
7.1.6
Curriculum Augmentation
To stabilize optimization and reduce numerical failures (e.g., NaNs), we use curricular augmentation.
The full augmentation pool includes random rotation, zoom, Gaussian noise, Gaussian sharpening,
intensity scaling, contrast scaling/clipping, low-resolution simulation, and contrast adjustment,
followed by spatial padding.
Under curriculum learning, augmentations are introduced in stages at predefined milestones (epochs
10, 20, 30 and 40):
1. Random zoom,
2. Random Gaussian noise + sharpening,
3. Random intensity scale + scale-and-clip contrast,
4. Random low-resolution simulation + contrast adjustment.
This staged schedule is compared against non-curriculum training. Initially, we observed a drop
in performance on Pelvis when curriculum data augmentation was enabled. We attributed this to a
shifted schedule leading to fewer transformations that the model saw during training. To ensure same
quantity of transformations, we adjust probabilities of each transformation in curriculum training to
match the non-curriculum-learning approach. We also tried different scheduling cadences and didn’t
see a change in performance thanks to the adjusted probabilities ensuring.
7.1.7
Encoder Ablations
We evaluate three encoder variants with identical decoder/training settings:
1. ViT-B, DINOv2 SSL →additional localization/bounding-box fine-tuning with X-DETR.
2. ViT-B, DINOv3 SSL →We pretrain ViT-B using DinoV3 SSL paradigm.
3. ViT-L, DINOv3 SSL →To test if scaling the encoder model helps, we pretrain ViT-L using
DinoV3 SSL paradigm.
7.1.8
Decoder Initialization Ablation: Cold Start vs Warm Start
While keeping the encoder fixed, we compare:
• Cold start: decoder initialized with default PyTorch initialization (Kaiming Uniform
weights) and trained directly on task labels.
• Warm start: two-stage transfer for decoder initialization:
1. Bootstrapping: pretrain decoder on 8.5k volumes with TotalSegmentator pseudo-labels
(hard labels).
2. Task fine-tuning: initialize from bootstrap weights and train on target annotations.

7.3
Segmentation: Decoder Size on KneeSeg
The knee results in the main text (Table 6, Fig. 5) use a larger SegResNet decoder than the other
segmentation tasks, with 58M trainable parameters. To test whether the knee results depend on
decoder capacity, we compare it with a bottleneck variant in which each convolution block is replaced
by 1 × 1, 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 convolutions (768 →192 →768 channels). This reduces the first
convolution layer from 16M to 1.3M parameters and the whole decoder from 58M to 4.3M trainable
parameters. Both decoders are trained on the frozen stage-1 encoder. Table 21 shows that the two
variants reach nearly the same Dice (78.4 vs. 78.7 over all structures), and the bottleneck variant has
a slightly lower mean Hausdorff distance (0.642 vs. 0.651 mm). It converges more slowly, however
(136 vs. 87 epochs; 4.9 vs. 3.7 days). Decoder capacity therefore has little effect on knee segmentation
accuracy.

7.4
Localization
Preprocessing.
We process all volumes with a MONAI-based [5] pipeline. Images are loaded in
channel-first format. To ensure a consistent layout across volumes stored with different conventions,
the out-of-plane (slice) axis is identified as the axis with the largest voxel spacing from the affine (or
the smallest dimension if no affine is available) and moved to the last spatial dimension. Foreground
is cropped with an Otsu threshold [29]. Intensities are mapped from the 1st–99th percentile range to
[−1, 1] with clipping. Volumes are then resized so that the larger in-plane dimension is 384 voxels,
preserving the in-plane aspect ratio, and the out-of-plane dimension is resized to 48 slices. Finally,
they are padded to 384 × 384 × 48 with a constant value of −1. All spatial operations are recorded
so that predicted boxes can be mapped back to the original image by applying their inverses. All
boxes are evaluated in the native image coordinates.
Metric.
Localization is measured by the 3D intersection-over-union (IoU) between axis-aligned
predicted and ground-truth boxes. Boxes are defined by inclusive voxel indices, so each box’s volume
equals the number of voxels it covers. Ground-truth boxes enclose all voxels of an organ’s label. We
average IoU over images for each organ, then over the 13 organs, so each organ contributes equally.
MedSAM2 setup.
To our knowledge, no public model predicts organ bounding boxes directly
from full 3D MRI volumes, so we use MedSAM2 [23] as a representative medical foundation model.
MedSAM2 treats a volume as a sequence of 2D slices and propagates a segmentation from prompted
slices to the rest of the volume. We use the public checkpoint (MedSAM2_latest, 512 × 512 input)
with its own preprocessing: slices along the stored axis, resized to 512 × 512. Prompts are 2D boxes
derived from the ground truth, either one box on the slice with the largest organ area (“1 box”), or
boxes on three slices at 25%, 50% and 75% of the organ’s extent (“3 slices”). Propagation runs
in both directions from the prompted slices and stops early when the mask shrinks to under 5%
or grows to over twice the prompted area. Only connected components overlapping the prompted
slices are kept, and the predicted mask is converted to a bounding box. Because MedSAM2 receives
ground-truth boxes as prompts, its results show what prompted segmentation can achieve rather than
fully automatic localization.
Excluded pairs.
We exclude 12 of the 259 pairs, with the same exclusions applied to every model.
Six are organs whose ground-truth box spans fewer than 8 voxels along at least one axis. Because
A4-MR is a vision transformer with 8 voxel patches, such an organ fits within a single patch along that
axis, so its extent cannot be resolved at the patch level. Seven have stray label voxels disconnected
from the organ, which enlarge the ground-truth box more than 1.3-fold (Fig. 9); one pair meets both
criteria. Excluding these pairs changed the overall scores by at most 1.4 and did not change the model
ranking (Table 22).
Per-organ results on AMOS.
Table 23 reports per-organ bounding-box IoU for all models in
the main localization comparison, after the exclusions described above. The nnUNet3D trained
on AMOS achieves the highest IoU for most organs. Among the zero-shot and prompted models,
MedSAM2 with three ground-truth box prompts per organ is strongest, particularly for small organs
such as the adrenal glands and the gallbladder. A4-MR, which receives no prompts, outperforms
prompted MedSAM2 on the esophagus, stomach, inferior vena cava and duodenum when MedSAM2
is given a single box.

7.5
Report Generation
The output for report generation follows this schema:
{ lesions : [{location : string ∪{null},
size : {max_mm : number ∪{null},
dims_mm : [number] ∪{null}},
pi_rads : {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} ∪{null}}],
prostate_volume_cc : number ∪{null} }
We use the following evaluation metrics to measure performance of the candidate models:
• avg_precision: Percentage of predicted lesions that match the ground truth. Higher is better.
• avg_recall: Percentage of ground truth lesions that match the predicted lesions. Higher is
better.
• avg_f1: F1-score of precision and recall. Higher is better.
• avg_size: Size agreement between ground truth and prediction measured as min over max.
Higher is better.
• avg_rouge_f1: Rouge-F1 score for lesion location. Higher is better.
• avg_pi_rads: Measured as 1 - MAE(predicted_pi_rads, ground_truth_pi_rads) / 4.0 where
MAE = mean absolute error. Higher is better.
• avg_prostate_volume_error_cc: MAE of predicted and ground truth prostate volumes.
Lower is better.

7.6
Linear Probing Classification
We applied a 3-layer MLP with 768-dimensional hidden layers, 20% dropout, and ReLU activations
(1.2M parameters) to the CLS embedding of the backbone ViT, serving as the classification or
regression head. All evaluations and comparisons followed a simple probing setup, with the weights
of the pretrained vision backbone frozen. A consistent learning rate of 1e-4 with plateau decay was
applied across all tasks, without intense hyper-parameter tuning.
We further assessed the impact of data preprocessing by evaluating performance on the PICAI dataset
using MRI images cropped around the prostate based on provided masks.
