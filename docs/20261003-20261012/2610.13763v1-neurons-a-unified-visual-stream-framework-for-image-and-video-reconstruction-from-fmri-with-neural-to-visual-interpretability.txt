NEURONS++: A Unified Visual-Stream Framework
for Image and Video Reconstruction from fMRI
with Neural-to-Visual Interpretability
Haonan Wang, Yuchen Wang, Jingyu Lu, Qixiang Zhang, Lehan Wang, Xuanqi Huang, and Xiaomeng Li
Abstract—Decoding visual experience from fMRI is important
for understanding human perception and developing brain-
computer interfaces. Recent generative decoding methods can
reconstruct plausible images and videos, but visual plausibility
alone does not ensure that the output is faithfully grounded in
brain activity. In this work, we propose NEURONS++, a visual-
stream fMRI reconstruction framework for both static image
and dynamic video reconstruction. Compared with our con-
ference version NEURONS, NEURONS++ extends the paradigm
from video-only reconstruction to visual-stream reconstruc-
tion for both images and videos, and replaces parallel task
conditioning with serial cortex-inspired decoding. The model
first uses image-fMRI pretraining to obtain a stable spatial-
semantic initialization, and then performs subject-specific fine-
tuning for image and video reconstruction separately. The se-
rial design organizes fMRI-derived representations into explicit
spatial, semantic, language-level, and temporal cues. Exten-
sive experiments show that NEURONS++ achieves state-of-the-
art video reconstruction and competitive image reconstruction
across standard benchmarks. Beyond reconstruction fidelity,
neural-to-visual analyses indicate that NEURONS++ exhibits
qualitative directional controllability under neural perturba-
tion, that its fMRI encoder read-out is sensitive to stimulus-
relevant cortical features, and that it supports ROI-guided
semantic modulation. Code and model weights are available at:
https://github.com/xmed-lab/NeuronsPlusPlus.
Index Terms—Brain-computer interface, fMRI-to-image recon-
struction, fMRI-to-video reconstruction, diffusion model.
1.
INTRODUCTION
Decoding visual experience from human brain activity is
a fundamental problem in cognitive neuroscience and brain-
computer interfaces. Early studies have shown that fMRI
responses contain rich information about perceived and imag-
ined visual content, ranging from object categories to natural
images and movies [1]–[3]. With the rapid development of
vision-language models and generative priors, recent fMRI-
based visual reconstruction methods have achieved increas-
ingly realistic outputs. For static image reconstruction, modern
methods typically map fMRI responses into CLIP [4], Stable
Diffusion [5], or related latent spaces to synthesize natural
images [6]–[9]. These methods demonstrate that large-scale
visual priors can compensate for the sparsity and noise of
fMRI signals and recover rich semantic and perceptual content
from brain activity.

Despite this progress, three fundamental limitations remain.
First, existing methods are generally developed under sep-
arated reconstruction settings: image decoders emphasize
spatial details and object semantics [6]–[13], whereas video
decoders focus on motion and temporal continuity [14]–[18].
This separation does not reflect continuous visual experience
and prevents the relatively clean spatial-semantic supervision
of image-fMRI data from supporting dynamic visual decoding.
Second, at the representation level, current methods also
suffer from disconnected neural-to-visual representations.
Although recent approaches model low-level structure, high-
level semantics, and temporal dynamics [13], [18], [19], these
signals are often aligned implicitly or aggregated as indepen-
dent conditioning cues rather than organized into a connected
decoding hierarchy. Consequently, it remains difficult to trace
how different levels of neural information contribute to the
reconstructed output. Third, fMRI reconstruction still faces
unverified neural grounding. A visually plausible output
may be dominated by generative priors rather than faithfully
reflecting stimulus-relevant brain activity, especially when
ground-truth stimuli are unavailable, as in imagined visual
decoding or communication for patients with severe motor
impairment [20].
To overcome these limitations, unlike previous methods
that treat fMRI-to-image and fMRI-to-video reconstruction as
separate tasks, we introduce a novel fMRI-to-visual-stream
reconstruction setting and develop a novel framework, NEU-
RONS++, to realize it. To bridge the separated reconstruc-
tion settings, we formulate images as single-frame visual
streams and videos as multi-frame visual streams under a
common neural-to-visual formulation, enabling multi-subject
image-fMRI pretraining to provide stable spatial-semantic
grounding and video-fMRI adaptation to extend the learned
representation to temporally evolving visual experience. To
build a connected representation hierarchy, we develop a
novel Serial Visual Decoupler that progressively transforms
neural activity into spatial object structure, object-level seman-
tics, scene-level language, and coarse temporal appearance,
forming a brain-inspired and traceable neural-to-cue pathway
rather than independent generation conditions. To verify neu-
ral grounding beyond visual fidelity, we conduct delta-fMRI
perturbation, ROI ablation, and ROI amplification experiments,
showing that the decoded content changes directionally under
graded neural perturbations, is sensitive to stimulus-relevant
cortical features, and can be modulated through region-specific
neural interventions.

2.
RELATED WORK
2.1.
fMRI-to-Image Reconstruction
Reconstructing visual stimuli from fMRI has been widely
studied for understanding neural visual representations and
developing brain-computer interface systems. Early studies
mainly focused on decoding visual categories or reconstruct-
ing simple visual patterns from brain activity [1]. With the
emergence of large-scale fMRI-image datasets, especially the
Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) [21], fMRI-to-image recon-
struction has rapidly advanced from low-level image recovery
to natural image generation. Recent diffusion-based methods
substantially improve reconstruction quality by aligning fMRI
representations with vision-language models and using gener-
ative priors for image synthesis [6]–[9], [11], [13], [22]–[27].
These methods typically map fMRI signals into CLIP [4],
Stable Diffusion [5], or related latent spaces. To preserve
low-level spatial details, several methods introduce additional
control signals such as blurry reconstructions, contours, or
masks [8], [9], [23], [25]. To improve high-level semantic
alignment, other methods leverage captions, category labels, or
text embeddings [7]–[9]. Cross-subject reconstruction methods
further attempt to reduce subject-specific data requirements
by aligning fMRI representations across individuals through
shared latent spaces or functional alignment [7], [10], [12],
[28]–[30]. Despite these advances, existing fMRI-to-image
methods are designed for static visual stimuli. They are usually
optimized for spatial fidelity, object identity, and image-level
semantics, but do not explicitly model temporal evolution,
motion, or scene transitions. As a result, they cannot directly
handle dynamic visual experiences such as videos.
2.2.
fMRI-to-Video Reconstruction
Compared with static image reconstruction, fMRI-to-video
reconstruction is more challenging because it requires recover-
ing object motion, temporal consistency, and scene transitions
from slow BOLD signals. Early studies explored dynamic
visual decoding with handcrafted features or low-resolution
reconstruction strategies [3], [14], [15]. Recent methods im-
prove video reconstruction by aligning fMRI representations
with vision-language embeddings and using generative video
priors [16], [17], [30]. These methods typically follow a two-
stage paradigm: mapping fMRI signals into CLIP or related
2


latent spaces, and then synthesizing videos with diffusion-
based generative models [5], [31], [32]. Recent state-of-the-
art methods further introduce more structured guidance for
video reconstruction. SemVideo [19] uses hierarchical se-
mantic guidance, including static anchors, motion narratives,
and holistic summaries, to improve object consistency and
temporal coherence. NEURONS [18] introduces cortex-inspired
explicit tasks to decompose fMRI-to-video reconstruction into
multiple visual cues. However, these methods remain video-
specific and do not connect static image reconstruction with
dynamic video reconstruction under a shared visual-stream
formulation. In addition, the explicit cues in NEURONS are
mainly aggregated in parallel, which limits their ability to form
a progressive neural-to-visual pathway.
2.3.
Neural-to-Visual Interpretability
Most generative brain decoding methods are primarily eval-
uated by reconstruction quality, including pixel-level similar-
ity, semantic retrieval, feature similarity, or temporal consis-
tency. These metrics are useful for measuring output fidelity,
but they do not directly verify whether the generated content
is faithfully grounded in brain activity. This issue becomes
more important as powerful diffusion priors can hallucinate
plausible visual details even when neural evidence is weak or
incomplete. Several existing methods improve controllability
or introduce intermediate visual cues, such as masks, captions,
concepts, or blurry reconstructions [8], [9], [18], [19], [23].
However, these cues are usually used as auxiliary conditions
for generation, rather than as objects of neural-to-visual verifi-
cation. In contrast, NEURONS++ formulates image and video
reconstruction as unified fMRI-to-visual-stream decoding, uses
serial cortex-inspired decoding to progressively recover spa-
tial, semantic, language-level, and temporal cues, and further
examines brain-grounding through delta-fMRI perturbation,
ROI ablation, and ROI amplification.

METHOD
3.1.
fMRI-to-Visual-Stream Problem Formulation
We formulate fMRI-to-image and fMRI-to-video recon-
struction as an fMRI-to-visual-stream decoding problem.
Given an fMRI response xc corresponding to a perceived
visual stimulus, the target visual stream is denoted as Yc =
{Ic,t}Tc
t=1, where Ic,t is the t-th visual frame and Tc is the
temporal length of the visual stream. When Tc = 1, the task
corresponds to static image reconstruction; when Tc > 1,
the task corresponds to video reconstruction. This formulation
unifies image and video decoding at the level of visual-stream
representation, without assuming that all image and video
datasets are jointly trained by a single decoder. Here, “uni-
fied” refers to a common visual-stream formulation, shared
architectural design, and consistent inference interface for both
image and video reconstruction. Because fMRI responses are
highly subject- and dataset-dependent, we perform subject-
specific adaptation for each target dataset while keeping the
same decoding formulation and module design.
For video datasets, each video is split into short clips accord-
ing to the temporal resolution of fMRI. In our implementation,
each clip contains F frames, i.e., Tc = F, and a single visual
stream is represented as Yc ∈RF ×C×H×W , where F = 6
unless otherwise specified. For image datasets, we set Tc = 1,
so the same formulation naturally reduces to single-frame
reconstruction. The goal of NEURONS++ is to learn fMRI-
derived representations that can be instantiated for both single-
frame image generation and multi-frame video generation.

3.2.
Overview of NEURONS++
Our conference model, NEURONS [18] (Fig. 3), introduced
a cortex-inspired fMRI-to-video reconstruction framework that
decomposes decoding into four explicit sub-tasks: key ob-
ject segmentation, concept recognition, scene description, and
blurry video reconstruction. Building on the visual-stream for-
mulation introduced in §3.1, NEURONS++ extends NEURONS
from video-only reconstruction to both single-frame image
reconstruction and multi-frame video reconstruction under a
shared architectural template. Image and video datasets are
adapted separately at the subject level, while multi-subject
image-fMRI pretraining provides a shared spatial-semantic ini-
tialization for both settings. As shown in Fig. 2, NEURONS++
consists of three components. First, a brain encoding module
maps voxel responses into visual and textual embeddings
aligned with vision-language foundation models (§3.3). Sec-
ond, a Serial Visual Decoupler converts the inherited tasks into
a progressive decoding process, producing spatial, semantic,
language-level, and temporal cues (§3.4). Third, a unified
visual-stream inference pipeline uses these cues for either
single-frame image reconstruction or multi-frame video re-
construction (§3.5). The construction of decoupled task labels
follows NEURONS and is described in Supplementary §A.

3.5.
Unified Visual-Stream Inference
During inference, NEURONS++ reconstructs visual streams
through the serial decoding process. Given a test fMRI re-
sponse xc, the brain encoding module first produces visual
and textual embeddings, which are then passed to the Serial
Visual Decoupler. The decoupler progressively predicts four
explicit cues: a key object mask, a concept set, a scene
description, and a blurry visual-stream reconstruction. These
cues provide spatial, semantic, language-level, and temporal
guidance for final generative reconstruction. We first recon-
struct a keyframe using an unCLIP-based image generation
pipeline [33]. The keyframe is conditioned on the fMRI-
derived visual embedding, the decoded scene description, and
the predicted key object mask. The binary key object mask
is rescaled to [0.5, 1] and used as a soft spatial condition,
emphasizing the salient object while preserving background
context. For image reconstruction, the keyframe is further
refined by a pretrained SDXL-based image-to-image diffu-
sion model, producing the final single-frame visual-stream
reconstruction; the specific checkpoint and img2img settings

image and fMRI-to-video reconstruction studies. For videos,

6.
BEYOND RECONSTRUCTION: NEURAL-TO-VISUAL
INTERPRETABILITY
Beyond reconstruction fidelity, we conduct three analyses to
examine whether NEURONS++ learns brain-grounded neural-
to-visual representations. First, delta-fMRI perturbation tests
whether graded changes along a neural direction produce
direction-consistent visual changes. Second, ROI ablation tests
whether the encoder read-out depends on the cortical regions
actually engaged by each stimulus. Third, ROI amplification
tests whether selectively enhancing functional ROIs can steer
the decoded semantics. The delta-fMRI analysis is conducted
on the CC2017 video-fMRI dataset, whereas ROI ablation
and ROI amplification are conducted on CC2017 subject 1.
Additional mathematical details, implementation notes, and
cross-subject replications are provided in Supplementary §I.
6.1.
NEURONS++ Exhibits Directional Controllability under
Delta-fMRI Perturbation
Reconstruction metrics evaluate whether the model recovers
a fixed ground-truth stimulus. A complementary property is
controllability: when the input fMRI response is moved along
a meaningful neural direction, the decoded visual content
should change coherently along the same direction.
Let xi and xj denote the trial-averaged fMRI responses to
two CC2017 test clips. We define a directional delta-fMRI
perturbation around xj as
˜xj(α) = xj + α(xj −xi),
α ∈{−0.5, −0.3, 0.3, 0.5}. (8)
Negative α moves xj toward xi, whereas positive α ex-
trapolates beyond xj along the same neural direction. Each
synthetic response is z-score normalized before being passed
to the encoder. Because the normalization is performed per re-
sponse, this experiment is interpreted as a qualitative direction-
consistency probe rather than a metrically calibrated pro-
portionality test; the normalization analysis is provided in
Supplementary §I.1.
As shown in Fig. 7, the reconstructed keyframes change
smoothly along the perturbation direction. Negative α values
produce reconstructions closer to clip i, while positive α values
move beyond clip j along the same direction. This suggests
that the encoder propagates graded fMRI perturbations into
coherent perceptual changes rather than unstructured visual
variation.

CONCLUSION
In this study, we present NEURONS++, a unified fMRI-to-
visual-stream reconstruction framework for both static image
and dynamic video reconstruction. By treating images as
single-frame visual streams and videos as multi-frame visual
streams, NEURONS++ connects two previously separated de-
coding settings under a common formulation and architectural
template. Compared with our conference version NEURONS,
NEURONS++ extends the task paradigm from video-only
reconstruction to visual-stream reconstruction for both images
and videos, and replaces parallel task aggregation with serial
cortex-inspired visual decoupling. The proposed framework
combines multi-subject image-fMRI model pretraining with
subject-specific adaptation, allowing the spatial-semantic rep-
resentations learned from image-fMRI data to benefit both
static and dynamic visual-stream reconstruction. Through se-
rial decoding, NEURONS++ progressively organizes fMRI-
derived representations into spatial, semantic, language-level,

reconstruction. Extensive experiments on image and video

interpretability of NEURONS++ through delta-fMRI pertur-
bation, ROI ablation, and ROI amplification analyses. These
analyses suggest that NEURONS++ exhibits qualitative direc-
tional controllability under graded neural perturbation, that
its encoder read-out is sensitive to stimulus-relevant cortical
regions, and that it supports ROI-guided semantic control. To-
gether, these results show that NEURONS++ advances fMRI-
based visual reconstruction toward a unified and interpretable
visual-stream decoding framework.

A.
DECOUPLED TASK CONSTRUCTION
We construct four types of task labels for the Serial Visual
Decoupler: scene descriptions, concept names, concept seg-
mentation masks, and rule-based key objects, as illustrated in
Fig. b.
Scene description generation. To construct labels for the
scene description task, we generate a descriptive caption for
each frame using Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct [40]. Each frame
is provided to Qwen with an instruction to produce a detailed
one-phrase caption, such as “a woman sitting on a
bed looking out a window at palm trees”.
The instruction prompts are shown in Fig. a.
Concept name generation. To construct labels for concept
recognition, we generate concept names for the primary ob-
jects in each frame. We first input each frame into Qwen and
ask it to recognize the primary objects, such as ['woman',
'bed', 'tree']. The detected primary objects are then mapped
into 51 concept categories summarized from WordNet [41],
such as ['animal', 'human', 'plant', 'furniture']. The
output is a concept name list that covers the primary objects
in the frame. The instruction prompts and the 51-concept list
are shown in Fig. a.
Concept segmentation mask generation. We use the object
list of each frame to generate segmentation masks for the
primary objects. Each frame and its object list are provided to
Grounded-SAM [42], which generates binary masks using the
object names as textual prompts.
Rule-based key object discovery. We identify key objects
in video sequences by integrating motion dynamics, object
size, and semantic importance. Background categories such
as sky and ocean are excluded using predefined labels. For
the remaining objects, inter-frame displacement is calculated,
with higher weights assigned to priority categories such as
humans and animals to reflect semantic importance. Objects
are ranked by weighted displacement, and excessively large
objects occupying more than 50% of the image area are filtered
out. Priority categories are selected first; otherwise, top-ranked
objects by motion dynamics are chosen. If all objects are
background, the largest object is selected as a fallback to
ensure that at least one key object is identified.

C.
TRAINING AND INFERENCE CONFIGURATION
For the CC2017 dataset, videos are downsampled from 30
FPS to 3 FPS to ensure fair comparison with prior methods.
The blurry visual-stream prediction is interpolated to 8 FPS
during inference to generate the final reconstructed video. The
brain encoding module and Serial Visual Decoupler are trained
for 30 and 50 epochs, respectively. The batch size is set to 120
for the brain encoding module and 10 for the Serial Visual
Decoupler.
We use AdamW [44] for optimization with a learning rate
of 5 × 10−5 and a OneCycle learning-rate schedule [45].
The loss-balancing weights in Eqs. (3) and (7) of the main
paper are set to λt = 0.5, λp = 1.0, and λdec = 1.0 in all
experiments. For video reconstruction, the temporal expansion
module can in principle be instantiated with different text-to-
video diffusion models. In our implementation, we use the
open-source AnimateDiff model [31], following prior fMRI-
to-video reconstruction work [17]. Inference is performed with
25 DDIM steps [46].
For image reconstruction, the keyframe is first generated
by an SDXL unCLIP pipeline and then refined by an SDXL-
based image-to-image diffusion model. The refinement uses
the publicly available ZavyChromaXL v3.0 SDXL checkpoint,
conditioned on the fMRI-derived CLIP visual embedding and
the decoded scene description. The img2img process runs for
38 sampler steps and starts from a partially noised latent of
the initial keyframe (i.e., the diffusion trajectory is entered
near the high-noise end rather than from pure noise), so that
the refined image preserves the layout of the keyframe while
sharpening object-level detail. All experiments are conducted
on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.
D.
DATASET DETAILS AND PREPROCESSING
D.1.
CC2017
We use the public CC2017 video-fMRI dataset [3] for
fMRI-to-video reconstruction. The dataset contains fMRI re-
sponses from three subjects viewing natural movie clips sub-
tending approximately 23◦×23◦of visual angle, with a central
fixation cross of 0.8◦× 0.8◦. The stimuli are divided into 18
training movies and 5 testing movies, each 8 minutes long. The
training and testing movies were presented 2 and 10 times to
15


each subject, respectively. Anatomical MRI, including T1- and
T2-weighted scans, and fMRI data were acquired using a 3T
MRI system. The fMRI signals have a temporal resolution of
2 seconds.
We follow the preprocessing protocol used in prior fMRI-
to-video reconstruction studies [3], [17], based on the minimal
preprocessing pipeline [47]. The fMRI volumes are corrected
for artifacts and head motion, registered to the MNI space,
and projected onto cortical surfaces co-registered to a tem-
plate [39]. To identify stimulus-activated voxels, we compute
the voxel-wise correlation between the fMRI signals of the
repeated presentations of each training movie. The correlation
coefficients are Fisher z-transformed, and the averaged z-
scores across the 18 training movie segments are evaluated
with a one-sample t-test. Voxels with significant activation
(Bonferroni-corrected, P
< 0.05) are selected to form a
subject-specific visual mask, yielding 13,447, 14,828, and
9,114 activated voxels in the visual cortex for the three
subjects, respectively. This mask is computed only from
the training set and then applied to both training and test
responses. Following prior work [2], [48], we use BOLD
signals with a 4-second hemodynamic lag to represent the
corresponding movie stimulus responses. After preprocessing,
the dataset contains 8,640 training fMRI-video pairs and
1,200 testing pairs for each subject. The testing responses are
averaged across repeated presentations, following the standard
evaluation protocol.
D.2.
HCP
We further evaluate fMRI-to-video reconstruction on the
movie-watching data from the Human Connectome Project
(HCP) [37]. The full dataset contains BOLD responses from
158 subjects. In our experiments, we use three subjects,
100610, 102816, and 104416, following the setting of prior
video reconstruction work. The data were acquired using a
7T MRI scanner with a spatial resolution of 1.6 mm and a
repetition time of 1 second.
We use the standard HCP preprocessing pipeline, includ-
ing head-motion correction, distortion correction, high-pass
filtering, and removal of temporal artifacts using independent
component analysis (ICA). The preprocessed BOLD responses
are registered to the MNI standard space. Since the HCP
movie-watching data do not provide repeated presentations
in the same way as CC2017, we identify visually responsive
voxels using a split-half reliability test on the cortical surface
(59,412 fsLR-32k vertices). For each movie, we divide its clips
into even- and odd-indexed halves and compute the voxel-wise
Pearson correlation between the two halves within consecutive
segments; the correlations are Fisher z-transformed and tested
against zero with a per-voxel one-sample t-test, and voxels
surviving Bonferroni correction (α = 0.01) are retained. If
fewer voxels survive than the target budget, we fall back to
the top 12,000 voxels ranked by adjusted p-value. This yields
12,000 voxels for subjects 100610 and 102816, and 13,292
for subject 104416.
Following prior fMRI video decoding protocols [2], [14],
[48], we use a 4-second hemodynamic lag to align BOLD
responses with movie stimuli. Since there is no widely estab-
lished train/test split for video reconstruction on these three
HCP subjects, the split must be constructed by us. The HCP
movie-watching stimuli are continuous films, and the clips are
1-second segments cut sequentially from four movies (728,
792, 745, and 775 clips, respectively; 3,040 in total). A naive
random shuffle of all clips into a 90%/10% split (as used
in the conference version) causes severe temporal leakage:
because temporally adjacent clips are highly correlated and
the 4-second hemodynamic lag spreads each clip’s BOLD
response onto its neighbors, a randomly held-out test clip
is almost always flanked by training clips that carry the
same hemodynamic signal. Concretely, under the random split,
100% of test clips have a training clip within the 4-second
hemodynamic window and 98% have an immediate (±1 s)
training neighbor (median nearest-training gap of 1 s), and the
voxel-wise fMRI response of a test clip correlates with its
adjacent training clip at r=0.43 on average, which inflates
test-time scores.
To eliminate this leakage, we adopt a group-aware, embar-
goed split. Within each movie, we partition the timeline into
contiguous 30-second blocks and assign whole blocks to the
training or test set (so all four movies are represented in both
sets), and at every train/test boundary we discard an embargo
of 4 seconds (the hemodynamic lag) of training clips adjacent
to any test clip. This yields 2,597 training and 355 test clips
(88 boundary clips discarded by the embargo) and reduces
the fraction of test clips with a training neighbor within the
hemodynamic window from 100% to 0% (minimum nearest-
training gap of 5 s, median 13 s), removing the temporal
leakage. All HCP results in this paper, including those in
Table I, are reported under this leakage-free split, and the split
is fixed across methods to ensure fair comparison.

G.
EVALUATION METRICS
G.1.
Video Reconstruction Metrics
For fMRI-to-video reconstruction, we evaluate the generated
videos at both the semantic level and the spatiotemporal level.
Semantic-level evaluation. Following prior fMRI-to-video
reconstruction studies, we use an N-way classification-based
evaluation protocol to measure whether the reconstructed
video preserves the semantic category of the ground-truth
stimulus. Here N denotes the number of candidate classes
used in the metric and is unrelated to the CLIP visual-token
count Ntok in the main paper. Specifically, we use a video
classifier based on VideoMAE [49], trained on the Kinetics-
400 dataset [50]. For each test sample, the ground-truth video
and reconstructed video are classified, and the trial is consid-
ered successful if the ground-truth class is correctly identified
among N randomly sampled candidate classes, including the
true class. We report 2-way and 50-way accuracy following
prior work.
Spatiotemporal consistency. To evaluate temporal smooth-
ness and consistency, we compute CLIP image embeddings
for each frame of the reconstructed video and report the av-
erage cosine similarity between adjacent frames. This metric,
denoted as CLIP-pcc, is commonly used in video editing and
video reconstruction tasks [51]. A higher CLIP-pcc indicates
stronger temporal consistency between neighboring frames.
G.2.
Frame-Level and Image Reconstruction Metrics
In addition to video-level metrics, we also evaluate recon-
structed frames and static image reconstructions using both
low-level and high-level metrics.
Low-level metrics. We use SSIM and PSNR to evaluate pixel-
level similarity between reconstructed frames/images and the
corresponding ground-truth visual stimuli. These metrics mea-
sure low-level spatial fidelity, including structure, luminance,
and pixel-wise reconstruction quality.
High-level semantic metrics. For semantic-level image eval-
uation, we use an N-way top-K classification test based on
an ImageNet classifier with 1,000 classes. For each trial, the
classifier predicts category probabilities for the reconstructed
image. The trial is considered successful if the ground-truth
class is included in the top-K predicted probabilities among
N randomly sampled candidate classes, including the true
class. We use top-1 prediction and repeat the random sampling
procedure 100 times to obtain a stable success rate.
For NSD-based fMRI-to-image reconstruction, we addition-
ally follow the standard evaluation protocol used in prior
image reconstruction studies. The metrics include low-level
similarity measures such as pixel correlation and SSIM, as well
as high-level feature-based metrics computed with pretrained
visual recognition networks, including AlexNet, Inception,
CLIP, EfficientNet, and SwAV. These metrics jointly assess
whether the reconstructed image preserves both low-level
visual structure and high-level semantic content.

I.3.
Details and Limitations of ROI Amplification
The ROI amplification analysis in Sec. 6.3 tests whether
selectively enhancing a functional ROI can steer the decoded
visual semantics toward the category associated with that ROI.
Given a target ROI R and an original trial-averaged fMRI
response x ∈RV , we construct a manipulated response by
setting all voxels in R to a fixed high activation value v = 3.0
and suppressing non-target voxels by a factor of 0.1:

The manipulated response is then z-score normalized and

the original response.
This intervention is intentionally strong. It bundles three
operations: target-ROI saturation, non-target suppression, and
global z-score renormalization. Therefore, the result should be
interpreted as a region-selective, directionally specific seman-
tic modulation test, not as a calibrated estimate of the causal
gain of the target ROI alone. In other words, the experiment
asks whether pushing the neural input into a face-dominant or
place-dominant direction changes the decoded content in the
expected semantic direction; it does not estimate how much
natural variation in a single ROI would change perception or
reconstruction.
This design also makes the intervention partially out-of-
distribution relative to the natural fMRI responses used for
training. The instability observed for one ROI direction in each
of the two additional CC2017 subjects (Table f) should there-
fore be interpreted cautiously. The same subjects still show
strong ROI-ablation stimulus specificity (Table e), indicating
that the encoder reads stimulus-relevant ROI information. The
sign instability is specific to the strong boost intervention and
its propagation through the downstream decoder. A milder in-
distribution intervention, such as scaling only the target ROI
by a modest factor without suppressing the remaining voxels,
would provide a cleaner test of graded ROI gain and is left to
future work.

L.1.
ROI Ablation Generalizes Across Subjects and Datasets
For each subject we repeat the per-sample stimulus-
specificity test of Section 6.2: for every stimulus we cor-
relate the encoder’s eight-ROI ablation profile ∆cos·(xi)
with the corresponding fMRI activation-energy profile E·(xi)
(matched Spearman ρm), and assess significance with the
same 1,000-permutation, bijective paired-permutation null. A
positive matched-minus-null gap ∆ρ indicates that the encoder
relies most on the ROIs that the stimulus most strongly drives.
As summarized in Table e, the effect is large, positive,
and highly significant for every subject in both datasets: the
matched-minus-null gap ranges from ∆ρ = 0.33 to 0.46 with
a pooled-SD Cohen’s d (matched vs. mismatched distribution)
between 0.86 and 1.27, and the empirical p-value reaches
the 1/1001 permutation floor (p < 10−3) in all cases. The
consistency of this result across two datasets with different
acquisition protocols, voxel counts, and stimulus distributions
indicates that stimulus-specific ROI dependence is a robust
and reproducible property of the learned encoder rather than
an artifact of any single subject.
We note that the population-level ROI ranking (the single
Spearman correlation between the across-sample mean abla-
tion profile and mean energy profile, last column of Table e)
is more variable and is in some HCP subjects negative. This
population statistic is dominated by a few high-energy early-
visual ROIs and aggregates away the per-stimulus structure;
it is therefore a coarse summary and is not the quantity
our specificity test targets. The per-sample matched-versus-
null gap, which preserves the stimulus-by-stimulus correspon-
dence, is the appropriate measure of stimulus specificity and
is uniformly strong.
L.2.
ROI Amplification Across Subjects and Datasets
We next replicate the ROI-boost double-dissociation ex-
periment of Section 6.3. For each subject we boost the
FACE ROI and, separately, the PLACE ROI, and score every
reconstructed keyframe with the same zero-shot CLIP classi-
fier (Appendix J). For a clean double dissociation, boosting
a region should raise its matched category score (the on-
diagonal entries of the 2 × 2 score matrix) while leaving or
suppressing the competing category (the off-diagonal entries).
All comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test against the
unmanipulated reconstruction.
L.2.0a.
HCP subjects show a clean, reproducible double
dissociation.: On all three HCP subjects the same asymmet-
ric double dissociation reported in the main text reproduces
cleanly (top block of Table f): boosting the face ROI raises
CLIP-face (on-diagonal d = +0.88 to +1.07) while lowering
CLIP-place, and boosting the place ROI raises CLIP-place
(on-diagonal d = +1.03 to +1.35) while lowering CLIP-face.
Both on-diagonal effects are positive and highly significant for
every HCP subject, and the off-diagonal entries are uniformly
suppressive, exactly matching the competitive, region-selective
pattern described in the main text. Because the HCP data
were acquired and preprocessed independently of CC2017, this
constitutes a strong cross-dataset confirmation that targeted
ROI amplification steers the reconstruction toward the matched
semantic category.
L.2.0b.
Additional CC2017 subjects.: We also apply the
boost intervention to two further CC2017 subjects (bottom
block of Table f). In each case one of the two ROI directions
reproduces the intended on-diagonal lift with a large effect
(CC2017 S2 place: d = +1.57; CC2017 S3 face: d = +0.80),
confirming that the boost can drive the matched category.
The complementary direction in each subject is, however,
slightly imperfect: the on-diagonal effect is mildly negative
(CC2017 S2 face: d = −0.77; CC2017 S3 place: d = −0.94),
so boosting the region does not raise its own matched category
as cleanly. We note that these same two subjects show among
the strongest ablation specificity in the study (Table e), so the
encoder clearly reads the relevant ROI signal; the amplification
direction simply does not always propagate with a consistent
sign through the decoder. This is a minor instability that
affects only a single ROI direction per subject, and reducing
the boost magnitude does not remove it, so we report it
for completeness rather than treating it as a failure of the
underlying representation.
